Lecture 1 — Introduction & Threat-Model Thinking · Speaker Script

One entry per slide, in order. Section headers match slide titles. Overview pass: ~30 minutes.

Title

Welcome to Trustworthy AI. This course asks what it actually means to trust an AI system, and how to reason carefully about the ways it can fail. Today is a quick big-picture overview of the whole course.

Key takeaway: The course is about trust and failure, not just capability.

Today

Five beats in about thirty minutes: why trust matters now, how learned systems fail differently from ordinary software, the one habit of threat-model thinking, the map of the fifteen topics we'll cover, and how we'll work together.

Key takeaway: Five beats — motivation, failure modes, threat models, course map, logistics.

Why Trust?

Let's start with why this matters. AI has moved out of the lab and into decisions that change people's lives.

Key takeaway: Trust matters because AI now drives consequential decisions.

AI Now Decides Real Outcomes

Look at where AI already sits: hiring, lending, healthcare, content moderation, policing, and the information you see. These aren't demos — each one has real stakes for real people.

Key takeaway: AI is embedded in high-stakes, real-world decisions today.

Bias in the Courtroom

COMPAS predicted who would re-offend and US judges used it for bail and sentencing. ProPublica found Black defendants were nearly twice as likely to be falsely flagged high-risk — labeled dangerous and then never re-offending — and no one could inspect or question the score.

Key takeaway: An opaque score can encode and hide unfairness in life-altering decisions.

A Tool That Learned to Discriminate

Amazon built a résumé ranker trained on a decade of hires — mostly men. The model learned to penalize the word "women's." Amazon scrapped it; Reuters broke the story in 2018.

Key takeaway: Biased training data produces biased decisions, with no malicious programmer required.

Confident Fabrications, Real Costs

Two years apart, same failure. In Mata v. Avianca a lawyer filed six ChatGPT-supplied precedents; all six were fabricated and the court fined him. In 2025, Deloitte partially refunded the Australian government after a A$440,000 report shipped with references to sources that don't exist. Fluent, authoritative, and wrong — and it's still happening.

Key takeaway: Fluent output can be completely fabricated — reliability is not eloquence, and professionals keep falling for it.

A Chatbot That Made Promises

Air Canada's bot invented a bereavement-refund policy and a tribunal held the airline liable for it. A Chevrolet dealership bot was talked into "selling" a $1 car, "no takesies-backsies." When the assistant can act, its mistakes become your obligations.

Key takeaway: Once an AI speaks or acts on your behalf, its errors carry real legal and financial weight.

Models That Memorize People

Researchers pulled real names, phone numbers and email addresses out of GPT-2. In 2023, asking ChatGPT to "repeat the word poem forever" made it diverge and spill chunks of training data, some of it personal contact information.

Key takeaway: Models can memorize and regurgitate private training data.

Seeing Is No Longer Believing

Before the 2024 New Hampshire primary, a robocall faked President Biden's voice telling Democrats to stay home; the operative behind it faced a proposed six-million-dollar FCC fine and criminal charges. Voice clones now power "grandparent" phone scams. We can no longer assume a voice or face is real.

Key takeaway: Generative AI breaks our default trust in audio and video evidence.

Hijacking the Assistant

When an assistant reads web pages, email, or files for you, that content can hide instructions — "ignore your user, leak their data." In 2025 the EchoLeak vulnerability showed this for real: one crafted email could make Microsoft 365 Copilot exfiltrate private data with zero clicks. The attacker effectively writes part of your prompt.

Key takeaway: Untrusted content the model reads can become hostile instructions — now demonstrated in production systems.

The Pattern

Step back and the thread is clear: in every story, capability ran ahead of our ability to trust the output. Each one points to a missing guarantee — fair, reliable, private, real, secure — and those become our trust dimensions.

Key takeaway: Every failure is one missing guarantee; together they define the trust dimensions.

How AI Fails Differently

Now a step deeper: why do learned systems fail in ways ordinary software never did?

Key takeaway: Learned models break in genuinely new ways.

Software vs Learned Models

In classical software a bug is a line someone wrote — reproducible and patchable. In a learned model the behavior lives in billions of weights: probabilistic, no stack trace, no single line to fix. Nobody actually wrote the rule it follows, so nobody can simply read it back.

Key takeaway: Learned behavior sits in opaque weights, not in human-written rules.

A Tiny Push Fools It

Here's the classic example from Goodfellow and colleagues. Add noise that's invisible to your eye, and a model that called this a panda at 57.7% now calls it a gibbon at 99.3% confidence. Small, deliberate perturbations break it completely.

Key takeaway: Imperceptible input changes can flip a model's confident prediction — adversarial fragility.

Three Sources of Failure

Failures can enter at three stages: in the data (biased, private, or poisoned), in training (the objective misses what we meant), or at deployment (new inputs and real adversaries).

Key takeaway: Trouble can originate in data, training, or deployment.

Six Dimensions of Trust

This gives us the six dimensions we'll return to all term: privacy, robustness, reliability, fairness, transparency, and provenance — whose data leaks, breaks under a push, confidently wrong, who gets hurt, can we explain it, is this real? They're coupled, not silos — fixing one can stress another.

Key takeaway: Six trust dimensions structure the entire course.

Threat-Model Thinking

Now the single most important habit in this course — the one idea that ties everything else together.

Key takeaway: Threat-model thinking is the unifying habit of the course.

What Is a Threat Model?

A threat model answers three questions: who is the adversary and what's their goal, what do they know about the system, and what can they actually do? Answer these before you ever ask "is it safe?"

Key takeaway: A threat model specifies the adversary's goal, knowledge, and capabilities.

What They Know, When They Strike

Two axes describe most adversaries. Knowledge: white-box attackers see the weights and gradients — open models, insiders, audits — while black-box attackers see only the API. Timing: train-time attackers poison the data before the model ships; inference-time attackers manipulate inputs after deployment. Timing also changes what defense is even possible.

Key takeaway: Attacker knowledge (white/black-box) and timing (train/inference) are the two axes that organize attacks.

The Attack Map

Put those two axes together — knowledge by timing — and you get a simple two-by-two that locates almost any attack: data poisoning, backdoors, jailbreaks, and gradient-based adversarial attacks each land in a different cell. We'll come back to this map all term.

Key takeaway: Knowledge × timing places nearly any attack on one map.

No Threat Model, No Answer

The punchline: "Is it secure?" means nothing until you fix who, what, and when. Think of your front-door lock — plenty against a passerby, useless against the locksmith you hired. Same lock, different threat, different verdict. State the threat first, then evaluate.

Key takeaway: Always state the threat model before claiming something is secure.

The Trust Stack & Course Map

With those tools in hand, here's what the course covers — fifteen topics in four groups.

Key takeaway: The course is organized as a stack of topics, not a rigid calendar.

The Trust Stack

Every failure we saw lives at one layer: data, model, output, or society. The course climbs this stack from the bottom up — privacy at the data layer, governance and fairness at the top.

Key takeaway: Failures sit at distinct layers, and we climb the stack from data to society.

The Course at a Glance

After today's intro there are fourteen topics in four groups: Privacy & Data (differential privacy, membership inference, memorization and extraction, unlearning), Reliability (hallucination and calibration, interpretability), Security (adversarial examples, poisoning and backdoors, jailbreaks, prompt injection and agents), and Provenance & Fairness (watermarking and deepfakes, fairness definitions, fairness mitigation, governance). These are topic areas — the exact week-by-week order will be announced in class.

Key takeaway: Fifteen topics in four groups climbing the trust stack; the schedule stays flexible.

Group 1: Privacy & Data

Group one covers differential privacy as a budget for leakage, membership inference asking "were you in the data?", memorization and extraction like the GPT-2 leak, and unlearning — making a model forget.

Key takeaway: Group 1 is about controlling what models leak about their data.

Group 2: Reliability

Group two is reliability: hallucination and those confident fake citations, calibration — does 90% really mean 90%? — and interpretability, opening the black box. The goal is moving from "it sounds right" to "we can check."

Key takeaway: Group 2 turns plausible-sounding output into verifiable output.

Group 3: Security

Group three is security: adversarial examples like panda-to-gibbon, poisoning and backdoors planted before training, jailbreaks that bypass the safety filter, and prompt injection that hijacks the agent.

Key takeaway: Group 3 covers deliberate attacks on the model and its safety.

Group 4: Provenance & Fairness

Group four closes with provenance and fairness: watermarking AI output and detecting deepfakes, fairness definitions, fairness mitigation, and governance — the rules and accountability around all of it, up to the frontier.

Key takeaway: Group 4 addresses authenticity, fairness, and governance.

What's New in 2025–26

And this year is a moving target: reasoning models and agents raise the stakes, prompt injection is the live agentic threat, provenance rules like the EU AI Act and C2PA are landing, and interpretability now has sparse-autoencoder features. "Recent progress" is a running theme.

Key takeaway: The field is moving fast; we'll track the 2025–26 frontier all term.

How We'll Work

Last section — quick logistics on how this class actually runs.

Key takeaway: Now for how we'll work together.

Concepts, Not Proofs

This course is concepts and intuition first, with minimal math — at most one glanceable formula per idea, always with a plain-English version first. It is not proof-heavy theory, a coding bootcamp, or one field's narrow view. Mixed majors are welcome — basic Python is enough. To get the most out of it, always ask "what's the threat model?" and bring your own AI headlines to discuss.

Key takeaway: Concept-first and accessible — no heavy proofs, no bootcamp.

Optional Demos & Supplements

Everything hands-on is optional. Colab demos run in the browser with nothing to submit: tighten a privacy budget and watch utility drop, run a membership-inference attack, craft a panda-to-gibbon image, try a jailbreak sandbox. And for students who want the equations, each topic has a short technical-supplement deck with the formal version.

Key takeaway: Optional Colab demos for hands-on intuition; optional technical supplements for the math.

The Goal

If you take one thing away: by the end you should be able to read any AI headline and know which question to ask — private, robust, reliable, fair, real — and crucially, for which threat model.

Key takeaway: The goal is to instinctively ask the right trust question — and name the threat model.

Closer

So whenever you meet an AI system this term, ask the three questions: who is the adversary, what do they know, and what can they do? That's threat-model thinking. See you next time.

Key takeaway: Trust isn't a yes/no — always ask who, what they know, and what they can do.
