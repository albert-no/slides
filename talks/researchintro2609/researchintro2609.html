The Analogy

A project is a new artwork

The Problem Nobody Hands You

The first box is the hard one.

Two Kinds of Question

Project as Artwork

An artist facing a blank canvas has your problem: no assignment, and a judging audience.

The artist asks

  • Has this been made before?
  • Is it more than decoration?
  • Can I say what it does?

You ask

  • Has this been published before?
  • Is it more than a script?
  • Can I say why it matters?

Same three questions, different studio.

What an Artwork Owes

Novel

Something here did not exist.

Nontrivial

Skill, thought, or risk was spent.

Not a copy

Not a reproduction of a known piece.

Fail any one and the gallery declines it.

What a Project Owes

ArtworkProject
NovelSome claim is new
NontrivialNot one script, not one prompt
Not a copyNot a rerun of a known result

The bar is not "a paper". The bar is all three.

Not Artwork

Each is fine to make. None gets a wall.

Not a Project

Artwork failureProject failure
Paint by numbersFollow a tutorial repository
ForgeryReimplement a published method
Poster reprintSame method, swapped dataset
DoodleOne prompt to Claude or GPT

Every one of these is a good exercise. None is a project.

The Single-Prompt Test

If one prompt finishes it, the machine already knew the answer.

The Existing-Repo Test

Before falling in love with an idea, search for it.

  1. GitHub search, top three results
  2. Google Scholar, sort by citations
  3. Hugging Face spaces and datasets
  4. Ask an LLM: "who already did this?"

Finding it done is good news: twenty minutes saved, one baseline found.

The Swapped-Dataset Test

Not a project

Known method, new dataset,
same conclusion.

"LoRA works on Korean legal text too."

A project

New domain breaks the method,
and you explain why.

"LoRA fails here: rank cannot hold citation formats."

A new domain earns its place only when it surprises you.

The Rerun Test

Replication is valuable, and it is not your novelty.

  1. Reproduce the number  →  engineering
  2. Reproduce, then break it  →  project
  3. Show the claim never held  →  paper

Start at step 1. Do not stop at step 1.

Survey

Knowing the history of the art

Artists Study Art History

Nobody invents a style by ignoring every style before it.

  • What was tried, and what failed
  • Which constraint each generation attacked
  • What today's rooms argue about

Without the history, "new to me" gets mistaken for new.

Movements Answer Constraints

Movement dates are conventional and approximate; the point is the pattern, not the chronology.

Every Movement Is a Reply

Read any movement as an answer to a blocking constraint.

  1. What did the previous generation take for granted?
  2. What new tool made breaking it possible?
  3. What became easy that had been impossible?

These three questions transfer to our field unchanged.

Mapping the Frontier

Example map for LLM agents. Draw yours before week three.

How to Survey

  1. One recent survey paper, read fully
  2. Its top-cited references, abstracts only
  3. Last two years of NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, ACL
  4. Three lab pages, and what they stopped doing
  5. Any workshop call: the open problems list

A workshop call for papers is a field's own list of what is unsolved.

Backwards, Then Forwards

A Survey Is a Map

A list

Twelve papers, one summary each.

Nothing follows from it.

A map

Two axes, papers placed, gaps visible.

The empty region is a candidate.

Deliverable: one page, one figure, six sentences.

03

Nontriviality

The question you never stop asking

The Artist's Running Question

Not asked once at the start. Asked at every session.

Why is this worth making?

A concept nobody has stated this way.

A side of people nobody has shown.

A technique nobody has tried.

If no answer comes for a week, the piece is in trouble.

Your Running Question

Is this nontrivial, and why is it interesting?

Nobody knows the answer, including me.

The obvious approach fails, for a reason I can name.

Either outcome tells us something.

Ask it weekly. The answer is allowed to change.

Two Ways to Earn a Place

New concept

The artwork says something unsaid.

Research twin: a phenomenon nobody had named or measured.

New technique

The artwork is made a way nobody made things.

Research twin: a method that does what prior methods could not.

One is enough. Neither is fatal.

The Two-Day Test

"No, because it takes 400 GPU-hours" fails the test too.

The Escalation Ladder

Escalating a Fine-Tune

  1. Task. Fine-tune Llama on hospital QA. One script, one afternoon.
  2. And then? It answers in the right tone. Are the facts new?
  3. Problem. Separate style transfer from knowledge gain.
  4. Question. Does fine-tuning add facts, or only reformat old ones?

Now it is hard: you need facts provably absent from the base model.

Escalating a RAG Bot

  1. Task. RAG over lecture PDFs. One prompt to an agent.
  2. And then? It answers confidently when retrieval missed.
  3. Problem. Predict retrieval failure before answering.
  4. Question. Can the model flag hops that retrieval never returned?

Deliverable: a coverage-versus-accuracy curve, not a demo.

Escalating an RL Agent

  1. Task. Train PPO on a standard environment. A tutorial.
  2. And then? High return, and the behaviour looks wrong.
  3. Problem. Detect reward hacking before deployment.
  4. Question. Does return collapse under held-out respecifications of the same intent?

Nontrivial because you must build the family of respecifications.

Find the Bottleneck

Shrink the Claim

Too big to defend

"We improve reasoning in LLMs."

No experiment can settle it.

Small and sharp

"Self-consistency stops helping past eight samples on multi-hop QA."

One plot decides it.

Narrow the claim until a single figure can kill it.

Five Questions Before Committing

  1. Who already tried this, and how far did they get?
  2. What is the obvious approach, and why does it fail?
  3. What would I see if the idea works?
  4. What would I see if it does not?
  5. Is the second outcome still worth reporting?

If question five is no, redesign before writing code.

Justification

Explaining why the work matters

Every Artwork Has a Label

The piece hangs beside a small card. Somebody wrote that card.

Artist, dates

Title, year

Oil on canvas

Painted outdoors in one sitting, when finish was still expected.
The loose surface was the argument.

The last line is the one that took work.

Your Project's Wall Label

Question. The one sentence you are answering.

Stakes. Who is blocked while it stays open.

Difficulty. Why the obvious approach fails.

Difference. What the closest prior work assumed.

Evidence. The result that would convince a skeptic.

Five lines. Write them before the first experiment.

The Positioning Sentence

The Skeptic in the Room

Four questions arrive at every talk. Prepare all four.

Answers you improvise sound like answers you never had.

"Isn't This Just X?"

  1. Name X out loud, generously
  2. State the assumption X needs
  3. Show one case where that assumption breaks
  4. Show what your method does in that case

"Yes, and here is where it stops working" beats any defensive answer.

The Smallest Convincing Experiment

Design the experiment that could change your mind fastest.

A Negative Result Counts

An honest "it does not work, and here is why" is a finding.

Counts

The method fails, and you isolated the cause.

Does not count

The method fails, and the code had a bug.

Well-posed means both outcomes teach something.

05

Working Habit

What to do over the next month

Read More Than Feels Reasonable

GPT as Accelerator, Not Author

Ask it to

  • Summarise 20 abstracts
  • Find where two papers disagree
  • Attack your idea as a reviewer

Do not ask it to

  • Give you a research topic
  • Judge whether the idea is new
  • Decide what is interesting

An idea handed over in one turn fails the single-prompt test.

A Sketchbook of Bad Ideas

Painters fill sketchbooks. Almost none of it is hung.

Write down every idea, including the embarrassing ones.

The Weekly Loop

Four Weeks to a Topic

Traps That Catch Everyone

  • Tool first. Picking a method, then hunting a use
  • Infinite survey. Reading instead of deciding
  • Demo drift. Building product, measuring nothing
  • Benchmark chasing. Small deltas, no explanation
  • Silent pivot. Changing the question, keeping the code

Four of the five are avoided by rewriting the label weekly.

One Page Before Any Code

  1. Question, one sentence
  2. Closest prior work, and its assumption
  3. Why the obvious approach fails
  4. The smallest experiment that decides it
  5. What you report if the answer is no

Bring this page to your advisor. Not a repository link.

Three Questions to Carry

Is it new?

Somebody would have to cite you.

Is it hard?

Two days gets a baseline, not an answer.

Can I say why?

Five lines, out loud, to a skeptic.

A project that survives all three is worth a semester.
