5. People Spot Generated Faces About as Well as a Coin Flip

The provenance problem · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈4 min

Nightingale and Farid asked people a simple question: is this face a real photo or generated? The generated faces came from StyleGAN2.

Untrained, 315 people averaged 48.2% correct, slightly below a coin flip. A new group of 219 got training and feedback after every answer, and reached only 59.0%.

A third group rated how trustworthy each face looked. The generated faces scored 7.7% higher than the real ones. Small, but statistically significant.

Key takeaway: People cannot reliably spot generated faces, even with training.

The figure

Bar chart redrawn from the paper's reported averages: 48.2% correct untrained, 59.0% after training, against a dashed coin-flip line at 50%.

Setup, model, date

Nightingale & Farid, PNAS 119(8), published online 14 February 2022. All three experiments drew faces from the same set of 800 real and StyleGAN2 faces; participants were Amazon Mechanical Turk Master Workers.

  • Experiment 1: 315 participants each classified 128 faces as real or synthesized. "The average accuracy is 48.2% (95% CI [47.1%, 49.2%])".
  • Experiment 2: 219 new participants, with training and trial-by-trial feedback: 59.0% (95% CI [57.7%, 60.4%]).
  • Experiment 3: 223 participants rated 128 faces from 1 (very untrustworthy) to 7 (very trustworthy). Real faces averaged 4.48, synthetic 4.82: "only 7.7% more trustworthy", but significant.

What this establishes

That for still faces from this generator, people did about as well as chance, and training helped only a little.

What this does not establish

Nothing about video, voice or other generators; and nothing about how well software detectors do.

Primary sources

  • Nightingale & Farid, "AI-synthesized faces are indistinguishable from real faces and more trustworthy", PNAS 119(8): e2120481119 (2022) — Experiments 1–3; open access, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0.

7. A Faked Video Call Moved US$25 Million

The provenance problem · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈7 min

In January 2024 an employee in Arup's Hong Kong office joined a video call with people who looked and sounded like the company's CFO and colleagues. Every other participant was fake. Following instructions on the call, the employee made 15 transfers to 5 accounts, about HKD 200 million, roughly USD 25 million.

Nothing about the content of the call had to be false in an obvious way. The failure was about origin: who was actually on the other end.

Keep this case in mind. A detector, a watermark or a signed record would each have helped in a different way, and each could also have failed.

Key takeaway: The question that mattered was origin, not content.

Setup, model, date

Arup, Hong Kong office, January 2024; reported by CNN on 16 May 2024, with the account coming from Hong Kong police. Press report; no technical analysis of how the fakes were produced is public.

What this establishes

That synthetic video and voice were convincing enough to drive a large financial loss in a real workplace.

What this does not establish

It does not tell us which generator was used, or whether any detector would have flagged the call.

Primary sources

  • CNN, "Arup revealed as victim of $25 million deepfake scam involving Hong Kong employee" (16 May 2024) — press report; the incident account comes from Hong Kong police.

9. An Invisible Watermark: Same Picture, Hidden Signal

The provenance problem · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈9 min

Now a mark you cannot see. Left: an Apollo 11 photo. Middle: the same photo with a secret, key-generated noise pattern added; each pixel moves by at most 3 out of 255. Right: the difference between the two, amplified 40 times. It is just fine random noise.

To the eye, left and middle are identical. But someone holding the key can test for the pattern. With the right key the detector's z-score is 79: strong evidence under this demo's approximate null model. With a wrong key it is 0.8, which is what unmarked noise looks like.

This is a toy we built for the lecture, not a production watermark. It shows the idea behind this keyed toy watermark: a keyed signal, too small to see, that a key holder can test for.

10. Three Ways to Ask "Where Did This Come From?"

The provenance problem · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈10.5 min

Three families of evidence. Embed: the generator hides a signal in its output when it creates it; that is a watermark. Guess: anyone inspects the content afterwards and estimates whether a machine made it; that is a detector. Sign: the creator or the creating tool attaches a cryptographically signed record; that is provenance.

They differ in who has to cooperate. A watermark needs the generator. A detector needs nobody, which is why it is attractive and why it is weak. Provenance needs the creating tool and every app along the sharing path to keep the record.

Key takeaway: Embed, guess, sign: three kinds of evidence with different cooperators.

11. Origin Is Not Truth

The provenance problem · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈12 min

Read the two boxes. Something found: a watermark means an AI tool likely made it; valid credentials tell you who signed and that the signature and covered data match. Neither says the content is true.

Nothing found is where people go wrong. No watermark does not mean human-made: the generator may not watermark, or the mark may have been removed. No credentials does not mean fake: most real photos carry none, and sharing apps often strip them. Absence proves nothing.

And AI-made content can carry perfectly honest credentials saying "made with model Y".

Key takeaway: Origin signals say who made something; absence of a signal proves little.

12. Three Questions for Every Method Today

The provenance problem · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈13 min

For every method we ask three questions. Who acts: the generator, anyone, or the creator? What must survive: edits, paraphrase, crops, re-encoding, copy-paste? Which error hurts: accusing a human, or missing AI content?

Study slides carry a strip with who acts, what was attacked, and the error rate used. Read it before reading the result.

Key takeaway: Who acts, what must survive, which error hurts.

13. Today: Marks on Outputs, Not on Models

The provenance problem · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈14 min

One boundary. Today's watermarks mark each generated output, so we can ask "did this model produce this text?" A different line of work marks the model weights, to ask "is this stolen copy my model?" That is out of scope today, as is copyright of training data and outputs.

Key takeaway: Output watermarks only; model-ownership marks and copyright are out of scope.

14. Section 02 — Text Watermarking

Text watermarking · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈14.5 min

We now build one text watermark from scratch, the one from Kirchenbauer and colleagues, usually called KGW. Everything later in the lecture is a variation or an attack on this idea.

Key takeaway: One mechanism, taught carefully.

15. A Model Writes One Token at a Time

Text watermarking · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈15.5 min

A language model does not write a sentence at once. Given the text so far, it gives odds for every possible next word, picks one at random by those odds, appends it, and repeats.

The numbers on the slide are illustrative. The point: there is randomness in every pick, and a watermark changes only how that pick is made.

Key takeaway: Generation is repeated random picks; a watermark changes the pick.

The figure

Illustrative bar chart of next-word probabilities after "The weather today is". The numbers are made up for teaching.

16. Step 1: A Secret Key Splits the Vocabulary

Text watermarking · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈17 min

At each position, the previous token and a secret key are hashed into a random seed. The seed splits the whole vocabulary into a green list, a fraction γ of tokens (here 25%, so 3 of the 12 shown), and a red list, the rest.

The split is determined by the context and the key: the same previous token and key always give the same split. Without the key, the split looks random; with it, anyone can recompute which tokens were green.

Tokens are words or word pieces; on the slides we say "words" for short.

Key takeaway: A keyed hash of the previous token picks the green list at each step.

The figure

Schematic of the KGW split: previous token "is" and secret key feed a hash, which seeds a green/red partition; 3 of 12 cells green (γ = 25%).

Setup, model, date

Kirchenbauer et al., University of Maryland; arXiv January 2023, ICML 2023. The rule shown is the "soft" watermark of §3.

Assumptions

The hash behaves like a random function; the key stays secret from the attacker.

Primary sources

17. Step 2: Give Green Words a Small Boost δ

Text watermarking · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈18.5 min

The generator adds a small constant δ to the scores of green words before turning scores into odds. Green words become more likely; red words stay possible.

When the model is very sure of one word, the boost barely changes the pick, so the mark is weak in low-entropy text such as code or memorised passages. That is why detection needs enough varied text.

Key takeaway: A soft boost tilts the odds toward green words without forbidding red ones.

The figure

Illustrative before/after bars: green words gain probability, red words lose some.

Primary sources

  • Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers & Goldstein, "A Watermark for Large Language Models" (ICML 2023) — green/red list rule (§3, Algorithm 2), z-test (§3), Fig. 1 example, Fig. 2 strength vs perplexity, Fig. 3a z vs length.

19. By Chance, About γ of Words Are Green

Text watermarking · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈21 min

If the text was written without the key, each word is green with probability about γ, like a biased coin. With γ = 25% we expect about 5 green words in 20. The watermarked line has 14.

The question becomes statistical: is the green count too high to be chance?

Key takeaway: Unmarked text should hit green at the chance rate γ.

20. The Threshold Sets the False-Alarm Rate

Text watermarking · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈23 min

z measures how many standard deviations the green count sits above chance. KGW flags text when z > 4.

If tokens acted like independent coin flips, the normal approximation says ordinary text crosses z = 4 with probability about 3×10⁻⁵, one in roughly 30,000. That is a normal-approximation tail, not a guarantee. At 20 tokens with γ = 0.25 the exact Binomial tail is about 1.8×10⁻⁴, six times larger. And the independence "if" matters; two slides on we see when it fails.

Moving the threshold trades false alarms for misses, as with any test.

Key takeaway: The threshold sets a nominal false-alarm rate, under an idealized model.

The figure

Sketch, not to scale: two bell curves of z, ordinary text centred near 0 and watermarked text far right, with the threshold at z = 4.

Setup, model, date

KGW §3: one-proportion z-test; threshold z = 4.

Assumptions

Idealized independent-Bernoulli model: each scored token is green independently with probability γ. Exact tail at T = 20: z > 4 needs ≥ 13 green tokens, P(Bin(20, 0.25) ≥ 13) ≈ 1.84×10⁻⁴. Details in lec11tech.html.

Primary sources

  • Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers & Goldstein, "A Watermark for Large Language Models" (ICML 2023) — green/red list rule (§3, Algorithm 2), z-test (§3), Fig. 1 example, Fig. 2 strength vs perplexity, Fig. 3a z vs length.

22. A Repeated Phrase Counts the Same Coin Flip Again

Text watermarking · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈26 min

The z-test assumes each word is a fresh coin flip. But a word's colour depends only on the previous word and the key. So a repeated phrase, like "of the United States", gets exactly the same colours every time it appears.

Repeat that phrase five times and you get 15 green words out of 20, far above the 25% chance rate. Nobody watermarked it; the same flips were simply counted again. That is how human text can cross the threshold.

The fix: score each (previous word, word) pair only once, and skip repeats.

Key takeaway: Repeats are not fresh coin flips; count each pair once.

The figure

Illustration, not data: the phrase "of the United States" coloured identically on its first and second occurrence; repeated five times, 15 of 20 tokens are green.

Setup

The original KGW paper (2023, §4) gives the example: "Assume a 2-gram, such as 'Barack Obama' happens to green-list 'Obama'. Repetitive usage of this 2-gram would result in a higher than expected number of green tokens". Its fix: "'Barack Obama' would be counted on its first occurrence, and then subsequently ignored when it appears again; it is counted as neither green nor red, and the token counter T is not incremented." Kirchenbauer et al. 2024 (App. A.3) measure the effect on human text; see the next slide.

Primary sources

  • Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers & Goldstein, "A Watermark for Large Language Models" (ICML 2023) — §4, "Evaluating Repetitive Text": repeated n-grams and the count-once fix.

25. Some Marks Leave the Output Distribution Unchanged

Text watermarking · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈30 min

KGW shifts the odds. Other designs do not. A distortion-free watermark (Kuditipudi and colleagues): with a fresh key, never reused, one reply has exactly the model's own odds, averaged over the key. It says nothing about repeated queries that reuse the key.

An undetectable watermark (Christ, Gunn and Zamir) is a different guarantee: without the key, efficient tests gain only a negligible edge, under cryptographic assumptions. That is not the same as identical distributions.

The next two slides show how each one works, at the level of the idea. The formal definitions are in the tech file.

Key takeaway: Exact odds for one reply and no practical keyless test are two different guarantees.

Setup, model, date

Kuditipudi et al., Stanford, TMLR (Definition 1). Christ, Gunn & Zamir, arXiv 2306.09194 (2023).

What this does not establish

Neither guarantee is about robustness to edits. Distortion-freeness is averaged over a fresh key; it does not cover key reuse across queries.

Primary sources

  • Kuditipudi, Thickstun, Hashimoto & Liang, "Robust Distortion-free Watermarks for Language Models" (TMLR) — distortion-free definition (Definition 1), EXP and EXP-edit watermarks, substitution robustness (Fig. 4, m = 35).
  • Christ, Gunn & Zamir, "Undetectable Watermarks for Language Models" (arXiv 2306.09194, 2023) — cryptographic definition of undetectability; venue not checked for this lecture.

26. Distortion-Free: A Secret List of Random Numbers Picks Each Word

Text watermarking · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈32 min

Kuditipudi's Figure 1 shows three parties. The provider holds a secret key and answers a prompt about World War 1. The user edits the reply. The detector, holding the same key, checks the edited text.

The key is a long secret list of random numbers. To generate word i, the model uses the i-th random numbers in the list, ξi, to make its pick. Averaged over the key, each pick has exactly the model's own odds: that is what distortion-free means.

To detect, line the text up against the list, allowing for inserted and deleted words. If the text fits this key better than it fits random keys, it is marked.

One practical detail: if every reply started at the top of the list, the same prompt would always get the same answer. So each reply starts at a random place in the list, and repeated prompts can give different answers; nothing guarantees they will.

Key takeaway: A secret random list drives each pick; detection asks whether the text fits that list.

The figure

Setup

  • Generate (Algorithm 1): "a generate method that deterministically maps a sequence ξ of random numbers encoded by a watermark key … to a sample from the language model". The EXP decoder is the "exponential minimum sampling" rule the paper attributes to Aaronson.
  • Detect (Algorithms 2–3): "a detect method that aligns a putative watermarked text with the watermark key sequence". The most robust version, EXP-edit, uses a soft edit (Levenshtein) distance, so inserted or deleted words do not break the alignment. The p-value comes from a permutation test: compare the alignment score against those of fresh random keys.
  • Random start: "if our watermark produced the same text every time for each prompt it would not be very useful". Each call uses a randomly chosen starting point in ξ.
  • Distortion-free holds "so long as the watermark key sequence is long enough that we do not reuse any part of it".

The numbers

Abstract: "reliably detect watermarked text (p ≤ 0.01) from 35 tokens even after corrupting between 40-50% of the tokens via random edits". For Alpaca-7B, around 25% of responses are detectable at p ≤ 0.01. Statistical power "improves exponentially with respect to the length of the putative watermarked text and diminishes only linearly with the length of the random number sequence".

What this does not establish

Protection against a determined rewriter: "a sufficiently motivated … user can still evade detection by simply rewriting the text from scratch".

27. Undetectable: Secret Pseudorandom Coins Replace the Model's Coins

Text watermarking · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈34 min

Christ, Gunn and Zamir take a cryptographer's approach. First, write every token as a string of bits, as in the row of boxes.

At the start, the model samples normally, with its own coin flips, until enough randomness has piled up. That opening stretch becomes a seed. From then on, each coin flip is replaced by a secret one: a keyed pseudorandom function, PRF, of the key, the seed and the position. The bit is 1 if that number is at most the model's probability of a 1. Without the key, no efficient observer can tell the secret coins from truly random ones, so the output is computationally indistinguishable from ordinary sampling.

The guarantee: without the key, no efficient test can tell this output from the normal model, even after many queries; any efficient adversary's advantage is negligible. This is a computational guarantee, not exact equality of probabilities. So no efficient test can detect a drop in quality.

With the key, the detector scores how well each bit agrees with the secret coins. Two limits: it needs enough randomness in the text, and paraphrasing can erase it.

Key takeaway: Secret pseudorandom coins make the mark invisible without the key, at a cost in robustness.

The figure

Illustration: a row of bits; the first stretch is sampled normally until there is enough randomness, and that prefix becomes the seed; later bits are set by "bit = 1 if PRF(key, seed, i) ≤ p(1)".

Setup

  • Undetectability (Definition 9): for all polynomial-time distinguishers, the gap in behaviour between the plain model and the watermarked model is negligible, where the distinguisher "is allowed to adaptively query both … with arbitrary prompts". Consequence: "the quality of outputs must be identical between Model and Wat_sk, as otherwise it would be possible to distinguish between them."
  • Construction (Algorithm 3): sample normally while adding up the empirical entropy, "the number of random bits that were required to draw x"; once it reaches the security parameter, fix the seed and set each later bit by the PRF. The PRF is built from one-way functions.
  • Detection (Algorithm 4): for unwatermarked text each per-bit score "is simply an exponential random variable with mean 1"; for watermarked text it is higher. "the detection algorithm does not need to know the distributions".
  • Theorem 1: "always undetectable and sound, and is also complete as long as there is enough empirical entropy in the model's response."

What this does not establish

Robustness. "it is inherent that low empirical entropy outputs are not watermarked in any undetectable watermarking scheme". Paraphrase or substitution attacks "might remove the watermark from our scheme", and an emoji attack "removes any watermark that relies on the detector seeing consecutive sequences of tokens, including ours" (§6.1). This is a theoretical construction; the paper reports no deployment.

Primary sources

  • Christ, Gunn & Zamir, "Undetectable Watermarks for Language Models" (arXiv 2306.09194v1, 25 May 2023) — Definition 9, Algorithms 3–4, Theorem 1, §6.1. Venue not confirmed from the saved v1 text.

33. TV Measures How Far Apart AI and Human Text Are

Robustness and production · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈41.5 min

Before the impossibility result, one idea: total variation, TV. Put some property of a text on the x-axis. Each source, human and AI, has a curve: how often it produces text with that property.

TV is one minus the overlap of the two curves. On the left the curves overlap a lot, TV about 0.3: many texts are equally plausible from either source, so they are hard to tell apart. On the right they barely overlap, TV about 0.97: easy to tell apart.

TV runs from 0, identical sources, to 1, sources that never produce the same text.

These curves are toy distributions over one text feature, there to show overlap. The theorem uses distributions over full texts, and overlap in one feature does not establish overlap of the full-text distributions.

Key takeaway: TV measures how distinguishable two sources are: 0 identical, 1 fully separate.

The figure

Illustration, not data: toy distributions over one text feature, two pairs of curves, one overlapping heavily (TV ≈ 0.3) and one barely overlapping (TV ≈ 0.97). The theorem's TV is over full-text distributions.

Setup

Sadasivan et al. use the TV between the AI-text distribution M and the human-text distribution H "over the set of all possible text sequences". In their proof it enters through one inequality: for any detector threshold, the gap between its true-positive and false-positive rates is at most TV(M, H). "TV = 1 − overlap" is the standard identity for total variation; the paper does not write a formula out.

Primary sources

  • Sadasivan, Kumar, Balasubramanian, Wang & Feizi, "Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?" (TMLR 2025) — total variation; Theorem 1 and App. C.1.

34. If AI Text Looks Human, Every Detector Is Near a Coin Flip

Robustness and production · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈43 min

Sadasivan and colleagues prove a bound: any detector's AUROC is at most ½ + TV − TV²/2. It always holds. The curve plots it.

Read the two ends. If AI text has been paraphrased to look human, TV is small and the bound is near 0.5: every detector is pushed toward a coin flip. If text is watermarked and we hold the key fixed, TV can be large and the bound is near 1, so it says little.

Neither end is automatic. If paraphrasing makes full-text TV small, the bound tightens. A fixed-key watermark can keep TV large. TV is a property of the two distributions, not of what an observer knows. The bound does not say how hard shrinking it is.

Key takeaway: Always true; forces near-chance only when TV is small.

The figure

Curve drawn from the formula AUROC ≤ ½ + TV − TV²/2, with two labelled regions: paraphrased to look human (bound near 0.5) and watermarked with the key fixed (bound near 1).

The numbers

Our arithmetic from the formula: TV = 0.2 caps AUROC at 0.68; TV = 0.5 caps it at 0.875. The paper makes the same point: a detector with AUROC above 0.9 needs a large TV, and a small TV keeps AUROC below 0.7.

Setup, model, date

Sadasivan et al., Theorem 1: "The area under the ROC of any detector D is bounded as AUROC(D) ≤ 1/2 + TV(M, H) − TV(M, H)²/2." On watermarks: "for a watermarked model, the above bound can be close to one as the TV between the watermarked distribution and human-generated distribution can be high". Corollary 1 applies the bound to paraphrased text.

Assumptions

Applies to any detector that sees one text sample. For a keyed detector, compare distributions with the key held fixed: the fixed-key watermarked distribution can be far from human text. Averaged over random keys, the watermarked distribution can be much closer to the model's own, so its TV from human text need not be large. The paper notes a counter-view (Chakraborty et al., 2023) that detection improves with several independent samples, and argues that sentences in a document are often correlated. Derivation in lec11tech.html.

Primary sources

  • Sadasivan, Kumar, Balasubramanian, Wang & Feizi, "Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?" (TMLR 2025) — recursive paraphrasing (Fig. 3a, OPT-13B, XSum), AUROC bound in terms of total variation (Theorem 1).

41. Tournament Sampling: Candidates Compete on the Key's Scores

Robustness and production · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈52 min

The sampling step is a knockout tournament. The paper's example context ends "tropical fruit is". Draw 8 candidate words from the model, with repeats: durian, mango, lychee, papaya and so on. Likely words appear more often.

The key gives each candidate a score, called g, for each round. Pair them up; the higher g wins, and a tie is broken at random. After three rounds one word is left: here, mango. That word is output.

The figure uses 3 rounds for readability; the paper's experiments use 30. Production settings are not documented in the paper.

Key takeaway: The key's scores pick a winner among the model's own candidates.

The figure

Dathathri et al. Fig. 2, tournament panel: 2m = 8 candidates drawn from the model, three rounds of pairwise matches on g-values, mango wins.

Setup

Figure settings: m = 3 rounds, two candidates per match, g-values drawn as Bernoulli(0.5), model probabilities 0.50 / 0.30 / 0.15 / 0.05 for mango / lychee / papaya / durian. Paper experiments: "m = 30 layers" (p. 820); the paper does not document production settings. Ties are broken at random.

Why the output keeps the model's odds

Because candidates come from the model and the g-values are random over the key, the paper shows that with two competitors per match a single token keeps the model's distribution. Its default additionally avoids reusing the same seed context, which it calls single-sequence non-distortionary. Details are in the paper.

51. "1% False Alarms" Is Not "1% of Flags Are Wrong"

Two different numbers. The false-positive rate: of human essays, the share flagged. It is set by the threshold and measured on some population of writers; it can differ on other writers. The share of flags that are human also depends on how common AI text is.

In this hypothetical, the detector catches 90% and has 1% false alarms, both held fixed. At 2% AI prevalence, about 35% of flags are human. At 20% prevalence, about 4%.

Key takeaway: With FPR held fixed, the chance a flagged person is innocent depends on prevalence.

53. Detectors Flag the US Constitution as AI-Written

54. Memorized Human Text Can Trigger AI Detectors

Detecting without a watermark · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈67.5 min

The underlying reason, as a sketch for perplexity-based detection. Such a detector asks, in effect: how predictable is this text to a language model? Put texts along that line. Typical AI output is very predictable. So is memorised human text, which sits even further toward predictable. Everyday human writing and unusual human writing sit further along.

Repeated or memorized human passages can be highly predictable and trigger some detectors. Being in a training set is not the same as being memorised, and not every detector is a raw perplexity threshold: Binoculars, for example, scores the ratio of perplexity to cross-perplexity, which involves two models (§3). An AI flag alone proves neither AI authorship nor plagiarism.

Key takeaway: Perplexity-based detectors measure predictability, and memorised human text can be very predictable.

60. Flip the Question: Record Origin Instead of Spotting Fakes

Provenance and policy · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈74 min

Detection has to keep up with every new generator. Provenance flips the question: content carries signed claims about where it came from.

Valid means the signed claims and their binding to the file check out. Whether to trust the signer is a separate decision. It only works if tools sign and the record survives. And provenance labels AI content too: a generator can sign "made with model X".

Key takeaway: Provenance records signed claims; trusting the signer is separate.

61. A C2PA Manifest: Signed Claims Locked to the File

Provenance and policy · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈75 min

C2PA defines a manifest attached to the file. It holds claims, such as "taken by camera X" or "cropped"; a hash of the covered file data; and a signature, where the signer's key seals both together.

A viewer recomputes the hash over the covered data and checks the signature: if the signature and covered data match, it shows valid. Change covered data and the hash no longer matches: invalid.

Key takeaway: A manifest locks signed claims to specific data.

The figure

Schematic: an image file with an attached manifest holding claims, a hash and a signature.

Precise version

The slide's "claims" row stands for these signed statements. The signature is verified with the signer's certified public key.

67. EU Law Now Expects a Watermark in Chatbot Text

Provenance and policy · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈82.5 min

Zoom in on the EU, because it is where text watermarks became expected. Two layers.

The AI Act, Article 50(2), is binding law: providers of generative AI must mark outputs in a machine-readable way, and text is included. It does not say how.

The Code of Practice is voluntary; about 190 organisations signed it, including OpenAI, Anthropic and Google. For text of 200 tokens or more, the Code's tool is an invisible watermark, because plain text cannot carry metadata the way an image file can.

The rule applies from 2 August 2026; systems already on the market must comply by 2 December 2026. The law sets the goal; the Code picks the watermark.

Key takeaway: The EU law requires marking; its Code of Practice makes that a watermark for text.

The figure

Two boxes: AI Act Art. 50(2) (binding) and the Code of Practice (voluntary), with the text rule below.

The texts

  • Art. 50(2): "Providers of AI systems, including general-purpose AI systems, generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content, shall ensure that the outputs of the AI system are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated."
  • Code of Practice, Measure 1.1, on text: "given that free-form text cannot transport metadata, a single-layer of marking as described in Sub-measure 1.1.2 is considered sufficient". The Code treats text under 200 tokens as very short text. Fingerprinting or logging alone is not enough.
  • Commission: "Even though adherence to the code is voluntary, the transparency requirements under article 50 of the AI Act are legal obligations." The final Code is dated 10 June 2026; on 31 July 2026 the Commission reported "about 190 organisations" signing, naming among others Anthropic, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Mistral and OpenAI.
  • Dates: Art. 113 applies Art. 50 from 2 August 2026. Regulation 2026/1744, Art. 111(4): systems "placed on the market before 2 August 2026 shall take the necessary steps in order to comply with Article 50(2) by 2 December 2026".

68. ChatGPT and Claude Now Watermark Their Text

Provenance and policy · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈84 min

Two chatbots now watermark their text. OpenAI: eligible ChatGPT and Codex text output, in the EU only. It shifts the randomness in the model's word choices. OpenAI's own evaluation: swapping 10% of words for synonyms cut detection from about 92% to 66%. That is one vendor test on 400-token passages, not general performance.

Anthropic: supported Claude models, worldwide; some platforms or formats may not support every marking type. Text gets a watermark and generated files get C2PA credentials where supported. The detector is in private preview only, so the public cannot check.

Some history: in 2024 OpenAI said it had built a text watermark and held it back, partly because translation or rewording beat it. Both companies now point to the EU AI Act. Not everyone signed: xAI, maker of Grok, did not sign the Code.

Key takeaway: Text watermarks are now live, driven by the EU rule, but checking them is not public.

70. Embed, Guess, Sign: Side by Side

Synthesis · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈86 min

Embed: the generator acts; the mark must survive edits and paraphrase; it fails when rewritten away; a hit means a keyed generator likely made it.

Guess: anyone acts; it must keep up with new generators; it fails by false accusation; a hit means only that the score is high.

Sign: creator and tools act; the record must survive sharing; it fails when stripped; a hit means these signers made these claims.

They complement each other; soft bindings join embed and sign.

Key takeaway: Each kind of evidence has its own failure; combine them.

71. Origin Is Not Truth: Three Cases

Synthesis · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈87 min

An AI chart with honest credentials could be wrong. A camera photo whose record was stripped has no origin signal, and could still be staged or misleading. A human-written false claim carries no mark and is false.

Origin tools answer who made it. Checking facts is a separate job.

Key takeaway: Origin and truth are independent questions.

72. Checklist for Any "We Can Detect It" Claim

Synthesis · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈88.5 min

Seven questions. How many tokens or pixels? Short content is weak evidence. False alarms measured on whose data? Nominal is not measured. How common is AI content here? Base rates drive accusations. Which attack, with what access? Each study is one rung. Tested on new generators? Held-out data is harder. Who holds the key or signal? Keyed and keyless tests differ. Which threshold, and as of when? Results hold for that setting and date.

Key takeaway: Seven questions turn a headline into a checkable claim.

73. Takeaways

Synthesis · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈89.5 min

Watermarks are a keyed signal, detectable while enough of it survives. Their robustness depends on the attack, the length and the error rate. Without marks, at fixed true- and false-positive rates, rarer AI content means a larger fraction of flags are false. Provenance signs claims, and can be stripped.

Origin is not truth, and absence of a signal proves little.

Key takeaway: Origin is not truth; absence of a signal proves little.
