1  Why Gaussians?

1.1 The deck's three cards, unpacked

The lecture's claim is that one distribution family carries three separate loads in deep learning. Generative models: a diffusion model's forward process is nothing but repeated Gaussian corruption, and its generator is a chain of Gaussian sampling steps — Section 8 builds this from the one-step recursion up to the DDPM notation of Ho et al. [2]. Classification: modeling each class's features as a Gaussian and inverting by Bayes gives Gaussian discriminant analysis — Section 9, which is also the lecture's first genuinely multivariate maximum-likelihood computation. Inference under noise: observing a Gaussian signal through additive Gaussian noise leaves a Gaussian posterior whose mean is a shrinkage of the observation — Section 7, delivering the closed-form MMSE estimator that prob07 promised.

1.2 Why this family and not another

Three prior results make the Gaussian the default rather than a convenient choice. From prob03: among all densities on $\mathbb{R}$ with fixed mean and variance, the Gaussian has maximum differential entropy [5, Ch. 8] — it is the least-committed noise model. From prob05: the CLT makes sums of many small independent effects approximately Gaussian regardless of the ingredients' distributions. And — the content of this lecture — the family is closed under everything one wants to do: affine maps, marginalization, conditioning, and convolution all stay Gaussian (Theorem 2), which is why every computation in Sections 7–9 terminates in a formula rather than an integral.

2  Warm-Up: Two Dimensions

2.1 Definition and the joint density

Definition (bivariate normal, nondegenerate). $(X_1, X_2)$ is bivariate normal with means $\mu_1, \mu_2$, variances $\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2 \gt 0$ and correlation $\rho \in (-1, 1)$ if its joint density is

\[ f(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{\det \Sigma}} \exp\!\Big( -\tfrac{1}{2} (x - \mu)^{\mathsf T} \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu) \Big), \qquad \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 \\ \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 & \sigma_2^2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The conditions matter: $\sigma_i \gt 0$ and $|\rho| \lt 1$ are exactly what make $\Sigma$ invertible ($\det \Sigma = \sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2(1 - \rho^2) \gt 0$), so the formula is well defined. The boundary cases $|\rho| = 1$ are the 2D degenerate Gaussians of Section 4.3: all mass on a line, no joint density. Explicitly, by the $2 \times 2$ inverse formula $\big(\begin{smallmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{smallmatrix}\big)^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\big(\begin{smallmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a\end{smallmatrix}\big)$,

\[ \Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 - \rho^2} \begin{pmatrix} 1/\sigma_1^2 & -\rho/(\sigma_1\sigma_2) \\ -\rho/(\sigma_1\sigma_2) & 1/\sigma_2^2 \end{pmatrix}, \]

so the exponent, written out, is the classical form

\[ -\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)} \left[ \frac{(x_1-\mu_1)^2}{\sigma_1^2} - \frac{2\rho (x_1-\mu_1)(x_2-\mu_2)}{\sigma_1 \sigma_2} + \frac{(x_2-\mu_2)^2}{\sigma_2^2} \right]. \]

Source slip (confirmed): the LaTeX source writes the exponent without the transpose, as $(x - \mu)\,\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)$ — dimensionally a $2\times 1$ column cannot left-multiply a $2 \times 2$ matrix. The deck's form above, with $(x-\mu)^{\mathsf T}$, is correct.

2.2 The running example, every number rechecked

The deck fixes $\mu = 0$, $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1$, $\rho = 0.6$, so $\Sigma = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0.6 \\ 0.6 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$. Checks: $\det \Sigma = 1 - 0.36 = 0.64$ ✓ and $\Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.64}\big(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -0.6 \\ -0.6 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$ ✓. Eigen-structure: for any matrix $\big(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$ the vectors $(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$ and $(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}$ are eigenvectors with eigenvalues $1 + \rho$ and $1 - \rho$; here $\lambda_1 = 1.6$ along $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.4$ along $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}$ ✓, matching the deck.

2.3 Geometry: why the level sets are ellipses

A level set $\{x : x^{\mathsf T} \Sigma^{-1} x = c\}$ is best read in the eigenbasis. Write $\Sigma = Q \Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}$ with orthonormal eigenvector columns $q_i$ and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$ (Section 3.3), and substitute $y = Q^{\mathsf T} x$ (a rotation):

\[ x^{\mathsf T} \Sigma^{-1} x = y^{\mathsf T} \Lambda^{-1} y = \frac{y_1^2}{\lambda_1} + \frac{y_2^2}{\lambda_2} = c \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{y_1^2}{(\sqrt{c\lambda_1})^2} + \frac{y_2^2}{(\sqrt{c\lambda_2})^2} = 1, \]

an ellipse with semi-axes $\sqrt{c\lambda_i}$ along the eigenvectors $q_i$. For the running example the $1\sigma$ ellipse ($c = 1$) has semi-axes $\sqrt{1.6} \approx 1.265$ along $(1,1)$ and $\sqrt{0.4} \approx 0.632$ along $(1,-1)$ — a $2{:}1$ aspect ratio tilted at $45^\circ$, exactly the shape drawn in the deck's SVG (its radii 101 px and 51 px are in the same $2{:}1$ ratio ✓). The $\rho$-gallery follows: $\rho = 0$ gives a circle ($\lambda_1 = \lambda_2$); $\rho \to \pm 1$ sends $\lambda_2 = 1 - |\rho| \to 0$ and the ellipse collapses toward the line $x_2 = \pm x_1$.

2.4 Theorem 1: slicing, full statement

Theorem 1 (2D conditional). Let $(X_1, X_2)$ be bivariate normal with mean $(\mu_1, \mu_2)$ and covariance entries $\sigma_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$, with $\sigma_{22} \gt 0$ and $|\rho| \lt 1$. Then for every $x_2 \in \mathbb{R}$,

\[ X_1 \mid X_2 = x_2 \;\sim\; \mathcal N\!\Big( \mu_1 + \frac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_{22}} (x_2 - \mu_2),\;\; \sigma_{11} - \frac{\sigma_{12}\sigma_{21}}{\sigma_{22}} \Big). \]

In the $(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \rho)$ parameters: mean $\mu_1 + \rho \tfrac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2}(x_2 - \mu_2)$, variance $\sigma_1^2 (1 - \rho^2)$.

Source slip (confirmed): the LaTeX source states the conditional variance as $\sigma_{22} - \sigma_{12}\sigma_{21}/\sigma_{22}$, which is wrong (for the running example it would give the same number by symmetry, but for $\sigma_1 \neq \sigma_2$ it fails — and it does not even reduce to $\operatorname{Var}(X_1)$ when $\sigma_{12} = 0$). The deck's $\sigma_{11} - \sigma_{12}\sigma_{21}/\sigma_{22}$ is correct.

Two structural facts worth naming before the proof: the conditional variance does not depend on the observed value $x_2$ (every slice of the mountain has the same width), and it is strictly smaller than the marginal variance whenever $\sigma_{12} \neq 0$ — conditioning on correlated information always helps. Both survive verbatim in $n$ dimensions (Section 6.9).

2.5 Proof of Theorem 1 by completing the square

Derivation. Take $\mu = 0$ and unit variances (restore them at the end by $X_i \mapsto \mu_i + \sigma_i X_i$). The conditional density is $f(x_1 \mid x_2) = f(x_1, x_2)/f(x_2)$; as a function of $x_1$ with $x_2$ fixed, only the exponent matters. Group the 2.1 exponent in $x_1$:

\[ \frac{x_1^2 - 2\rho x_1 x_2 + x_2^2}{1 - \rho^2} = \frac{(x_1 - \rho x_2)^2 + (1 - \rho^2) x_2^2}{1 - \rho^2} = \frac{(x_1 - \rho x_2)^2}{1 - \rho^2} + x_2^2, \]

where the middle step is one completed square: $x_1^2 - 2\rho x_1 x_2 = (x_1 - \rho x_2)^2 - \rho^2 x_2^2$, and $-\rho^2 x_2^2 + x_2^2 = (1-\rho^2)x_2^2$. Hence

\[ f(x_1, x_2) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-\rho^2)}} \exp\!\Big( \! -\frac{(x_1 - \rho x_2)^2}{2(1-\rho^2)} \Big)}_{=\,f(x_1 \mid x_2)} \; \cdot \; \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x_2^2/2}}_{=\,f(x_2)} . \]

The first factor is, for each fixed $x_2$, exactly the $\mathcal N(\rho x_2, \, 1 - \rho^2)$ density; integrating over $x_1$ confirms the second factor is the $X_2$ marginal (so $X_2 \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$ — the 2D case of "marginals of a Gaussian are Gaussian"). Restoring means and scales gives the theorem. $\blacksquare$

Worked slice rechecked. Running example, condition on $X_2 = 1$: mean $\rho x_2 = 0.6 \cdot 1 = 0.6$ ✓, variance $1 - \rho^2 = 1 - 0.36 = 0.64$ ✓, i.e. $X_1 \mid X_2 = 1 \sim \mathcal N(0.6, 0.64)$, standard deviation $0.8$. The deck's slicing SVG is metrically consistent: with 80 px per unit, the conditional-mean marker sits $0.6 \cdot 80 = 48$ px right of center and the slice line $1 \cdot 80 = 80$ px above it ✓.

3  Positive Semidefinite Matrices

3.1 Definitions, and the eigenvalue characterization proved

Definition. A symmetric $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is positive semidefinite (PSD, $A \succeq 0$) if $x^{\mathsf T} A x \geq 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, and positive definite (PD, $A \succ 0$) if $x^{\mathsf T} A x \gt 0$ for all $x \neq 0$.

Symmetry is part of the definition here (the source's convention); for a symmetric matrix the spectral theorem — imported from linear algebra, not re-proved — provides real eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ and an orthonormal eigenbasis $z_1, \dots, z_n$, i.e. $A = Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf T} = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i z_i z_i^{\mathsf T}$ with $Q^{\mathsf T} Q = I$.

Lemma 3.1. $A \succeq 0 \iff$ all $\lambda_i \geq 0$; and $A \succ 0 \iff$ all $\lambda_i \gt 0$.

($\Rightarrow$) Test the definition on an eigenvector: $z_i^{\mathsf T} A z_i = \lambda_i \|z_i\|^2 = \lambda_i$, so the sign condition on the quadratic form forces the same sign condition on each $\lambda_i$.

($\Leftarrow$) Expand any $x$ in the eigenbasis, $x = \sum_i c_i z_i$ with $c_i = z_i^{\mathsf T} x$. Then by orthonormality

\[ x^{\mathsf T} A x = \sum_i \lambda_i (z_i^{\mathsf T} x)^2 = \sum_i \lambda_i c_i^2, \]

a nonnegative combination when all $\lambda_i \geq 0$; and strictly positive for $x \neq 0$ when all $\lambda_i \gt 0$, since some $c_i \neq 0$. $\blacksquare$

3.2 The workbench matrix, every number rechecked

$A = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$. PD certificate: $x^{\mathsf T} A x = 2x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + 2x_2^2 = x_1^2 + x_2^2 + (x_1 + x_2)^2$ — expand the right side: $x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + x_2^2 = 2x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + 2x_2^2$ ✓; a sum of squares that vanishes only at $x = 0$, so $A \succ 0$ with no eigenvalue computation. Eigenpairs: $A(1,1)^{\mathsf T} = (3,3)^{\mathsf T}$, $A(1,-1)^{\mathsf T} = (1,-1)^{\mathsf T}$, so $\lambda = 3$ at $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\lambda = 1$ at $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}$ ✓ (trace $4 = 3 + 1$ ✓, det $3 = 3 \cdot 1$ ✓).

3.3 Functions of a PSD matrix: square root and inverse

Lemma 3.2 (square root). If $A = \sum_i \lambda_i z_i z_i^{\mathsf T} \succeq 0$, then $A^{1/2} := \sum_i \sqrt{\lambda_i}\, z_i z_i^{\mathsf T}$ is symmetric PSD and satisfies $(A^{1/2})^2 = A$.

Proof. Symmetry and PSD-ness are immediate from the form (each $z_i z_i^{\mathsf T}$ is symmetric PSD and $\sqrt{\lambda_i} \geq 0$). Squaring, the cross terms die by orthonormality, $z_i z_i^{\mathsf T} z_j z_j^{\mathsf T} = (z_i^{\mathsf T} z_j)\, z_i z_j^{\mathsf T} = \delta_{ij}\, z_i z_i^{\mathsf T}$:

\[ \Big( \sum_i \sqrt{\lambda_i}\, z_i z_i^{\mathsf T} \Big)^2 = \sum_{i,j} \sqrt{\lambda_i \lambda_j}\, z_i z_i^{\mathsf T} z_j z_j^{\mathsf T} = \sum_i \lambda_i z_i z_i^{\mathsf T} = A. \;\blacksquare \]

Remark. $A^{1/2}$ is in fact the unique PSD square root (uniqueness is a standard linear-algebra fact, not needed below — any $B$ with $BB^{\mathsf T} = A$ works for the constructions in Section 4). Likewise, if $A \succ 0$ then $A^{-1} = \sum_i \lambda_i^{-1} z_i z_i^{\mathsf T}$: multiply and use the same orthonormality collapse to get $\sum_i z_i z_i^{\mathsf T} = QQ^{\mathsf T} = I$.

Worked square root rechecked. For the workbench $A$: $A^{1/2} = \sqrt3 \cdot \tfrac12 \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\big) + 1 \cdot \tfrac12 \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\big) = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} (\sqrt3+1)/2 & (\sqrt3-1)/2 \\ (\sqrt3-1)/2 & (\sqrt3+1)/2 \end{smallmatrix}\big) \approx \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 1.366 & 0.366 \\ 0.366 & 1.366 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$ ✓. Direct check of $B^2 = A$: diagonal entry $\tfrac{(\sqrt3+1)^2 + (\sqrt3-1)^2}{4} = \tfrac{(4 + 2\sqrt3) + (4 - 2\sqrt3)}{4} = \tfrac84 = 2$ ✓; off-diagonal $\tfrac{2(\sqrt3+1)(\sqrt3-1)}{4} = \tfrac{2(3-1)}{4} = 1$ ✓.

3.4 PSD $\iff$ Gram: the $A = B^{\mathsf T} B$ characterization

Lemma 3.3. A symmetric $A$ is PSD $\iff$ $A = B^{\mathsf T} B$ for some matrix $B$.

($\Leftarrow$, the deck's exercise) $x^{\mathsf T} B^{\mathsf T} B x = (Bx)^{\mathsf T}(Bx) = \|Bx\|^2 \geq 0$ for every $x$. (Symmetry: $(B^{\mathsf T} B)^{\mathsf T} = B^{\mathsf T} B$.)

($\Rightarrow$) Take $B = A^{1/2}$ from Lemma 3.2: $B^{\mathsf T} B = A^{1/2} A^{1/2} = A$. $\blacksquare$

Read forward: covariance matrices will turn out to be exactly the PSD matrices — Section 5.2 shows every covariance is PSD, and Section 6.2 ($\operatorname{Cov}(DW + \mu) = DD^{\mathsf T}$) shows every PSD matrix is a covariance.

3.5 Why $\Sigma \succ 0$ buys a density: the normalization computed

Claim. If $V \succ 0$ then $\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \exp\!\big(-\tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T} V^{-1} x\big)\, dx = (2\pi)^{n/2} (\det V)^{1/2}$ — so the Definition-1 density below normalizes, and PD-ness is exactly what makes the integral finite.

Derivation. Write $V = Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}$ and rotate, $x = Qy$ (Jacobian $|\det Q| = 1$ since $Q$ is orthogonal):

\[ \int e^{-\frac12 x^{\mathsf T} V^{-1} x} dx = \int e^{-\frac12 \sum_i y_i^2 / \lambda_i}\, dy = \prod_{i=1}^n \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y_i^2 / (2\lambda_i)}\, dy_i = \prod_{i=1}^n \sqrt{2\pi \lambda_i}, \]

each 1D factor being the $\mathcal N(0, \lambda_i)$ normalization from prob03 — finite precisely because every $\lambda_i \gt 0$. The product is $(2\pi)^{n/2} \big(\prod_i \lambda_i\big)^{1/2} = (2\pi)^{n/2}(\det V)^{1/2}$. If instead some $\lambda_i = 0$, the integrand is constant $= 1$ along the direction $z_i$ (interpreting $V^{-1}$ on the complement) and the integral diverges: a singular $V$ admits no density on $\mathbb{R}^n$, which is why Definition 1 needs $V \succ 0$. $\blacksquare$

4  Three Definitions

4.1 The three definitions, in full

Definition 1 (density). Let $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ symmetric with $V \succ 0$. Then $X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, V)$ if $X$ has joint density

\[ f_X(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2} (\det V)^{1/2}} \exp\!\Big( -\tfrac12 (x - \mu)^{\mathsf T} V^{-1} (x - \mu) \Big), \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}^n. \]

(Section 3.5 verified this integrates to 1; it requires $V$ invertible.)

Definition 2 (construction). $X$ is multivariate normal if $X = DW + \mu$ for some matrix $D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, some $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^n$, and $W \sim \mathcal N(0, I_m)$, i.e. $W_1, \dots, W_m$ i.i.d. standard normal. (The joint density of $W$ is the product $f_W(w) = (2\pi)^{-m/2} e^{-\|w\|^2/2}$ — independence from prob04's product rule.)

Definition 3 (projections). $X$ is multivariate normal if for every $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ the scalar $a^{\mathsf T} X = \sum_i a_i X_i$ is normal, where constants count as degenerate normals $\mathcal N(c, 0)$.

The quantifier in Definition 3 is for all $a$, and the constant convention is not cosmetic: it is what lets $a = 0$ (and any $a$ annihilating a degenerate direction) pass the test. Also note Definition 3 makes no moment assumption — finiteness of all moments is a consequence (Section 4.4).

4.2 Definitions 2 and 3 do more: the degenerate case

Take $n = 2$, $D = (1, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ (one noise source, $m = 1$), $\mu = 0$: then $X = (W_1, W_1)^{\mathsf T}$ puts all its mass on the line $x_1 = x_2$. Its covariance is $V = DD^{\mathsf T} = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$, which is singular ($\det = 0$; eigenvalues $2$ and $0$), so by Section 3.5 there is no joint density and Definition 1 cannot apply. But Definition 2 applies by construction, and Definition 3 does too: $a^{\mathsf T} X = (a_1 + a_2) W_1 \sim \mathcal N(0, (a_1 + a_2)^2)$ — a genuine normal, or the constant $0$ when $a_1 = -a_2$ (this is where the convention earns its keep). In general, a Definition-2 vector lives on the affine subspace $\mu + \operatorname{range}(D)$, and $\operatorname{range}(DD^{\mathsf T}) = \operatorname{range}(D)$, so the support is $\mu + \operatorname{range}(V)$: singular directions of the covariance are exactly the deterministic directions of the vector. Degenerate Gaussians are not pathologies — they appear whenever data sits on (or near) a low-dimensional subspace, which is the normal state of affairs for learned representations.

4.3 The equivalence, stated precisely

Theorem (equivalence). (a) Nondegenerate case: for a random vector $X$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$, the following are equivalent: (i) $X$ satisfies Definition 1 for some $\mu$ and $V \succ 0$; (ii) $X = DW + \mu$ with $D$ square and invertible; (iii) $X$ satisfies Definition 3 and $\operatorname{Cov}(X) \succ 0$. (b) In general: Definitions 2 and 3 are equivalent (with matching $\mu = \mathbb E[X]$ and $V = \operatorname{Cov}(X) = DD^{\mathsf T}$), and each is equivalent to Definition 1 precisely when the covariance is invertible.

The deck's equivalence map draws two arrows solid — Def 2 $\Rightarrow$ Def 1 (invertible $D$; proved as part 4 of Theorem 2, Section 6.4) and Def 2 $\Rightarrow$ Def 3 (part 3 + part 1, Section 6.3) — and leaves the two reverse arrows dashed, "stated in the source, not proved today". They are proved next. One tool is imported: multivariate MGF uniqueness — if $M_X(s) = \mathbb E[e^{s^{\mathsf T} X}]$ and $M_Y(s)$ are finite and equal for all $s$ in a neighborhood of $0$, then $X$ and $Y$ have the same distribution. Prob05 stated the scalar version (also without proof — it needs complex analysis / characteristic functions); the multivariate version is Section 4.10 of the MIT OCW 6.436J notes [1] and rests on the same machinery (equivalently, the Cramér–Wold device). Everything else below is self-contained.

4.4 Closing the dashed arrows

Proposition (Def 1 $\Rightarrow$ Def 2). If $X$ has the Definition-1 density with parameters $(\mu, V)$, $V \succ 0$, then $X \overset{d}{=} V^{1/2} W + \mu$ with $W \sim \mathcal N(0, I_n)$.

Proof. Let $Y = V^{1/2} W + \mu$, a Definition-2 vector with $D = V^{1/2}$ invertible (Lemma 3.2; its eigenvalues $\sqrt{\lambda_i} \gt 0$). By part 4 of Theorem 2 (Section 6.4 — whose proof uses only Definition 2, so no circularity), $Y$ has the Definition-1 density with mean $\mu$ and covariance $DD^{\mathsf T} = V^{1/2}V^{1/2} = V$: the same density as $X$. Two random vectors with the same density have the same distribution. $\blacksquare$

Proposition (Def 3 $\Rightarrow$ Def 2). If every $a^{\mathsf T} X$ is normal, then $X \overset{d}{=} V^{1/2} W + \mu$ where $\mu = \mathbb E[X]$ and $V = \operatorname{Cov}(X)$.

Proof. Step 1 (moments exist). Each coordinate $X_i = e_i^{\mathsf T} X$ is normal, hence has finite moments of all orders (prob05), so $\mu$ and $V$ are well defined; $V \succeq 0$ by Section 5.2.

Step 2 (identify each projection's parameters). For fixed $s$, the scalar $s^{\mathsf T} X$ is normal by hypothesis; its mean and variance need no Gaussianity, only linearity and bilinearity (Section 5): $\mathbb E[s^{\mathsf T} X] = s^{\mathsf T}\mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(s^{\mathsf T} X) = s^{\mathsf T} V s$. So $s^{\mathsf T} X \sim \mathcal N(s^{\mathsf T}\mu,\, s^{\mathsf T} V s)$.

Step 3 (compute the MGF). A normal's scalar MGF is finite everywhere (prob05: $\mathbb E[e^{tZ}] = e^{mt + v t^2/2}$ for $Z \sim \mathcal N(m, v)$; and $e^{c t}$ for the degenerate $v = 0$). Evaluating at $t = 1$:

\[ M_X(s) = \mathbb E\big[e^{s^{\mathsf T} X}\big] = M_{s^{\mathsf T} X}(1) = \exp\!\big( s^{\mathsf T}\mu + \tfrac12 s^{\mathsf T} V s \big) \qquad \text{for all } s \in \mathbb{R}^n. \]

Step 4 (match). Part 5 of Theorem 2 (Section 6.5, proved from Definition 2 alone) shows $Y = V^{1/2}W + \mu$ has exactly this MGF. Both MGFs are finite everywhere, so MGF uniqueness [1, §4.10] gives $X \overset{d}{=} Y$. $\blacksquare$

This closes the loop: Def 1 $\Rightarrow$ Def 2 $\Rightarrow$ Def 3 $\Rightarrow$ Def 2, and Def 2 $\Rightarrow$ Def 1 when the covariance is invertible. Honest accounting: the only unproved ingredient in this whole note is MGF uniqueness, flagged above. Source slip (confirmed): in two places the LaTeX source refers to "the density in Definition 2"; the density belongs to Definition 1 in the source's own numbering, and the deck silently corrects the label. A useful byproduct of the proof: a multivariate Gaussian's distribution is completely determined by $(\mu, V)$ — used repeatedly below.

4.5 Whitening (omitted detail)

The map in 4.4 inverts: if $X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, V)$ with $V \succ 0$, then $W = V^{-1/2}(X - \mu)$ satisfies $W \sim \mathcal N(0, I_n)$ — by part 3 of Theorem 2 it is Gaussian with mean $V^{-1/2}(\mu - \mu) = 0$ and covariance $V^{-1/2} V V^{-1/2} = I$ (using symmetry of $V^{1/2}$), and a Gaussian is determined by its parameters. Every nondegenerate Gaussian is a linear reshaping of white noise, and can be linearly “whitened” back; this is the standing trick behind Sections 3.5, 6.4, and every “reduce to the standard normal” step.

5  Vector Means & Covariances

5.1 Definitions and the transformation rules

Definition. For random vectors $X \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $Y \in \mathbb{R}^m$ with finite second moments: $\mathbb E[X] = (\mathbb E[X_1], \dots, \mathbb E[X_n])^{\mathsf T}$ (entrywise), and the cross-covariance matrix

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb E\big[ (X - \mathbb E[X])(Y - \mathbb E[Y])^{\mathsf T} \big] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y_j). \]

Write $\operatorname{Cov}(X) := \operatorname{Cov}(X, X)$; its diagonal holds the variances, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(Y, X)^{\mathsf T}$.

Lemma 5.1 (affine rules). $\mathbb E[AX + b] = A\,\mathbb E[X] + b$, and

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(AX + b,\; CY + d) = A \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)\, C^{\mathsf T}. \]

Proof. The mean rule is linearity of $\mathbb E$ applied entrywise. For the covariance rule, the shifts $b, d$ cancel inside the centered factors, and with $\tilde X = X - \mathbb E X$, $\tilde Y = Y - \mathbb E Y$: $\operatorname{Cov}(AX+b, CY+d) = \mathbb E[(A\tilde X)(C\tilde Y)^{\mathsf T}] = A\, \mathbb E[\tilde X \tilde Y^{\mathsf T}]\, C^{\mathsf T}$, pulling the constant matrices out of the entrywise expectations. $\blacksquare$ (Special cases used constantly: $\operatorname{Cov}(AX + b) = A\operatorname{Cov}(X)A^{\mathsf T}$, and $\operatorname{Var}(a^{\mathsf T} X) = a^{\mathsf T} \operatorname{Cov}(X)\, a$.)

5.2 Every covariance matrix is PSD (the deck's exercise, solved)

Claim. $\operatorname{Cov}(X) \succeq 0$ for every $X$ with finite second moments.

Proof. For any $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$, by the last special case of Lemma 5.1, $a^{\mathsf T} \operatorname{Cov}(X)\, a = \operatorname{Var}(a^{\mathsf T} X) \geq 0$, a scalar variance. (Symmetry is visible from the definition.) $\blacksquare$ Moreover $\operatorname{Cov}(X) \succ 0$ fails exactly when some $\operatorname{Var}(a^{\mathsf T} X) = 0$ with $a \neq 0$, i.e. when $X$ has a deterministic direction — tying back to Section 4.2. Combined with Section 6.2, the covariance matrices are exactly the PSD matrices.

5.3 Correlation, and $|\rho| \leq 1$ proved

$\rho_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) / (\sigma_i \sigma_j)$ when $\sigma_i, \sigma_j \gt 0$. That $|\rho_{ij}| \leq 1$ is Cauchy–Schwarz: for centered $U = X_i - \mu_i$, $T = X_j - \mu_j$ and every $t \in \mathbb{R}$, $0 \leq \mathbb E[(U - tT)^2] = \mathbb E[U^2] - 2t\,\mathbb E[UT] + t^2 \mathbb E[T^2]$; a quadratic in $t$ that is never negative has discriminant $\leq 0$, i.e. $\mathbb E[UT]^2 \leq \mathbb E[U^2]\,\mathbb E[T^2]$, which is $|\rho_{ij}| \leq 1$ after dividing by $\sigma_i^2\sigma_j^2$. Running-example check: $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = 0.6$, $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1$, so $\rho_{12} = 0.6$ ✓ — for the 2D parameterization of Section 2, the off-diagonal entry $\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$ divided by $\sigma_1\sigma_2$ returns $\rho$, i.e. the symbol $\rho$ in the density really is the correlation.

6  The Properties Theorem

Throughout, $X = DW + \mu$ is a Definition-2 Gaussian, $W \sim \mathcal N(0, I_m)$, $d_i^{\mathsf T}$ the $i$-th row of $D$. The theorem:

Theorem 2. (1) Each $X_i$ is normal; more generally $a^{\mathsf T} X$ is normal for every $a$. (2) $\mathbb E[X] = \mu$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X) = DD^{\mathsf T}$. (3) $AX + b$ is again multivariate normal, with mean $A\mu + b$ and covariance $A\,DD^{\mathsf T} A^{\mathsf T}$. (4) If $D$ is square and invertible, $X$ has the Definition-1 density with $V = DD^{\mathsf T}$. (5) $M_X(s) = \exp(s^{\mathsf T}\mu + \tfrac12 s^{\mathsf T} V s)$, and the pair $(\mu, V)$ determines the distribution. (6) If $X$ is jointly Gaussian, its components are independent $\iff$ they are uncorrelated ($V$ diagonal); blockwise version for subvectors. (7) If $(X, Y)$ are jointly Gaussian with $V_{YY} \succ 0$, then $\mathbb E[X \mid Y] = \mu_X + V_{XY} V_{YY}^{-1} (Y - \mu_Y)$, the residual is independent of $Y$, and $X \mid Y = y \sim \mathcal N\big(\mu_X + V_{XY}V_{YY}^{-1}(y - \mu_Y),\; V_{XX} - V_{XY} V_{YY}^{-1} V_{YX}\big)$.

6.1 Part 1: projections are normal

Recalled tool (prob05). If $U \sim \mathcal N(m_1, v_1)$ and $T \sim \mathcal N(m_2, v_2)$ are independent, then $U + T \sim \mathcal N(m_1 + m_2, v_1 + v_2)$: MGFs multiply under independence, and $e^{m_1 t + v_1 t^2/2} \cdot e^{m_2 t + v_2 t^2/2} = e^{(m_1+m_2)t + (v_1+v_2)t^2/2}$ is again a normal MGF; conclude by (scalar) MGF uniqueness. By induction the same holds for any finite independent sum, degenerate summands allowed.

Proof of part 1. $a^{\mathsf T} X = a^{\mathsf T} D W + a^{\mathsf T}\mu = \sum_{k=1}^m (a^{\mathsf T} D)_k\, W_k + a^{\mathsf T}\mu$: a finite sum of independent normals $(a^{\mathsf T} D)_k W_k \sim \mathcal N(0, (a^{\mathsf T} D)_k^2)$ plus a constant, hence normal — specifically $\mathcal N(a^{\mathsf T}\mu,\; \|D^{\mathsf T} a\|^2)$. Taking $a = e_i$: $X_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu_i, \|d_i\|^2)$. $\blacksquare$ (This is the arrow Def 2 $\Rightarrow$ Def 3.)

6.2 Part 2: the parameters

$\mathbb E[X] = D\,\mathbb E[W] + \mu = \mu$ and, by Lemma 5.1 with $\operatorname{Cov}(W) = I_m$ (unit variances on the diagonal, zero covariances off it, by independence): $\operatorname{Cov}(X) = D\, I_m\, D^{\mathsf T} = DD^{\mathsf T}$. With Lemma 3.3 this shows every PSD matrix arises as a Gaussian covariance: given $V \succeq 0$, take $D = V^{1/2}$.

6.3 Part 3: affine closure, and marginals

$AX + b = A(DW + \mu) + b = (AD)W + (A\mu + b)$ is again of Definition-2 form with matrix $AD$ — a one-line proof, and the reason Definition 2 is the workhorse: the other two definitions make this closure invisible. Parameters from part 2 / Lemma 5.1: mean $A\mu + b$, covariance $(AD)(AD)^{\mathsf T} = A\,V A^{\mathsf T}$. Corollary (marginals). Any subvector $(X_{i_1}, \dots, X_{i_k})$ is $A X$ for a selector matrix $A$ (rows $e_{i_1}^{\mathsf T}, \dots, e_{i_k}^{\mathsf T}$), hence Gaussian, with mean and covariance the corresponding sub-blocks of $\mu$ and $V$. Marginalizing a Gaussian is deleting rows and columns — no integral is ever computed.

6.4 Part 4: the density, by change of variables

Recalled tool (multivariate change of variables, stated). If $Y = g(Z)$ with $g$ an invertible differentiable map, then $f_Y(y) = f_Z(g^{-1}(y))\, |\det J_{g^{-1}}(y)|$. For the affine $g(w) = Dw + \mu$: $g^{-1}(x) = D^{-1}(x - \mu)$, constant Jacobian $\det D^{-1} = 1/\det D$.

Proof of part 4. With $f_W(w) = (2\pi)^{-n/2} e^{-\|w\|^2/2}$ and $w = D^{-1}(x-\mu)$:

\[ f_X(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2} |\det D|} \exp\!\Big( -\tfrac12 \big\|D^{-1}(x - \mu)\big\|^2 \Big), \]

and the exponent rearranges to the $V^{-1}$ quadratic form, with $V = DD^{\mathsf T}$:

\[ \|D^{-1}(x-\mu)\|^2 = (x-\mu)^{\mathsf T} (D^{-1})^{\mathsf T} D^{-1} (x-\mu) = (x-\mu)^{\mathsf T} (DD^{\mathsf T})^{-1} (x-\mu), \]

since $(D^{-1})^{\mathsf T} D^{-1} = (D^{\mathsf T})^{-1}D^{-1} = (DD^{\mathsf T})^{-1}$. Finally $|\det D| = (\det D \cdot \det D^{\mathsf T})^{1/2} = (\det V)^{1/2}$, giving exactly the Definition-1 density. $\blacksquare$ (This is the arrow Def 2 $\Rightarrow$ Def 1; note $V = DD^{\mathsf T} \succ 0$ automatically when $D$ is invertible, since $\|D^{\mathsf T} a\|^2 = 0$ forces $a = 0$.)

6.5 Part 5: the MGF

Derivation. By part 1, $s^{\mathsf T} X \sim \mathcal N(s^{\mathsf T}\mu,\, s^{\mathsf T} V s)$. Evaluate its scalar MGF (prob05) at $t = 1$:

\[ M_X(s) = \mathbb E\big[e^{s^{\mathsf T} X}\big] = M_{s^{\mathsf T} X}(1) = \exp\!\big( s^{\mathsf T} \mu + \tfrac12\, s^{\mathsf T} V s \big), \qquad s \in \mathbb{R}^n, \]

finite for every $s$. Since the MGF depends on $(\mu, V)$ only, MGF uniqueness (the one imported black box, Section 4.3) gives: two multivariate Gaussians with the same mean and covariance are identically distributed — regardless of which $D$ built them. $\blacksquare$ E.g. $D$ and $DR$ for any orthogonal $R$ give the same distribution ($DR(DR)^{\mathsf T} = DD^{\mathsf T}$): the construction is many-to-one, the distribution is not.

6.6 Part 6: independence $\iff$ zero covariance — both directions

Proof. ($\Rightarrow$, no Gaussianity needed) Independent $X_i, X_j$ with finite second moments satisfy $\mathbb E[X_iX_j] = \mathbb E[X_i]\mathbb E[X_j]$ (prob04), so $\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = 0$.

($\Leftarrow$, the Gaussian miracle) Suppose $X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, V)$ with $V = \operatorname{diag}(v_1, \dots, v_n)$. Build an independent-by-construction comparison vector: $Y_i = \mu_i + \sqrt{v_i}\, W_i$ with $W \sim \mathcal N(0, I_n)$ — i.e. $Y = \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{v_i})\, W + \mu$, a Definition-2 Gaussian whose components are independent because the $W_i$ are. Its mean is $\mu$ and its covariance is $\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{v_i})\, I \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{v_i}) = V$. By part 5, $X \overset{d}{=} Y$; independence is a property of the joint distribution, so $X$'s components are independent. $\blacksquare$

Blockwise version (used in Sections 6.9 and 7): if $(X, Y)$ jointly Gaussian with $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$, the same matching argument with block-diagonal $V$ shows the subvectors $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

Counterexample (joint Gaussianity cannot be dropped). Let $X \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$ and let $S$ be an independent random sign, $\mathbb P(S = \pm 1) = \tfrac12$; set $Y = SX$.

(i) $Y$ is standard normal: $\mathbb P(Y \leq t) = \tfrac12 \mathbb P(X \leq t) + \tfrac12 \mathbb P(-X \leq t) = \Phi(t)$, using the symmetry of $X$. (ii) $X, Y$ are uncorrelated: $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb E[S X^2] = \mathbb E[S]\,\mathbb E[X^2] = 0 \cdot 1 = 0$ by independence of $S$ and $X$. (iii) Yet they are not independent: $|Y| = |X|$ always, so e.g. $\mathbb P(|X| \leq 1, |Y| \gt 1) = 0 \neq \mathbb P(|X| \leq 1)\,\mathbb P(|Y| \gt 1)$.

The escape hatch is that $(X, Y)$ is not jointly Gaussian: $X + Y = (1 + S)X$ equals $0$ with probability $\tfrac12$ and is $\mathcal N(0,4)$-distributed otherwise — a mixture with an atom, not a normal (and not a constant), so Definition 3 fails at $a = (1,1)^{\mathsf T}$. Part 6's hypothesis "jointly Gaussian" is doing real work: two marginally Gaussian, uncorrelated variables need not be independent.

6.7 Part 7, step 1: the orthogonality characterization (recalled and completed)

Lemma 6.1. $\mathbb E[X \mid Y]$ is the unique (up to a.s. equality) function $h(Y)$ with finite second moment satisfying $\mathbb E[(X - h(Y))\, g(Y)] = 0$ for every (bounded measurable) $g$; entrywise for vectors.

Proof. That $h = \mathbb E[X|Y]$ passes the test is prob07's orthogonality principle (tower property: $\mathbb E[(X - \mathbb E[X|Y])g(Y)] = \mathbb E\big[ g(Y)\, \mathbb E[X - \mathbb E[X|Y] \mid Y] \big] = 0$). Conversely, if $h$ passes it, set $d(Y) = \mathbb E[X|Y] - h(Y)$; subtracting the two orthogonality statements gives $\mathbb E[d(Y) g(Y)] = 0$ for all $g$, and choosing $g = d$ (truncated if unbounded, then let the truncation grow) yields $\mathbb E[d(Y)^2] = 0$, so $h(Y) = \mathbb E[X|Y]$ a.s. $\blacksquare$

6.8 Part 7, step 2: guess linear, verify orthogonality

Derivation. Center everything: replace $X, Y$ by $X - \mu_X$, $Y - \mu_Y$ (restore the means at the end). Guess $\hat X = A Y$ with $A = V_{XY} V_{YY}^{-1}$, and let $\tilde X = X - \hat X$ be the residual.

The residual is uncorrelated with $Y$:

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) - A \operatorname{Cov}(Y, Y) = V_{XY} - V_{XY} V_{YY}^{-1} V_{YY} = 0, \]

using linearity of $\operatorname{Cov}$ in its first slot (Lemma 5.1). Uncorrelated upgrades to independent: the stacked vector $(\tilde X, Y)$ is an affine image of the jointly Gaussian $(X, Y)$ — explicitly $\big(\begin{smallmatrix} I & -A \\ 0 & I \end{smallmatrix}\big)\big(\begin{smallmatrix} X \\ Y \end{smallmatrix}\big)$ — hence jointly Gaussian by part 3, so part 6 (blockwise) makes $\tilde X$ independent of $Y$. Orthogonality test: for any $g$, $\mathbb E[\tilde X\, g(Y)] = \mathbb E[\tilde X]\, \mathbb E[g(Y)] = 0$ since $\mathbb E[\tilde X] = 0$. By Lemma 6.1, $\hat X = \mathbb E[X \mid Y]$. Restoring means:

\[ \mathbb E[X \mid Y] = \mu_X + V_{XY} V_{YY}^{-1} (Y - \mu_Y). \;\blacksquare \]

Why the guess is legitimate: nothing is assumed — the verification is unconditional. The Gaussian structure is used exactly once, at "uncorrelated $\Rightarrow$ independent"; for non-Gaussian $(X,Y)$ the same $\hat X$ is still the best linear estimator (prob07's LMMSE), but need not be $\mathbb E[X|Y]$.

6.9 Part 7, step 3: the full conditional distribution, and Theorem 1 as a corollary

Derivation. Decompose $X = \hat X + \tilde X$ with $\tilde X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y$. Conditioning on $Y = y$ freezes $\hat X$ at the constant $\mu_X + V_{XY}V_{YY}^{-1}(y - \mu_Y)$ and, by independence, leaves the distribution of $\tilde X$ untouched. $\tilde X$ is Gaussian (affine image, part 3) with mean $0$ and covariance

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde X) = \operatorname{Cov}(X - AY, \, X - AY) = V_{XX} - A V_{YX} - V_{XY} A^{\mathsf T} + A V_{YY} A^{\mathsf T}, \]

and with $A = V_{XY}V_{YY}^{-1}$ the last two terms cancel ($A V_{YY} A^{\mathsf T} = V_{XY} V_{YY}^{-1} V_{YX} = V_{XY} A^{\mathsf T}$), leaving

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde X) = V_{XX} - V_{XY} V_{YY}^{-1} V_{YX}. \]

Hence $X \mid Y = y \;\sim\; \mathcal N\big( \mu_X + V_{XY}V_{YY}^{-1}(y - \mu_Y),\;\; V_{XX} - V_{XY}V_{YY}^{-1}V_{YX} \big)$: Gaussian in Gaussian out, conditional covariance free of $y$ (all slices congruent), and covariance reduced by the PSD matrix $V_{XY}V_{YY}^{-1}V_{YX}$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 1 recovered. Take scalar blocks $X = X_1$, $Y = X_2$: mean $\mu_1 + \tfrac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_{22}}(x_2 - \mu_2)$, variance $\sigma_{11} - \tfrac{\sigma_{12}\sigma_{21}}{\sigma_{22}}$ — exactly Section 2.4, now with the "deeper reason" on display: slicing is projection onto the residual. Running-example recheck: $0 + \tfrac{0.6}{1}(1 - 0) = 0.6$ ✓, $1 - \tfrac{0.36}{1} = 0.64$ ✓.

6.10 New tools: completing the square in matrix form, and the density route

The deck derives part 7 without ever touching the density. For completeness — and because Section 8.7 needs the scalar version — here is the density route (Bishop [4, Ch. 2.3] runs it the same way), with its two lemmas proved.

Lemma 6.2 (matrix completing the square). For symmetric $P \succ 0$ and any $b$:

\[ x^{\mathsf T} P x - 2 b^{\mathsf T} x = (x - P^{-1}b)^{\mathsf T} P\, (x - P^{-1}b) - b^{\mathsf T} P^{-1} b. \]

Proof. Expand the right side: $x^{\mathsf T} P x - x^{\mathsf T} P P^{-1} b - b^{\mathsf T} P^{-1} P x + b^{\mathsf T} P^{-1} P P^{-1} b - b^{\mathsf T} P^{-1} b = x^{\mathsf T} P x - 2b^{\mathsf T} x$, using symmetry of $P, P^{-1}$ to merge the two middle terms and cancel the last two. $\blacksquare$ (Read off: a density $\propto \exp(-\tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T} P x + b^{\mathsf T} x)$ is $\mathcal N(P^{-1}b,\, P^{-1})$ — the coefficient matrix of the quadratic term is the inverse covariance; the mean is where the quadratic is minimized.)

Lemma 6.3 (block factorization / Schur complement). Let $V = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} V_{XX} & V_{XY} \\ V_{YX} & V_{YY} \end{smallmatrix}\big) \succ 0$ (so $V_{YY} \succ 0$), $A = V_{XY}V_{YY}^{-1}$, and $S = V_{XX} - V_{XY}V_{YY}^{-1}V_{YX}$ (the Schur complement of $V_{YY}$). Then

\[ V = \begin{pmatrix} I & A \\ 0 & I \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} S & 0 \\ 0 & V_{YY} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ A^{\mathsf T} & I \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det V = \det S \cdot \det V_{YY}, \]

and consequently, for $u = x - \mu_X$, $v = y - \mu_Y$,

\[ \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \end{pmatrix}^{\mathsf T} V^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \end{pmatrix} = (u - Av)^{\mathsf T} S^{-1} (u - Av) \; + \; v^{\mathsf T} V_{YY}^{-1} v. \]

Proof. Multiply the three factors: the product's blocks are $(S + AV_{YY}A^{\mathsf T},\; AV_{YY};\; V_{YY}A^{\mathsf T},\; V_{YY})$, and $A V_{YY} = V_{XY}$, $AV_{YY}A^{\mathsf T} = V_{XY}V_{YY}^{-1}V_{YX} = V_{XX} - S$ — so the product is $V$. Determinants multiply, and the triangular factors have determinant 1. Inverting the factorization (each factor is easy: triangular ones flip the sign of $A$), $V^{-1} = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} I & 0 \\ -A^{\mathsf T} & I \end{smallmatrix}\big) \big(\begin{smallmatrix} S^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & V_{YY}^{-1} \end{smallmatrix}\big) \big(\begin{smallmatrix} I & -A \\ 0 & I \end{smallmatrix}\big)$; sandwiching with $(u, v)$ gives the quadratic-form split, since $\big(\begin{smallmatrix} I & -A \\ 0 & I\end{smallmatrix}\big)\big(\begin{smallmatrix} u \\ v \end{smallmatrix}\big) = \big(\begin{smallmatrix} u - Av \\ v \end{smallmatrix}\big)$. $\blacksquare$

The density route to part 7. Insert the split into the Definition-1 joint density: it factors as

\[ f(x, y) = \underbrace{\frac{\exp\big(-\tfrac12 (u - Av)^{\mathsf T} S^{-1} (u - Av)\big)}{(2\pi)^{n_X/2}(\det S)^{1/2}}}_{f(x \mid y)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\exp\big(-\tfrac12 v^{\mathsf T} V_{YY}^{-1} v\big)}{(2\pi)^{n_Y/2}(\det V_{YY})^{1/2}}}_{f(y)}, \]

using $\det V = \det S \det V_{YY}$ to split the normalizer. The second factor is the $Y$ marginal (consistent with Section 6.3), and the first, for fixed $y$, is the $\mathcal N(\mu_X + A(y - \mu_Y),\, S)$ density — the same answer as 6.9, and Section 2.5 is its $1{+}1$ scalar case. The conditional covariance is the Schur complement; that name is the standard one in the linear-algebra literature.

7  The Gaussian Channel

The additive-noise model of this section is the scalar Gaussian channel of information theory [5, Ch. 9]; here only its posterior is needed, not its capacity.

7.1 Setup and the joint covariance

$Y = X + Z$ with $X \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_x^2)$ and $Z \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_z^2)$ independent, $\sigma_x^2, \sigma_z^2 \gt 0$. The pair $(X, Y)$ is jointly Gaussian — it is the affine image $\big(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\big) (X, Z)^{\mathsf T}$ of the (independent, hence jointly) Gaussian $(X, Z)$ — with

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, X) + \operatorname{Cov}(X, Z) = \sigma_x^2 + 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2, \]

so the joint covariance matrix is $\big(\begin{smallmatrix} \sigma_x^2 & \sigma_x^2 \\ \sigma_x^2 & \sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$ ✓ (deck's matrix confirmed).

7.2 The posterior, read off part 7

Derivation. Part 7 with $V_{XY} = \sigma_x^2$, $V_{YY} = \sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2$, $V_{XX} = \sigma_x^2$, zero means:

\[ X \mid Y = y \;\sim\; \mathcal N\!\left( \frac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2}\, y,\;\; \frac{\sigma_x^2 \sigma_z^2}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2} \right), \]

the variance from $\sigma_x^2 - \tfrac{(\sigma_x^2)^2}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2} = \tfrac{\sigma_x^2(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2) - \sigma_x^4}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2} = \tfrac{\sigma_x^2\sigma_z^2}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2}$. By prob07's MMSE theorem, the posterior mean $\hat X = \tfrac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_x^2+\sigma_z^2} Y$ is the minimum-mean-squared-error estimate — the closed form prob07 promised. $\blacksquare$

Shrinkage reading: the slope $\tfrac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2} \in (0,1)$ always: the estimate pulls the observation toward the prior mean $0$, by exactly the signal's share of the total variance (the deck's SVG line below the identity). Precision form (omitted detail): the posterior variance satisfies

\[ \frac{1}{\operatorname{Var}(X \mid Y)} = \frac{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2}{\sigma_x^2\sigma_z^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_x^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_z^2} : \]

precisions add — prior information plus channel information. In particular the posterior variance is below $\min(\sigma_x^2, \sigma_z^2)$: observing through even terrible noise strictly helps.

7.3 Worked numbers and extremes, rechecked

$\sigma_x^2 = 1$, $\sigma_z^2 = \tfrac13$: slope $\tfrac{1}{1 + 1/3} = \tfrac{1}{4/3} = \tfrac34$ ✓, so $\hat X = \tfrac34 y$; variance $\tfrac{1 \cdot 1/3}{4/3} = \tfrac14$ ✓ (precision check: $1 + 3 = 4$ ✓). Extremes: $\sigma_z^2 \to 0$ gives slope $\to 1$, variance $\to 0$ (trust the observation); $\sigma_z^2 \to \infty$ gives slope $\to 0$, variance $\to \sigma_x^2$ (fall back on the prior); $\sigma_z^2 = \sigma_x^2$ gives slope $\tfrac12$, variance $\tfrac{\sigma_x^2}{2}$ — all three rows of the deck's table confirmed.

7.4 Two sampling orderings — and why the second one matters

Claim. The following two procedures produce the same joint distribution of $(X, Y)$: (forward) sample $X \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_x^2)$, then $Y = X + Z$ with fresh $Z \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_z^2)$; (observation-first) sample $Y \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2)$, then $X \sim \mathcal N\big(\tfrac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_x^2+\sigma_z^2}Y,\; \tfrac{\sigma_x^2\sigma_z^2}{\sigma_x^2+\sigma_z^2}\big)$.

Proof. Both procedures produce a jointly Gaussian pair — the first by Section 7.1; the second because $(Y, X) = (Y, \; aY + \tilde Z)$ with $a = \tfrac{\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_x^2+\sigma_z^2}$ and independent $\tilde Z$, an affine image of independent Gaussians. So by part 5 it suffices to match means (all zero: $\mathbb E[X] = a\,\mathbb E[Y] = 0$) and covariances. Second procedure: $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2$ ✓; $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = a \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \sigma_x^2$ ✓; $\operatorname{Var}(X) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(Y) + \operatorname{Var}(\tilde Z) = \tfrac{\sigma_x^4}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2} + \tfrac{\sigma_x^2\sigma_z^2}{\sigma_x^2+\sigma_z^2} = \sigma_x^2$ ✓. Same $(\mu, V)$, same distribution. $\blacksquare$

The forward ordering is how nature (or a diffusion forward pass) generates data; the observation-first ordering is how a generator must run — it holds $y$ and needs a coherent sample of the cleaner variable. Section 8.8 uses exactly this reversal, one diffusion step at a time.

8  Gaussian Diffusion

8.1 The forward recursion and its kernel

Fix $\beta \in (0, 1)$. Given $X_0$ (any starting distribution with an MGF finite near 0), define

\[ X_n = \sqrt{1 - \beta}\; X_{n-1} + \sqrt{\beta}\; Z_n, \qquad Z_n \sim \mathcal N(0, 1) \text{ i.i.d., independent of the past.} \]

This is a Markov chain (prob04) on $\mathbb{R}$ with Gaussian transition kernel $X_n \mid X_{n-1} = x \sim \mathcal N(\sqrt{1-\beta}\,x,\; \beta)$: shrink toward 0, then add a fixed dose of noise.

8.2 Variance preserving — and contracting (deck's exercise, generalized)

Claim. $\mathbb E[X_n] = (1-\beta)^{n/2}\, \mathbb E[X_0] \to 0$, and $\operatorname{Var}(X_n) - 1 = (1-\beta)^n \big( \operatorname{Var}(X_0) - 1 \big) \to 0$. In particular if $X_0 \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$ then $X_n \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ for every $n$: the standard normal is a fixed point.

Proof. Means: $\mathbb E[X_n] = \sqrt{1-\beta}\,\mathbb E[X_{n-1}]$, iterate. Variances (independence of $Z_n$ from $X_{n-1}$): $\operatorname{Var}(X_n) = (1-\beta)\operatorname{Var}(X_{n-1}) + \beta$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X_n) - 1 = (1-\beta)\big(\operatorname{Var}(X_{n-1}) - 1\big)$, a contraction with factor $1 - \beta \lt 1$; iterate. The fixed-point claim: with $X_0 \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$, each $X_n$ is a linear combination of independent Gaussians, hence Gaussian (Section 6.1's tool), with mean 0 and variance 1 by the recursions. $\blacksquare$

8.3 The MGF telescope, by full induction

Claim. For every $n \geq 0$ and $t$ where defined,

\[ M_{X_n}(t) = M_{X_0}\!\big( (1-\beta)^{n/2}\, t \big) \cdot \exp\!\Big( \tfrac{t^2}{2}\, \big(1 - (1-\beta)^n\big) \Big). \]

Proof. One step, by independence of $X_{n-1}$ and $Z_n$ and the standard-normal MGF $e^{t^2/2}$ (prob05):

\[ M_{X_n}(t) = \mathbb E\big[ e^{t\sqrt{1-\beta}X_{n-1}} \big] \, \mathbb E\big[ e^{t\sqrt{\beta}Z_n} \big] = M_{X_{n-1}}\!\big(\sqrt{1-\beta}\, t\big)\, e^{\beta t^2 / 2}. \]

Induction on $n$: the base $n = 0$ is trivial ($1 - (1-\beta)^0 = 0$). Assuming the claim at $n - 1$ and applying the one-step identity at argument $\sqrt{1-\beta}\,t$:

\[ M_{X_n}(t) = M_{X_0}\!\big( (1-\beta)^{(n-1)/2} \sqrt{1-\beta}\, t \big) \exp\!\Big( \tfrac{(1-\beta)t^2}{2} \big( 1 - (1-\beta)^{n-1} \big) \Big) e^{\beta t^2/2}, \]

and the exponents combine: $(1-\beta)\big(1 - (1-\beta)^{n-1}\big) + \beta = 1 - (1-\beta)^n$. $\blacksquare$ Two-step sanity check: at $n = 2$ the noise coefficient is $1 - (1-\beta)^2 = \beta + (1-\beta)\beta = \beta(2 - \beta)$. Source slip (confirmed): the LaTeX source's two-step display writes this exponent as $1 - (1-\beta)$; the geometric sum gives $1 - (1-\beta)^2$, as the deck notes on the slide and as the numerical check $\beta + (1-\beta)\beta = 0.0396 = 1 - 0.98^2$ (at $\beta = 0.02$) confirms.

8.4 Theorem 3: convergence to the standard normal

Theorem 3. For any $X_0$ whose MGF is finite in a neighborhood of 0, $X_n \to \mathcal N(0, 1)$ in distribution as $n \to \infty$.

Proof. Fix $t$. Since $(1-\beta)^{n/2} t \to 0$ and $M_{X_0}$ is finite near 0 and continuous there with $M_{X_0}(0) = 1$, the first factor of 8.3 tends to $1$; the second tends to $e^{t^2/2}$. So $M_{X_n}(t) \to e^{t^2/2}$, the $\mathcal N(0,1)$ MGF, for all $t$ in a neighborhood of 0; MGF convergence on a neighborhood of 0 implies convergence in distribution (the continuity theorem cited alongside MGF uniqueness — prob05 / [1]). $\blacksquare$ The striking part: the limit is the same for every starting distribution — the forward process is a data destroyer by design, and the destination is exactly the fixed point of 8.2.

8.5 The closed form for $X_n \mid X_0$, two ways

Claim. Conditionally on $X_0 = x_0$:

\[ X_n \mid X_0 = x_0 \;\sim\; \mathcal N\!\Big( (1-\beta)^{n/2}\, x_0, \;\; 1 - (1-\beta)^n \Big). \]

Way 1 (from the telescope). Condition the telescope on $X_0 = x_0$, i.e. replace $M_{X_0}(s)$ by $e^{s x_0}$: $M_{X_n \mid X_0 = x_0}(t) = \exp\big( (1-\beta)^{n/2} x_0\, t + \tfrac{t^2}{2}(1 - (1-\beta)^n) \big)$ — the MGF of the claimed normal; conclude by MGF uniqueness.

Way 2 (unrolling, independent check). Iterate the recursion:

\[ X_n = (1-\beta)^{n/2} x_0 \; + \; \sqrt{\beta} \sum_{k=1}^{n} (1-\beta)^{(n-k)/2}\, Z_k, \]

a constant plus a linear combination of independent standard normals: Gaussian, mean $(1-\beta)^{n/2}x_0$, variance $\beta \sum_{k=1}^n (1-\beta)^{n-k} = \beta \cdot \tfrac{1 - (1-\beta)^n}{1 - (1-\beta)} = 1 - (1-\beta)^n$ by the geometric sum. Same answer. $\blacksquare$

So the whole $n$-step corruption collapses to one Gaussian sample — the property that makes diffusion training practical (draw a random $n$, jump straight to $X_n$; no simulation of the chain).

8.6 The DDPM dictionary, and the schedule numbers rechecked

Ho et al. [2] write $\alpha_n = 1 - \beta_n$ and $\bar\alpha_n = \prod_{i=1}^n \alpha_i$, allowing a step-dependent schedule $\beta_n$; the closed form generalizes verbatim (the same unrolling, with products replacing powers) to

\[ X_n \mid X_0 = x_0 \sim \mathcal N\big( \sqrt{\bar\alpha_n}\, x_0,\; 1 - \bar\alpha_n \big), \]

which for the deck's constant schedule is $\bar\alpha_n = (1-\beta)^n$: amplitude $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_n} = (1-\beta)^{n/2}$, noise variance $1 - (1-\beta)^n$ — the 8.5 formula in the standard notation. Worked schedule rechecked ($\beta = 0.02$, $n = 100$): $\bar\alpha_{100} = 0.98^{100} = e^{100 \ln 0.98} \approx e^{-2.0203} \approx 0.1326$, matching the deck's "$\approx 0.133$" ✓; noise variance $\approx 0.8674$; and the deck's chart is metrically consistent (its curve passes through value $0.604$ at $n = 25$, and $0.98^{25} \approx 0.6035$ ✓). One clarifying remark the slide compresses: $0.133$ is the variance share $\bar\alpha_n$ of the signal; the signal's amplitude multiplier is $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{100}} = 0.98^{50} \approx 0.364$. Either way, at $n = 100$ the sample is mostly noise.

8.7 The exact reverse posterior $q(x_{n-1} \mid x_n, x_0)$ (the deck stops short of this)

Claim (Ho et al. [2], eq. (6)–(7); constant schedule shown, general schedule identical with $\alpha_n, \bar\alpha_n$). With $\alpha = 1 - \beta$ and $\bar\alpha_k = (1-\beta)^k$:

\[ X_{n-1} \mid (X_n = x_n, X_0 = x_0) \;\sim\; \mathcal N\big( \tilde\mu_n(x_n, x_0),\; \tilde\beta_n \big), \] \[ \tilde\mu_n = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{n-1})\, x_n + \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{n-1}}\,\beta\, x_0}{1 - \bar\alpha_n}, \qquad \tilde\beta_n = \frac{\beta\, (1 - \bar\alpha_{n-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_n}. \]

Derivation (completing the square). By the Markov property, $q(x_{n-1} \mid x_n, x_0) \propto q(x_n \mid x_{n-1})\, q(x_{n-1} \mid x_0)$ as a function of $x_{n-1}$ (Bayes, with $x_0$ carried along). Both factors are known Gaussians (the kernel of 8.1, and the closed form of 8.5 at step $n-1$), so the log-posterior is, up to constants in $x_{n-1}$,

\[ -\frac{(x_n - \sqrt{\alpha}\, x_{n-1})^2}{2\beta} \; - \; \frac{(x_{n-1} - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{n-1}}\, x_0)^2}{2 (1 - \bar\alpha_{n-1})}. \]

Collect the quadratic and linear coefficients of $x_{n-1}$ (Lemma 6.2 in one dimension: coefficient of $x_{n-1}^2$ is the posterior precision; precision times mean is the linear coefficient):

\[ \text{precision} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta} + \frac{1}{1 - \bar\alpha_{n-1}} = \frac{\alpha(1 - \bar\alpha_{n-1}) + \beta}{\beta(1 - \bar\alpha_{n-1})} = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_n}{\beta(1 - \bar\alpha_{n-1})}, \]

using $\alpha(1 - \bar\alpha_{n-1}) + \beta = \alpha - \bar\alpha_n + 1 - \alpha = 1 - \bar\alpha_n$ — so $\tilde\beta_n$ is the reciprocal, as claimed. Linear coefficient: $\tfrac{\sqrt\alpha}{\beta} x_n + \tfrac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{n-1}}}{1-\bar\alpha_{n-1}} x_0$; multiplying by $\tilde\beta_n$:

\[ \tilde\mu_n = \frac{\beta(1-\bar\alpha_{n-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_n} \left( \frac{\sqrt\alpha}{\beta}\, x_n + \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{n-1}}}{1-\bar\alpha_{n-1}}\, x_0 \right) = \frac{\sqrt\alpha (1-\bar\alpha_{n-1})\, x_n + \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{n-1}}\,\beta\, x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_n}. \;\blacksquare \]

Sanity checks. (i) $n = 1$: $\bar\alpha_0 = 1$ gives $\tilde\beta_1 = 0$ and $\tilde\mu_1 = x_0$ — conditioning on both endpoints of a one-step chain pins $X_0$ exactly. (ii) The coefficients of $x_n$ and $x_0$ in $\tilde\mu_n$ sum to $\tfrac{\sqrt\alpha(1-\bar\alpha_{n-1}) + \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{n-1}}\beta}{1-\bar\alpha_n}$, which at $\alpha \to 1$ tends to $1$ (a convex-combination flavor). (iii) $\tilde\beta_n \lt \beta$: the reverse step, told $x_0$, is less noisy than the forward kernel.

This exact posterior is what DDPM training actually regresses against (its mean, reparameterized through the noise, is the target of the $\epsilon_\theta$ network in [2]); at generation time $x_0$ is unknown, and the network's prediction stands in for it. The deck deliberately stops at the approximation below — this subsection is the missing rigorous step between the two.

8.8 The deck's approximation: one reverse step via the channel

Claim (stationary regime). If $X_{n-1} \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$ (late in the chain, justified by Theorem 3 / the fixed point of 8.2), then exactly

\[ X_{n-1} \mid X_n = x_n \;\sim\; \mathcal N\big( \sqrt{1-\beta}\; x_n, \;\; \beta \big). \]

Proof. $(X_{n-1}, X_n)$ is jointly Gaussian with $\operatorname{Var}(X_{n-1}) = 1$, $\operatorname{Var}(X_n) = (1-\beta) + \beta = 1$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_{n-1}, X_n) = \sqrt{1-\beta}\, \operatorname{Var}(X_{n-1}) = \sqrt{1-\beta}$. Part 7: mean $\tfrac{\sqrt{1-\beta}}{1} x_n = \sqrt{1-\beta}\, x_n$; variance $1 - \tfrac{(\sqrt{1-\beta})^2}{1} = 1 - (1-\beta) = \beta$. $\blacksquare$

Flag (deck slide "Reverse Step via the Gaussian Channel"): the slide's headline result $\mathcal N(\sqrt{1-\beta}\,x_n, \beta)$ is exactly right, but its derivation bullet reads "variance $(1-\beta)\beta/1 = (1-\beta)\beta \approx \beta$". That intermediate is the Section-7 posterior variance of the scaled signal $\sqrt{1-\beta}\,X_{n-1}$ (channel with $\sigma_x^2 = 1-\beta$, $\sigma_z^2 = \beta$: $\tfrac{(1-\beta)\beta}{1}$); dividing by the scale$^2$, $(1-\beta)$, recovers the variance of $X_{n-1}$ itself as exactly $\beta$ — no small-$\beta$ approximation is needed at that step. The genuine approximation on the slide is elsewhere and is honestly labeled: using the stationary $\mathcal N(0,1)$ for $X_{n-1}$, which is only asymptotically true early in the chain. Consistency check against 8.7: averaging the exact posterior over $x_0 \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$'s conditional given $x_n$ at stationarity indeed returns $\mathcal N(\sqrt{\alpha}\,x_n, \beta)$, matching.

8.9 Vectors, and the generation skeleton

Everything above is coordinatewise-independent, so it lifts to $\mathbb{R}^d$ verbatim: $X_n = \sqrt{1-\beta}\,X_{n-1} + \sqrt{\beta}\,Z_n$ with $Z_n \sim \mathcal N(0, I_d)$, closed form $X_n \mid X_0 = x_0 \sim \mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_n}\, x_0,\; (1 - \bar\alpha_n) I_d)$, limit $\mathcal N(0, I_d)$ — all covariances stay multiples of $I_d$, since the recursion never mixes coordinates. The sampler the deck sketches is Algorithm 2 of [2]: draw $x_T \sim \mathcal N(0, I_d)$; for $n = T, \dots, 1$ sample $x_{n-1} \sim \mathcal N(\mu_\theta(x_n, n),\, \beta I_d)$; return $x_0$. Every line is a Gaussian sample; all the learning is hidden in the mean function $\mu_\theta$ — the network's stand-in for the exact $\tilde\mu_n$ of 8.7, whose $x_0$-dependence is what must be learned. Scope: what this lecture does not cover — the variational training objective, its KL decomposition, score matching, and continuous-time limits — is the subject of Sohl-Dickstein et al. [3] and Ho et al. [2]; this note stays, like the deck, at the probability layer that those papers build on.

9  Gaussian Discriminant Analysis

9.1 The model and the decision rule

Labels $Y \in \{0, 1, \dots\}$ with priors $\pi_y = \mathbb P(Y = y)$; class-conditional features $X \mid Y = y \sim \mathcal N(\mu_y, \Sigma_y)$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$, each $\Sigma_y \succ 0$. Classification is prob07's MAP rule, with the density standing in for the likelihood:

\[ \hat y(x) = \arg\max_y \; \pi_y \, f(x \mid \mu_y, \Sigma_y) = \arg\max_y \Big[ \log \pi_y - \tfrac12 \log \det \Sigma_y - \tfrac12 (x - \mu_y)^{\mathsf T} \Sigma_y^{-1} (x - \mu_y) \Big], \]

taking logs (monotone) and dropping the shared $-\tfrac n2 \log 2\pi$. By prob07's Bayes-optimality theorem, if the model is correct this rule minimizes the probability of error among all classifiers.

9.2 The boundary: quadratic in general, linear for shared $\Sigma$ (the SVG's caption, proved)

Derivation (two classes). The boundary is where the two bracketed scores tie. Their difference is

\[ \tfrac12 (x-\mu_1)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_1^{-1}(x-\mu_1) - \tfrac12 (x-\mu_0)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_0^{-1}(x-\mu_0) + \tfrac12 \log\frac{\det\Sigma_1}{\det\Sigma_0} + \log\frac{\pi_0}{\pi_1} = 0, \]

whose $x$-quadratic part is $\tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T}(\Sigma_1^{-1} - \Sigma_0^{-1})x$: a genuine quadric surface whenever $\Sigma_0 \neq \Sigma_1$ — the curved boundary in the deck's SVG. If $\Sigma_0 = \Sigma_1 = \Sigma$, the quadratic terms cancel; expanding $(x-\mu)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu) = x^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}x - 2\mu^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}x + \mu^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mu$ for both classes, the difference collapses to the linear equation

\[ (\mu_0 - \mu_1)^{\mathsf T} \Sigma^{-1} x \;=\; \tfrac12\big( \mu_0^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mu_0 - \mu_1^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mu_1 \big) + \log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_0}, \]

a hyperplane with normal $w = \Sigma^{-1}(\mu_0 - \mu_1)$ — linear discriminant analysis [4, Ch. 4.2], and (one sigmoid away) the functional form of logistic regression. $\blacksquare$

9.3 The likelihood, assembled

Estimation decouples across classes (each sample's log-likelihood only involves its own class's parameters), so fix one class with samples $x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}$ i.i.d. $\mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)$. The log-likelihood, from Definition 1 and independence:

\[ \ell(\mu, \Sigma) = -\frac{Nn}{2}\log 2\pi \; - \; \frac N2 \log\det\Sigma \; - \; \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^N (x^{(i)} - \mu)^{\mathsf T} \Sigma^{-1} (x^{(i)} - \mu), \]

matching the deck's display ✓ (prob07's recipe: log of a product of densities).

9.4 Maximizing over $\mu$

Lemma 9.1 (gradient of a quadratic form). For symmetric $M$: $\nabla_\mu\, (x - \mu)^{\mathsf T} M (x - \mu) = -2M(x - \mu)$.

Proof. Expand: $(x-\mu)^{\mathsf T}M(x-\mu) = x^{\mathsf T}Mx - 2\mu^{\mathsf T}Mx + \mu^{\mathsf T}M\mu$ (merging the cross terms by symmetry). Gradients in $\mu$: $0 - 2Mx + 2M\mu = -2M(x - \mu)$, using $\nabla_\mu (\mu^{\mathsf T} a) = a$ and $\nabla_\mu(\mu^{\mathsf T}M\mu) = 2M\mu$ for symmetric $M$ (both from the entrywise definition of the gradient). $\blacksquare$

Derivation. With $M = \Sigma^{-1}$ (symmetric since $\Sigma$ is):

\[ \nabla_\mu \ell = \Sigma^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^N (x^{(i)} - \mu) = \Sigma^{-1}\Big( \sum_i x^{(i)} - N\mu \Big) \overset{!}{=} 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \hat\mu = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N x^{(i)}, \]

since $\Sigma^{-1}$ is invertible. It is a maximum in $\mu$: $\ell$ is a concave quadratic in $\mu$ (Hessian $-N\Sigma^{-1} \prec 0$). The sample mean — the same answer as prob07's 1D Gaussian MLE, now in vector form. $\blacksquare$

9.5 Reparameterizing in the precision $\Phi = \Sigma^{-1}$, and the trace trick

Maximizing over $\Sigma$ directly puts the variable inside an inverse and a determinant. The deck's move: optimize over the precision $\Phi = \Sigma^{-1}$ instead (a bijection of PD matrices, $\log\det\Sigma = -\log\det\Phi$), and flatten the quadratic forms with the trace trick:

Lemma 9.2. $v^{\mathsf T} \Phi v = \operatorname{tr}(v v^{\mathsf T} \Phi)$ for any vector $v$ and square $\Phi$.

Proof. $v^{\mathsf T}\Phi v$ is a scalar, hence equals its own trace; and $\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \operatorname{tr}(BA)$ (both equal $\sum_{i,j} A_{ij}B_{ji}$) with $A = v^{\mathsf T}$, $B = \Phi v$ gives $\operatorname{tr}(v^{\mathsf T} \cdot \Phi v) = \operatorname{tr}(\Phi v \cdot v^{\mathsf T}) = \operatorname{tr}(v v^{\mathsf T} \Phi)$. $\blacksquare$

Summing over samples at $\mu = \hat\mu$, with the scatter matrix $S = \sum_{i=1}^N (x^{(i)} - \hat\mu)(x^{(i)} - \hat\mu)^{\mathsf T}$ (all the data the likelihood ever sees about $\Sigma$):

\[ \ell(\hat\mu, \Phi) = \text{const} + \frac N2 \log\det\Phi - \frac12 \operatorname{tr}(S\,\Phi). \]

9.6 The two matrix-calculus identities (deck's Exercises A and B), proved

Lemma 9.3 (Exercise A). $\nabla_X \operatorname{tr}(AX) = A^{\mathsf T}$, where $(\nabla_X f)_{ij} = \partial f / \partial X_{ij}$ and $X$ has independent entries.

Proof. $\operatorname{tr}(AX) = \sum_k (AX)_{kk} = \sum_{k,l} A_{kl} X_{lk}$ (the deck's hint). Each entry $X_{ij}$ appears exactly once, with coefficient $A_{ji}$; so $\partial \operatorname{tr}(AX)/\partial X_{ij} = A_{ji}$, i.e. $\nabla_X \operatorname{tr}(AX) = A^{\mathsf T}$. $\blacksquare$

Lemma 9.4 (Exercise B). For invertible $X$ with $\det X \gt 0$: $\nabla_X \log\det X = (X^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$.

Proof. Cofactor-expand along row $i$: $\det X = \sum_l X_{il} C_{il}$, where the cofactor $C_{il}$ ($(-1)^{i+l}$ times the minor deleting row $i$, column $l$) contains no entry of row $i$. So $\partial \det X / \partial X_{ij} = C_{ij}$, i.e. $\nabla_X \det X = C$. Cramer's adjugate identity $X^{-1} = \operatorname{adj}(X)/\det X$ with $\operatorname{adj}(X) = C^{\mathsf T}$ gives $C = \det X \cdot (X^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$. Chain rule: $\nabla_X \log\det X = \tfrac{1}{\det X}\, C = (X^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$. $\blacksquare$ (For symmetric PD $\Phi$, $(\Phi^{-1})^{\mathsf T} = \Phi^{-1}$, as the source's exercise remarks — the standard caveat that treating symmetric entries as constrained changes off-diagonal derivatives by a factor of 2 does not change the stationary point, since the unconstrained critical point found below is already symmetric.)

9.7 Solving for $\Sigma$, and Theorem 4

Derivation. By Lemmas 9.3 and 9.4,

\[ \nabla_\Phi\, \ell = \frac N2 (\Phi^{-1})^{\mathsf T} - \frac12 S^{\mathsf T} = \frac N2 \Sigma - \frac12 S \overset{!}{=} 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\Sigma = \frac1N S = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N (x^{(i)} - \hat\mu)(x^{(i)} - \hat\mu)^{\mathsf T}, \]

using symmetry of $\Phi^{-1} = \Sigma$ and $S$ to drop the transposes — exactly the deck's display ✓. That it is the global maximum, not a saddle: $\Phi \mapsto \log\det\Phi$ is concave on PD matrices (standard; Boyd & Vandenberghe [7, §3.1.5]) and $\operatorname{tr}(S\Phi)$ is linear, so $\ell(\hat\mu, \cdot)$ is concave and the unique critical point is the global maximizer. (When $S \succ 0$ — guaranteed a.s. once $N \gt n$ — $\ell \to -\infty$ on the boundary and at infinity, so the maximum is attained.) $\blacksquare$

Theorem 4 (Gaussian MLE). For i.i.d. $\mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)$ samples, the maximum-likelihood estimates are $\hat\mu = \tfrac1N\sum_i x^{(i)}$ and $\hat\Sigma = \tfrac1N\sum_i (x^{(i)} - \hat\mu)(x^{(i)} - \hat\mu)^{\mathsf T}$; in GDA, applied per class with $\hat\pi_y = N_y / N$ (the count MLE, prob07's Bernoulli/multinomial estimate).

Bias remark, computed. $\mathbb E[\hat\Sigma] = \tfrac{N-1}{N}\Sigma$: writing $\bar x = \hat\mu$,

\[ \sum_i (x^{(i)} - \bar x)(x^{(i)} - \bar x)^{\mathsf T} = \sum_i x^{(i)} x^{(i)\mathsf T} - N \bar x \bar x^{\mathsf T}, \]

(expand and use $\sum_i x^{(i)} = N\bar x$); take expectations with $\mathbb E[x x^{\mathsf T}] = \Sigma + \mu\mu^{\mathsf T}$ and $\mathbb E[\bar x \bar x^{\mathsf T}] = \tfrac1N \Sigma + \mu\mu^{\mathsf T}$ (the latter since $\operatorname{Cov}(\bar x) = \Sigma / N$ for i.i.d. samples): $N(\Sigma + \mu\mu^{\mathsf T}) - N(\tfrac1N\Sigma + \mu\mu^{\mathsf T}) = (N-1)\Sigma$. Divide by $N$. Same $\tfrac{N-1}{N}$ shrinkage as prob07's 1D variance MLE; vanishes as $N \to \infty$ (asymptotically unbiased), and $n$ plays no role in the factor. $\blacksquare$

Pipeline check (deck's closing slide): fit $(\hat\pi_y, \hat\mu_y, \hat\Sigma_y)$ per class by Theorem 4, classify by Section 9.1's rule — and in one dimension with two classes and shared variance this collapses exactly to prob07's two-Gaussian height classifier, with the 9.2 boundary reducing to the midpoint-shifted-by-prior threshold derived there.
