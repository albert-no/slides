1 · Title

0:00 – 0:15
  • Hi, I’m [name]. This is a position paper: our claim is that the term “machine unlearning” is overused in LLMs.
  • Joint work with my co-first author and Albert No at Yonsei.
~30 words. Don’t linger — get to the hook.

2 · One Term, Many Tasks

0:15 – 1:00
  • Today, “unlearning” is a single label stretched over very different tasks.
  • It can mean deleting training data for privacy. It can mean refusing harmful requests for safety. It can mean suppressing copyrighted text, or editing a fact.
  • These have different goals, different guarantees, different metrics — but they all get filed under one word.
  • Our position: only one of these is really “unlearning.” The rest need different names.
~85 words. This is the hook — slow down on the last line.

3 · What Unlearning Should Mean

1:00 – 1:45
  • So what should the word mean? Start from the motivation: GDPR’s “right to be forgotten.” A user can demand you remove their data — and that means removing its influence, not just hiding the visible traces.
  • Formally: you have a precisely specified forget set $F$, and a retain set — everything else.
  • An unlearned model should behave as if $F$ had never been seen — indistinguishable from a model retrained without it.
  • Why pick this definition out of all the candidates? Because it’s the only one with a concrete baseline — the retrained model. The other goals have no ground truth to check against.
~95 words. The “as if F had never been seen” line is the anchor of the whole talk.

4 · But the Field Does Something Else

1:45 – 2:35
  • Here’s the problem — a recurring pattern in LLM unlearning papers.
  • They claim retrain-indistinguishability in the framing.
  • They build a method that just controls the output — gradient ascent, refusal templates, obfuscation.
  • And they measure output failure — lower ROUGE or QA accuracy on forget queries, usually with no retrained reference at all.
  • So the goal is distributional, but the method and the metric are output-level.
  • To be clear — suppression and refusal matter. We’re only saying they are not unlearning.
~100 words. Trace the three boxes — claim → build → measure — as you say them.

5 · Why It’s Not Just Semantics

2:35 – 3:35
  • Why does this matter beyond vocabulary? Derived capabilities.
  • Say your forget set is a handful of math examples. The model didn’t just memorize them — it picked up general math ability from them.
  • Now: suppress the output, and the model refuses those specific problems — but it still solves new ones. The skill is intact.
  • Retrain without that data, and the model never had the skill — so it loses the ability.
  • So if you only suppress the output, the capability the data created is still there — you haven’t unlearned anything.
  • Same story for data poisoning: the backdoor survives surface suppression.
~110 words. Most persuasive slide — give it the most time.

6 · Surface Success Breaks Easily

3:35 – 4:15
  • And this isn’t hypothetical. Methods that pass the output metrics routinely break under mild perturbation.
  • Paraphrase, or another language, recovers the “forgotten” content.
  • Benign fine-tuning on unrelated data un-suppresses it.
  • Quantization alone can re-expose the targets.
  • The training influence was never removed — it was just hidden by the prompting protocol.
~75 words. List the three attacks crisply; land the last line.

7 · A Call for Action

4:15 – 4:50
  • So here’s what we ask of the field.
  • Reserve the word “unlearning” for retrain-indistinguishability.
  • Evaluate against a retrained reference — or a clearly stated proxy.
  • Probe derived capabilities — not just output on forget queries.
  • And if you’re doing output control, name it — don’t claim deletion.
  • Yes, indistinguishability is hard to measure. But that is not a license to blur the terminology.
~85 words.

~25 words.
