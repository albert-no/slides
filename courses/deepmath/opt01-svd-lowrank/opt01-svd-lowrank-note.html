1. SVD and Best Low-Rank Approximation

Message. Replace a matrix by one of lower rank, and say exactly how much is lost.

Lecture 10 of Deep Learning Math (150 minutes). The lecture asks one question: given a matrix, which matrix of rank at most $k$ is closest to it, and how close is it? The singular value decomposition answers it in closed form, and the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem proves that the answer cannot be beaten.

4. A Smaller Matrix, Controlled Error

Message. Low-rank approximation replaces a fully observed matrix $A$ by a matrix $B$ of rank at most $k$ and judges the result by the size of the residual $A - B$.

The setting: $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is given and every entry is observed; we choose $B$ with $\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k$. The slide shows one equation drawn as three boxed matrices: the data $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 0\\ 4 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ (rank 2, grey box) equals a rank-1 candidate $B = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}1.5 & 1.5\\ 4.5 & 4.5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ (blue box) plus the residual $A - B = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}1.5 & -1.5\\ -0.5 & 0.5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ (red dashed box). The decomposition $A = B + (A - B)$ holds for any $B$; what changes from one candidate to another is how big the residual is, and that size will be measured by a matrix norm (Part 2). This sets up the two questions for the lecture: which rank-$k$ matrix is closest, and how small can the error be. At this point $B$ is just one candidate; nothing yet says it is the best one.

Storage is a reason to want low rank, but not automatically a saving: a rank-$k$ matrix can be stored as two factors with $k(m+n)$ numbers, which beats the $mn$ entries of $A$ only when $k(m+n) \lt mn$. For the $2\times 2$ example with $k = 1$ both counts are $4$, so there is no saving at all; the gain appears for large matrices and small $k$.

Details

Full observation versus completion. Here every entry of $A$ is known and the residual is computed over all entries. Matrix completion is a different problem: only some entries are observed, the fit is measured on those, and the low-rank assumption fills in the rest. The methods of this lecture do not directly solve completion.

Why the problem is not convex. The feasible set $\{B : \operatorname{rank}(B) \le k\}$ is not convex: $\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ and $\operatorname{diag}(0,1)$ both have rank 1, but their midpoint $\tfrac12 I_2$ has rank 2. So convex-optimization guarantees do not apply; the lecture still reaches an exact answer through the SVD.

Check of the example. $B + (A - B) = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}1.5+1.5 & 1.5-1.5\\ 4.5-0.5 & 4.5+0.5\end{smallmatrix}\right] = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 0\\ 4 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right] = A$. Both columns of $B$ equal $(1.5, 4.5)$, so $\operatorname{rank}(B) = 1$.

Sources

Problem statement: Wilkinson, Multivariate Statistics (MATH3030 notes), §3.5 “Low-rank approximation”. The matrices $A$, $B$ and $A - B$ are our illustration (evidence E08); the storage count $k(m+n)$ is our arithmetic.

5. Recall: Span and Range

Message. Every output $Ax$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$, so the set of all outputs is the span of those columns.

Write $A = [\,a_1 \cdots a_n\,] \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with columns $a_j \in \mathbb{R}^m$, and take an input $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Multiplying out gives $Ax = x_1a_1 + \cdots + x_na_n$, a vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$: the entries of $x$ are the weights on the columns. The range (column space) collects every possible output, $\operatorname{range}(A) = \{Ax : x \in \mathbb{R}^n\} = \operatorname{span}\{a_1, \dots, a_n\}$, a subspace of the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$. The figure shows the case $m = n = 2$ with $a_1 = (2,1)$ and $a_2 = (1,2)$ as two blue arrows from the origin; the amber arrow is $Ax$ for $x = (1,1)$, the diagonal of the dashed parallelogram spanned by $a_1$ and $a_2$, i.e. $a_1 + a_2 = (3,3)$.

Details

The span of $v_1, \dots, v_k$ is the set of all combinations $\sum_i \alpha_i v_i$. A subspace is a nonempty set closed under addition and scalar multiplication; $\operatorname{range}(A)$ is one because $Ax + Ay = A(x+y)$ and $\alpha Ax = A(\alpha x)$. A basis is a linearly independent spanning list, and the dimension is its length (well defined by the Steinitz exchange lemma, proved in the backup note).

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, Mathematics for Machine Learning (CUP 2020), §2.7.3, Definition 2.23 and eq. (2.124): $\operatorname{Im}(A) = \{Ax\} = \operatorname{span}[a_1, \dots, a_n] \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$. Figure: our illustration.

6. Rank Counts Independent Directions

Message. The rank of $A$ is the dimension of its range: the number of independent output directions.

Definition: $\operatorname{rank}(A) = \dim \operatorname{range}(A)$, the number of vectors in any basis of the range. The block diagram shows what this means for the shape of $A$: if $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$ and $u_1, \dots, u_r$ is a basis of the range, then $A$ (a wide grey $m\times n$ block) factors as $U$ (a tall thin blue $m\times r$ block whose columns are the $u_i$) times $C$ (a short amber $r\times n$ block). Column $j$ of $C$ holds the coordinates of $a_j$ in that basis. The block sizes are schematic, not to scale. Rank is not the number of nonzero entries and not the number of columns: $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 1\\ 2 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ has four nonzero entries and two columns, yet both columns point along $(1,2)$, so its rank is 1.

Details

The factorization. Each column $a_j$ lies in $\operatorname{range}(A)$, so $a_j = Uc_j$ for a unique $c_j \in \mathbb{R}^r$. Stacking the columns gives $A = UC$ with $C = [c_1 \cdots c_n]$. Equivalently $A = \sum_{i=1}^r u_i \tilde c_i^\top$, a sum of $r$ rank-one matrices, where $\tilde c_i^\top$ is row $i$ of $C$.

Row rank equals column rank ($\operatorname{rank}(A^\top) = \operatorname{rank}(A)$) follows from the same factorization; the proof and the four-subspace picture are in the backup note.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §2.6.2 (rank as the number of linearly independent columns) and §2.7.3, eq. (2.124) ($\operatorname{rk}(A) = \dim \operatorname{Im}(A)$). The factorization $A = UC$ is our derivation from the definition.

7. Two Inputs Collapse to One Direction

Message. The matrix $C = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 1\\ 2 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ sends every input onto the single line through $(1,2)$, so its rank is 1.

Both columns of $C$ equal $(1,2)$, so $Cx = x_1(1,2) + x_2(1,2) = (x_1 + x_2)(1,2)$: whatever the input, the output is a multiple of $(1,2)$. The figure has two coordinate panels joined by an arrow labelled $C$. The left (input) panel marks $e_1$ and $e_2$ as blue dots and $(1,1)$ as an amber square. The right (output) panel shows the range as a dashed light-blue line through the origin with slope 2; $e_1$ and $e_2$ both land on the same blue dot $(1,2)$, and $(1,1)$ lands on the amber square $(2,4)$, further out on the same line. All points are drawn at their exact coordinates. Two independent inputs produce only one output direction: $\operatorname{range}(C) = \operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}$ and $\operatorname{rank}(C) = 1$.

Details

Image calculation: $Ce_1 = (1,2)$, $Ce_2 = (1,2)$, $C(1,1) = (2,4) = 2\cdot(1,2)$. Since every output is $(x_1 + x_2)(1,2)$ and $x_1 + x_2$ can be any real number, the range is the whole line $\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}$, not just a segment. A basis of the range is $\{(1,2)\}$, so the dimension is 1.

8. Null Space Means Lost Directions

Message. The null space collects the input directions that $A$ sends to zero; for $C$ it is the line spanned by $(1,-1)$.

Definition: $\operatorname{null}(A) = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : Ax = 0\}$, a set of inputs. For $C$, $Cx = (x_1 + x_2)(1,2)$ vanishes exactly when $x_1 + x_2 = 0$, so $\operatorname{null}(C) = \operatorname{span}\{(1,-1)\}$. The figure repeats the two panels of the previous slide. In the input panel a solid red line of slope $-1$ through the origin is the null space; a dashed red arrow from $e_2$ to $e_1$ is the vector $e_1 - e_2 = (1,-1)$, which lies along that line. In the output panel the origin is a red dot labelled $C(1,-1) = 0$, and $Ce_1 = Ce_2$ is a single blue dot on the dashed range line. The point: $e_1$ and $e_2$ differ by a null vector, so $C$ cannot tell them apart. The null space lives in the input space $\mathbb{R}^n$; it is not a set of outputs and not part of the range.

Details

$\operatorname{null}(A)$ is a subspace: if $Ax = 0$ and $Ay = 0$ then $A(x + y) = 0$ and $A(\alpha x) = 0$. Inputs differing by a null vector give the same output: $A(x + z) = Ax$ when $z \in \operatorname{null}(A)$. For $C$, solving $x_1 + x_2 = 0$ gives $x = t(1,-1)$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$; one free parameter, so $\dim \operatorname{null}(C) = 1$.

Sources

Definition: Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §2.7.3, Definition 2.23 (kernel/null space). Example: our calculation (evidence E09).

9. Rank and Nullity Add Up

Message. For any $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ the kept directions and the lost directions add up to the number of columns $n$, so low rank forces a large null space.

Rank–nullity theorem: for every $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, square or rectangular, $\operatorname{rank}(A) + \dim \operatorname{null}(A) = n$. The count is over columns, i.e. input dimensions, not rows. The bar below the theorem splits all $n$ input dimensions into a solid blue part labelled “rank: kept” and a dashed red part labelled “nullity: lost”; the widths are schematic. Check on $C$: rank 1 plus nullity 1 equals 2 columns. For a wide matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 3}$ the rank is at most 2 (the range sits inside $\mathbb{R}^2$), so the nullity is at least 1: some nonzero input is always lost. This is the fact used later for an arbitrary competitor $B$: if $\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k$ then $\dim \operatorname{null}(B) \ge n - k$.

Details

Proof. Let $x_1, \dots, x_t$ be a basis of $\operatorname{null}(A)$ and extend it to a basis $x_1, \dots, x_t, y_1, \dots, y_s$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, so $t + s = n$. It suffices to show that $Ay_1, \dots, Ay_s$ is a basis of $\operatorname{range}(A)$. Spanning: any $x = \sum_i \alpha_i x_i + \sum_j \beta_j y_j$ has $Ax = \sum_j \beta_j Ay_j$, because $Ax_i = 0$. Independence: if $\sum_j \beta_j Ay_j = 0$ then $\sum_j \beta_j y_j \in \operatorname{null}(A)$, so it equals some $\sum_i \alpha_i x_i$; this is a linear relation among basis vectors of $\mathbb{R}^n$, so every $\beta_j = 0$. Hence $\operatorname{rank}(A) = s = n - t$. $\blacksquare$

Corollary used later. $\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k$ gives $\dim \operatorname{null}(B) = n - \operatorname{rank}(B) \ge n - k$.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §2.7.3, Theorem 2.24 (rank–nullity, stated for a linear map $V \to W$: $\dim \ker + \dim \operatorname{Im} = \dim V$; here $V = \mathbb{R}^n$ and the map is $x \mapsto Ax$). Proof: standard basis-extension argument, retained from the previous version of this note.

11. What Makes a Norm?

Message. A norm is a size function that is zero only at zero, scales with $|\alpha|$, and obeys the triangle inequality.

Definition: a norm on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a function $\|\cdot\| : \mathbb{R}^n \to [0,\infty)$ such that for all $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$: $\|x\| = 0$ if and only if $x = 0$; $\|\alpha x\| = |\alpha|\,\|x\|$ (the absolute value means flipping the sign does not change the size); and $\|x + y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|$. The sketch shows the triangle inequality: a solid blue arrow $x$ followed by a solid blue arrow $y$ (the detour), and a dashed amber arrow $x + y$ from the start of $x$ to the tip of $y$ (the direct route). The direct route is never longer than the detour. These three rules are all we need in this lecture; we use norms to measure residuals, not to study them for their own sake.

Details

Norm versus squared norm. The squared Euclidean norm $\|x\|_2^2$ is not a norm. It fails homogeneity, since $\|2x\|_2^2 = 4\|x\|_2^2$ rather than $2\|x\|_2^2$, and it fails the triangle inequality: for $x = y = e_1$, $\|x + y\|_2^2 = 4 \gt 2 = \|x\|_2^2 + \|y\|_2^2$. Squared Euclidean and Frobenius norms are still useful as objectives: they are smooth, and squaring a nonnegative quantity does not move its minimizers, which is why the Frobenius “energy” is often written squared later.

Nonnegativity follows from the other rules: $0 = \|x + (-x)\| \le \|x\| + \|-x\| = 2\|x\|$. A consequence used for continuity arguments is the reverse triangle inequality $\big|\|x\| - \|y\|\big| \le \|x - y\|$.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §3.1, Definition 3.1 (absolutely homogeneous, triangle inequality, positive definite). Squared-norm counterexample: our calculation.

12. Three Familiar Vector Norms

Message. The $\ell_1$, $\ell_2$ and $\ell_\infty$ norms measure different aspects of the same coordinate vector.

For $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ the slide shows three side-by-side cards. $\ell_1$, the absolute sum $\|x\|_1 = \sum_i |x_i|$: the distance walked along the coordinate axes. $\ell_2$, the Euclidean norm $\|x\|_2 = (\sum_i x_i^2)^{1/2}$: the straight-line length. $\ell_\infty$, the maximum $\|x\|_\infty = \max_i |x_i|$: the largest single coordinate. $\ell_2$ is Euclidean (straight-line) length; all three are norms. Do not confuse it with the sum of absolute coordinates: $x = (1,1)$ has $\|x\|_1 = 2$ but $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{2}$.

Details

These are three members of the $\ell_p$ family $\|x\|_p = (\sum_i |x_i|^p)^{1/p}$ for $p \ge 1$, with $\ell_\infty$ the limit as $p \to \infty$. For $p \lt 1$ the formula is not a norm: with $p = \tfrac12$, $x = (1,0)$, $y = (0,1)$ we get $\|x\|_{1/2} = \|y\|_{1/2} = 1$ but $\|x+y\|_{1/2} = (1+1)^2 = 4 \gt 2$. The proof that $\ell_p$ is a norm for $p \ge 1$ (Young, Hölder, Minkowski) is in the backup note.

Sources

$\ell_1$ (Manhattan) and $\ell_2$ (Euclidean): Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §3.1, Examples 3.1 and 3.2. $\ell_p$: Wilkinson, MATH3030 notes, §2.3.1, Definition 2.9. $\ell_\infty = \max_i |x_i|$: Boyd and Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization (CUP 2004), Appendix A.1.3, p. 635, https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf. The $(1,1)$ and $p = \tfrac12$ numbers: our calculation.

13. One Vector, Three Answers: 7, 5, 4

Message. For $x = (3,4)$ the three norms report three different sizes, 7, 5 and 4, so the choice of norm is part of the question.

Arithmetic: $\|x\|_1 = |3| + |4| = 7$; $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$; $\|x\|_\infty = \max(3, 4) = 4$. The figure is drawn to scale on a grid with the origin at bottom left and the point $x = (3,4)$ marked. The amber dashed path walks 3 to the right and then 4 up: its total length, $3 + 4 = 7$, is $\ell_1$. The solid blue diagonal from the origin straight to $x$ has length 5: that is $\ell_2$. A red bracket beside the vertical leg marks the longer coordinate, 4: that is $\ell_\infty$. For every vector, $\|x\|_\infty \le \|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_1$, here $4 \le 5 \le 7$.

Details

Why the ordering always holds. $\|x\|_\infty^2 = \max_i x_i^2 \le \sum_i x_i^2 = \|x\|_2^2$. And $\|x\|_1^2 = \sum_i x_i^2 + \sum_{i \ne j} |x_i||x_j| \ge \|x\|_2^2$. Taking square roots of nonnegative quantities gives $\|x\|_\infty \le \|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_1$.

Interpretation: $\ell_1$ adds up every coordinate, $\ell_\infty$ looks only at the worst one, and $\ell_2$ sits between them. The same contrast between “worst case” and “total” reappears for matrices as spectral norm versus Frobenius norm.

Our worked example (evidence E09: 7, 5, 4). Ordering proof: our derivation.

14. Unit Balls Reveal the Metric

Message. The unit ball $\{x : \|x\| \le 1\}$ is the set of vectors a norm calls at most 1, and its shape shows how the norm measures.

The figure has three panels in the plane, all at the same scale with axes crossing at the centre. Left: the $\ell_1$ ball is a diamond with corners on the axes at $(\pm1, 0)$ and $(0, \pm1)$. Middle: the $\ell_2$ ball is a round disc of radius 1. Right: the $\ell_\infty$ ball is a square with corners at $(\pm1, \pm1)$. Each panel marks one boundary point with an amber dot: $(1,0)$ on the diamond ($|1| + |0| = 1$), $(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$ on the circle ($\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ under the square root), and the corner $(1,1)$ on the square ($\max(1,1) = 1$). Each marked point has norm exactly 1 in its own panel's norm. The shapes are nested: diamond inside circle inside square. The panels are labelled by shape name as well as by norm, so the identification does not rely on colour.

Details

Nesting. From $\|x\|_\infty \le \|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_1$: if $\|x\|_1 \le 1$ then $\|x\|_2 \le 1$, and if $\|x\|_2 \le 1$ then $\|x\|_\infty \le 1$. So the $\ell_1$ ball lies inside the $\ell_2$ ball, which lies inside the $\ell_\infty$ ball. The point $(1,1)$ is in the square but not in the circle ($\|(1,1)\|_2 = \sqrt2 \gt 1$).

15. Orthogonal Maps Preserve Geometry

Message. A square matrix $Q$ with $Q^\top Q = I$ turns vectors without changing their Euclidean length.

Definition: $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is orthogonal if $Q^\top Q = I = QQ^\top$; equivalently its columns are orthonormal, and $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$. The figure shows a dashed grey unit circle. A blue arrow is $e_1$; an amber arrow is $Qe_1$ for the $45^\circ$ rotation $Q = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & -1\\ 1 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, so $Qe_1 = (\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$; a small arc marks the $45^\circ$ angle between them. Both tips sit on the unit circle: the direction changed, the Euclidean length stayed 1. Squareness matters. The tall matrix $W = [e_1\; e_2] \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}$ has orthonormal columns, so $W^\top W = I_2$, but $WW^\top = \operatorname{diag}(1,1,0) \ne I_3$; it is not an orthogonal matrix. And the protection is for $\ell_2$ only, not every norm (next slide).

Details

For square $Q$, $Q^\top Q = I$ already implies $QQ^\top = I$ (a square matrix with a left inverse is invertible, and the inverse is that left inverse). Further equivalent characterizations, orthonormal rows, $\det Q = \pm1$, and Gram–Schmidt are in the backup note. Check of the example: the columns $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1,1)$ each have length 1 and dot product $\tfrac12(-1+1) = 0$.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §3.4, Definition 3.8 (orthogonal matrix: square, $AA^\top = I = A^\top A$). Rotation and $W$ examples: our illustration.

16. Why Length Is Preserved

Message. One line of algebra using $Q^\top Q = I$ shows that a square orthogonal $Q$ preserves the Euclidean norm of every vector.

Claim: for square $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ with $Q^\top Q = I$ and every $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $\|Qx\|_2 = \|x\|_2$. Proof: $\|Qx\|_2^2 = (Qx)^\top(Qx) = x^\top Q^\top Q x = x^\top x = \|x\|_2^2$, where the middle step uses $Q^\top Q = I$; both norms are nonnegative, so taking square roots gives the claim. The same step with two vectors gives $(Qx)^\top(Qy) = x^\top y$, so inner products, and hence angles, are preserved too. The protection is specific to the Euclidean norm: for the $45^\circ$ rotation, $\|Qe_1\|_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} = \sqrt2$, while $\|e_1\|_1 = 1$.

Details

The proof uses only $Q^\top Q = I$, not squareness, so it also shows that a tall matrix with orthonormal columns (like $W$ on the previous slide) preserves Euclidean length. Squareness is needed for the converse direction and for $QQ^\top = I$. Conversely, a square $Q$ with $\|Qx\|_2 = \|x\|_2$ for all $x$ is orthogonal, by the polarization identity $x^\top y = \tfrac12(\|x+y\|_2^2 - \|x\|_2^2 - \|y\|_2^2)$; details in the backup note.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §3.4, Definition 3.8 and eqs. (3.31) (length preserved) and (3.32) (angles preserved). $\ell_1$ counterexample: our calculation.

17. Spectral Norm: Worst Stretch

Message. The spectral norm of a matrix is the largest factor by which it stretches any unit-length input.

Definition: for $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $\|A\|_2 = \max_{x \in \mathbb{R}^n,\ \|x\|_2 = 1} \|Ax\|_2$. It is one number for the whole matrix. The figure uses the running example $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 0\\ 4 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. On the left is the input unit circle (drawn three times larger than the output scale so it is visible), with one input $x^\star$ marked by a blue radius. An arrow labelled $A$ leads to the right panel, where the image of the circle under $A$ is a tall, tilted ellipse drawn to scale. The red radius from the centre to the ellipse's farthest point is $Ax^\star$, the longest image: its length is $\|A\|_2$. The picture is only intuition for “worst direction”; how to compute that length comes later in the lecture. Keep three things apart: $\|A\|_2$ is a matrix norm; $\|x\|_2$ is the length of a vector; and the $\ell_2$ norm of all entries of $A$ is a different matrix norm, the Frobenius norm (next slide).

Details

Figure geometry. The ellipse is the exact image $\{A(\cos t, \sin t)\} = \{(3\cos t,\ 4\cos t + 5\sin t)\}$. The marked input is $x^\star = (1,1)/\sqrt2$, and $Ax^\star = (3, 9)/\sqrt2$; a numerical scan of $t$ confirms this is where $\|A(\cos t, \sin t)\|_2$ is largest. The numerical value is deliberately not stated on the slide; it is derived with the singular values later.

The maximum is attained because $x \mapsto \|Ax\|_2$ is continuous and the unit sphere is compact. Equivalently $\|A\|_2 = \max_{x \ne 0} \|Ax\|_2 / \|x\|_2$, by homogeneity. Other induced norms ($p = 1, \infty$) and submultiplicativity $\|AB\|_2 \le \|A\|_2\|B\|_2$ are in the backup note.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §4.6, Definition 4.23 (spectral norm, eq. (4.93)); Wilkinson, MATH3030 notes, §3.5.1, Definition 3.2 (matrix $p$-norms, here $p = 2$). Figure: our running example (evidence E08), computed exactly.

18. Frobenius Norm: Total Energy

Message. The squared Frobenius norm adds up every squared entry, so $\|A - B\|_F^2$ is the total squared error over all entries.

Definition: $\|A\|_F = (\sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}^2)^{1/2}$, and for a residual $\|A - B\|_F^2 = \sum_{i,j} (a_{ij} - b_{ij})^2$. The figure is a $2\times 2$ grid of tiles, one per entry of the residual $A - B$ from the opening example, each showing its square: the top row $1.5^2 = 2.25$ and $(-1.5)^2 = 2.25$ in dark red solid tiles, the bottom row $(-0.5)^2 = 0.25$ and $0.5^2 = 0.25$ in pale red dashed tiles; darker means a larger contribution. The sum is $5$, so $\|A - B\|_F = \sqrt5$. Qualifiers: every entry is observed and counts equally (no weights), and the Frobenius norm is not the spectral norm in general. For the identity $I_2$, $\|I_2\|_2 = 1$ (it stretches nothing) but $\|I_2\|_F = \sqrt2$.

Details

Square root versus square. The norm is the square root, which makes it a norm (it is the Euclidean norm of the matrix read as a vector of $mn$ numbers). The squared version is the “energy”: it is additive over entries, which is why least-squares objectives use it.

Trace form (optional). $\|A\|_F^2 = \operatorname{tr}(A^\top A)$, since the diagonal entries of $A^\top A$ are the squared column lengths.

Arithmetic. $2.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 5$; $\sqrt5 \approx 2.236$.

20. Recall: Symmetric Eigendecomposition

Message. The SVD is built from one imported fact: a symmetric matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis, and for $A^\top A$ every eigenvalue is nonnegative.

The slide opens with the recall line: every symmetric $S \in \mathbb R^{n\times n}$ factors as $S = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $\Lambda$ real diagonal. It then applies this to $A^\top A$, which is symmetric and $n \times n$, so it acts on input vectors $x \in \mathbb R^n$. The display gives $A^\top A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ and $\lambda_i = q_i^\top A^\top A q_i = \|Aq_i\|_2^2 \ge 0$. The highlighted line states the payoff: $A^\top A$ supplies orthonormal input directions $q_1, \dots, q_n$ in $\mathbb R^n$.

Details

Why the eigenvalues are nonnegative. For a unit eigenvector $q_i$, $\lambda_i = q_i^\top (\lambda_i q_i) = q_i^\top A^\top A q_i = (Aq_i)^\top (Aq_i) = \|Aq_i\|_2^2 \ge 0$. More generally $x^\top A^\top A x = \|Ax\|_2^2 \ge 0$ for every $x$, so $A^\top A$ is positive semidefinite.

What is imported. The spectral theorem itself is not proved in this lecture; it is the one black box. Everything else in Part 3 is derived from it.

Why input space. $A^\top A$ is $n \times n$, the same size as the input dimension of $A$. Its eigenvectors will become the right singular vectors $v_i$ on the next slides.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, Mathematics for Machine Learning (Cambridge University Press, 2020), https://mml-book.github.io/, Section 4.2, Theorem 4.14 ($A^\top A$ symmetric positive semidefinite) and Theorem 4.15 (spectral theorem).

21. Every Matrix Has an SVD

Message. Every real matrix, of any shape and rank, factors as an orthogonal matrix times a nonnegative diagonal stretch times an orthogonal matrix.

The theorem card states the SVD theorem for every $A \in \mathbb R^{m\times n}$ of rank $r$, with no assumption of squareness, symmetry or invertibility. The display reads $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ with $U \in \mathbb R^{m\times m}$ and $V \in \mathbb R^{n\times n}$ orthogonal. The last card line says $\Sigma \in \mathbb R^{m\times n}$ has $\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i$, zeros off the diagonal, $\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r \gt 0$, and the remaining diagonal slots equal to $0$. A muted line under the card names the construction: eigenvectors of $A^\top A$ give $V$, then $u_i = Av_i/\sigma_i$ for $\sigma_i \gt 0$.

Details

Construction sketch. Take the eigendecomposition $A^\top A = V\Lambda V^\top$ with eigenvalues in decreasing order and set $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$. Exactly $r$ of them are positive because $\operatorname{rank}(A^\top A) = \operatorname{rank}(A) = r$. For $i \le r$ put $u_i = Av_i/\sigma_i$; these are orthonormal because $u_i^\top u_j = v_i^\top A^\top A v_j/(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = \lambda_j v_i^\top v_j/(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = \delta_{ij}$. Extend $u_1, \dots, u_r$ to an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb R^m$. Then $AV = U\Sigma$ column by column (for $i \gt r$, $\|Av_i\|_2^2 = \lambda_i = 0$), so $A = U\Sigma V^\top$.

Uniqueness. The singular values are unique. The vectors are unique up to sign when the $\sigma_i$ are distinct and positive; repeated values allow any orthonormal basis of the shared subspace. The full existence and uniqueness proof is kept in the archived full note.

Naming. The deck cites its results by name, not number: the SVD theorem here, then the spectral-norm and Frobenius-norm identities read off $\Sigma$.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, Mathematics for Machine Learning (Cambridge University Press, 2020), https://mml-book.github.io/, Section 4.5, Theorem 4.22 (SVD theorem); Section 4.5.2, eq. (4.78), $u_i = Av_i/\sigma_i$.

Message. Multiplying by $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ is three steps: an orthogonal change of input coordinates, a stretch by the singular values, and an orthogonal change of output coordinates.

26. A Sum of Rank-One Layers

Message. Expanding $U\Sigma V^\top$ writes $A$ as a sum of $r$ rank-one layers $\sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$, ordered by weight.

The left column shows $A = U\Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$, with braces marking $u_i$ as $m \times 1$ and $v_i^\top$ as $1 \times n$. Three lines follow: each $u_i v_i^\top$ is $m \times n$ with every column a multiple of $u_i$; when $\sigma_i \gt 0$ the term has rank one; and all $r$ terms together give $A$ exactly. The right column shows three stacked translucent sheets; the front sheet is labelled $\sigma_1 u_1 v_1^\top$ with largest weight, the caption reads $A$ = layer $1 + \dots +$ layer $r$, and the back layers carry smaller $\sigma_i$.

Details

Derivation. The columns of $U\Sigma$ are $\sigma_i u_i$ for $i \le r$ and zero otherwise. Multiplying by $V^\top$, whose rows are $v_i^\top$, gives $U\Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^r (\sigma_i u_i) v_i^\top$ by the column-times-row form of matrix multiplication.

Rank one. Column $j$ of $u_i v_i^\top$ is $(v_i)_j u_i$, a multiple of $u_i$, and $v_i \ne 0$, so the column space is exactly the span of $u_i$.

Forward pointer. The partial sum of the first $k$ layers is written $A_k = \sum_{i \le k}\sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$. Whether $A_k$ is the best rank-$k$ approximation is a separate theorem, proved later in the lecture.

27. Compact SVD Keeps Active Directions

Message. Dropping the zero singular values leaves the compact SVD $A = U_r\Sigma_r V_r^\top$, which is still exact; its factors $U_r$ and $V_r$ have orthonormal columns but need not be square orthogonal matrices.

The figure shows $A$ ($m \times n$) equal to a thin tall block $U_r$ ($m \times r$), a small square $\Sigma_r$ ($r \times r$) with a full diagonal, and a short wide block $V_r^\top$ ($r \times n$); a gray note says only the $r$ nonzero $\sigma_i$ are kept. The display reads $A = U_r\Sigma_r V_r^\top$, $\Sigma_r = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r)$, $U_r^\top U_r = V_r^\top V_r = I_r$. The muted qualifier says that when $r \lt m$, $U_r U_r^\top \ne I_m$: the thin factors have orthonormal columns but are not square orthogonal matrices.

Details

Why it is exact. The rank-one expansion uses only $i \le r$, and $U_r\Sigma_r V_r^\top$ is exactly that sum. The discarded columns of $U$ and $V$ only ever multiply zeros of $\Sigma$.

What $U_r U_r^\top$ is. It is the orthogonal projector onto the column space of $A$; it equals $I_m$ only when $r = m$. Likewise $V_r V_r^\top$ projects onto the row space.

Notation. This revision writes the compact factors with subscript $r$ ($U_r, \Sigma_r, V_r$), matching the rank.

35. Eigenvalues Are Not Singular Values

Message. For a non-symmetric matrix the eigenvalues and the singular values are different lists; the eigenvalues of $A^\top A$ are the squared singular values.

Message. After the orthogonal change of coordinates $y = V^\top x$, every unit input is stretched by at most $\sigma_1$.

39. Frobenius Energy Sums Squared Stretches

Message. The total energy $\|A\|_F^2$, the sum of all squared entries, equals the sum of all squared singular values.

40. Orthogonal Coordinates Preserve Total Energy

Message. Orthogonal matrices on either side do not change the Frobenius norm, so $\|A\|_F = \|U^\top A V\|_F = \|\Sigma\|_F$.

The first line fixes orthogonal $Q$ and $W$ and a matrix $B$ with columns $b_j$. The two-line display proves invariance on each side. Columns: $\|QB\|_F^2 = \sum_j\|Qb_j\|_2^2 = \sum_j\|b_j\|_2^2 = \|B\|_F^2$, using vector invariance column by column. Rows, via the transpose: $\|BW\|_F = \|W^\top B^\top\|_F = \|B^\top\|_F = \|B\|_F$. The final display applies both with $Q = U^\top$ and $W = V$: $\|A\|_F^2 = \|U^\top A V\|_F^2 = \|\Sigma\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r\sigma_i^2$, end of proof.

Details

Transposition. $\|B^\top\|_F = \|B\|_F$ because both sum the same squared entries.

Alternative trace proof. $\|A\|_F^2 = \operatorname{tr}(A^\top A) = \operatorname{tr}(V\Sigma^\top\Sigma V^\top) = \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^\top\Sigma V^\top V) = \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^\top\Sigma) = \sum_i\sigma_i^2$, using the cyclic property of the trace.

42. Candidate: Keep the $k$ Largest Layers

Message. Sort the singular values and keep the $k$ largest rank-one layers; this truncation $A_k$ is the natural candidate, not yet a proven optimum.

The SVD writes $A = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$ as a sum of $r$ rank-one layers, each scaled by a singular value. Under a rank budget $k$ the obvious move is to keep the $k$ layers with the largest singular values and drop the rest. That gives $A_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$.

The slide's display writes $A$ as one long sum. A brace under the first $k$ terms labels them as $A_k$, the kept part. A second brace under the remaining terms, printed in grey, labels them as discarded.

For $i \leq k$, $A_k v_i = \sigma_i u_i$, so the range of $A_k$ contains the $k$ independent vectors $\sigma_1 u_1, \dots, \sigma_k u_k$; it also lies in their span, because every column of $A_k$ is a combination of $u_1, \dots, u_k$. So $A_k$ has rank exactly $k$ and is inside the budget $\operatorname{rank} \leq k$. Whether some other rank-$k$ matrix does better is the question the rest of the part answers.

Details

The singular values are sorted, $\sigma_1 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r \gt 0$. When there are ties, $A_k$ depends on which singular vectors are chosen, but its error does not.

Edge cases. For $k = 0$ the empty sum gives $A_0 = 0$, with error $\|A\|$ in either norm. For $k \geq r$ we have $A_k = A$ and the error is zero, so the interesting range is $0 \leq k \lt r$, the range the theorems use.

In matrix form $A_k = U \Sigma_k V^\top$, where $\Sigma_k$ keeps the first $k$ diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ and zeroes the rest.

45. Eckart–Young–Mirsky: Spectral Error

Message. In the spectral norm, no matrix of rank at most $k$ gets closer to $A$ than $\sigma_{k+1}$, and $A_k$ achieves exactly $\sigma_{k+1}$.

The theorem box states the setting in full: a real $m \times n$ matrix of rank $r$, sorted positive singular values, and a budget $0 \leq k \lt r$. The minimum of $\|A - B\|_2$ over every $m \times n$ matrix $B$ of rank at most $k$ equals $\sigma_{k+1}$, and $B = A_k$ attains it.

The quantifier is the point. The minimum runs over all rank-$\leq k$ matrices, not only over truncations of the SVD. The Frobenius version is a separate theorem with a separate proof, stated later.

Details

Scope. The theorem concerns a fully observed matrix and the unweighted spectral error. It says nothing about uniqueness; in the spectral norm the minimizer is in general not unique (see the next article).

7.3 The spectral-norm case, proved (compact form).

Theorem (Eckart–Young–Mirsky, spectral norm). For every $B$ with $\operatorname{rank}(B) \leq k$, $\|A - B\|_2 \geq \sigma_{k+1} = \|A - A_k\|_2$.

Proof. By rank–nullity, $\dim\operatorname{null}(B) \geq n - k$. Let $W = \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_{k+1}\}$, of dimension $k+1$. Then $\dim(\operatorname{null}(B) \cap W) \geq (n-k) + (k+1) - n = 1$, so choose a unit vector $x \in \operatorname{null}(B) \cap W$. Since $Bx = 0$,

\[\begin{aligned} \|A - B\|_2^2 &\geq \|(A-B)x\|_2^2 = \|Ax\|_2^2 \\ &= \Big\|\sum_{i \leq k+1} \sigma_i (v_i^\top x)\, u_i\Big\|_2^2 \\ &= \sum_{i \leq k+1} \sigma_i^2 (v_i^\top x)^2 \geq \sigma_{k+1}^2,\end{aligned}\]

using $x \in W$ (so $v_i^\top x = 0$ for $i \gt k+1$), orthonormality of the $u_i$, and $\sum_{i \leq k+1} (v_i^\top x)^2 = \|x\|_2^2 = 1$ with $\sigma_i \geq \sigma_{k+1}$ throughout. Since $A_k$ attains $\sigma_{k+1}$, it is optimal. $\blacksquare$

The slides that follow unpack this proof step by step.

History. The result is usually attributed to Eckart and Young [1], who proved the Frobenius case in 1936; Mirsky [2] gave the general unitarily invariant statement in 1960, and Erhard Schmidt [3] had proved the analogous theorem for integral operators already in 1907. See [9] for the history of who proved what.

A common phrasing calls $A_k$ the minimizer. For the spectral norm that is false. Counterexample: $A = \operatorname{diag}(2,1)$, $k = 1$, so $\sigma_2 = 1$ and the optimal value is $1$. 48. Large Subspaces Must Intersect

Message. Two subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^n$ whose dimensions add up to more than $n$ must share a nonzero vector.

The fact box states the bound $\dim(S_1 \cap S_2) \geq \dim S_1 + \dim S_2 - n$ for subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^n$. The reason in one line: the sum $S_1 + S_2$ sits inside $\mathbb{R}^n$, so its dimension is at most $n$, and the dimension formula below converts that into the bound.

The application on the slide uses the two dimensions the proof will need, $n - k$ and $k + 1$. They add to $n + 1$, so the intersection has dimension at least one and contains a nonzero vector.

Details

2.3 The dimension formula, proved.

Theorem 2.3. For subspaces $W_1, W_2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, $\dim(W_1 + W_2) + \dim(W_1 \cap W_2) = \dim W_1 + \dim W_2$, where $W_1 + W_2 = \{w_1 + w_2 : w_i \in W_i\}$.

Proof. Let $z_1, \dots, z_t$ be a basis of $W_1 \cap W_2$. Extend it to a basis $z_1, \dots, z_t, x_1, \dots, x_p$ of $W_1$ and to a basis $z_1, \dots, z_t, y_1, \dots, y_q$ of $W_2$, so $\dim W_1 = t + p$, $\dim W_2 = t + q$. The combined list spans $W_1 + W_2$; we claim it is independent. Suppose $\sum \gamma_i z_i + \sum \alpha_j x_j + \sum \beta_\ell y_\ell = 0$. Then $v := \sum_\ell \beta_\ell y_\ell = -\sum \gamma_i z_i - \sum \alpha_j x_j$ lies in $W_1$ and in $W_2$, hence in $W_1 \cap W_2$, so $v = \sum_i \delta_i z_i$ for some $\delta$. But $\sum_\ell \beta_\ell y_\ell - \sum_i \delta_i z_i = 0$ with the $z$'s and $y$'s independent forces all $\beta_\ell = 0$ (and all $\delta_i = 0$). The relation collapses to $\sum \gamma_i z_i + \sum \alpha_j x_j = 0$, and independence in $W_1$ kills the rest. Hence $\dim(W_1 + W_2) = t + p + q$, and $(t+p+q) + t = (t+p) + (t+q)$. $\blacksquare$

Corollary 2.4. $\dim(W_1 \cap W_2) \geq \dim W_1 + \dim W_2 - n$. In particular, if $\dim W_1 + \dim W_2 \gt n$ then $W_1 \cap W_2$ contains a nonzero vector. Proof. $W_1 + W_2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ gives $\dim(W_1 + W_2) \leq n$; substitute into Theorem 2.3. $\blacksquare$

This count is the engine of the spectral proof: it converts "$B$ has low rank" into "$B$ is blind at a specific place where $A$ is strong".

Standard linear algebra; proof given in full above. See also Horn and Johnson [6].

51. Top $k{+}1$ Directions Stretch by $\geq \sigma_{k+1}$

Message. Every unit vector in the span of the top $k+1$ right singular vectors is stretched by $A$ by at least $\sigma_{k+1}$.

Write a unit $w$ in that span as $w = \sum_{i \leq k+1} c_i v_i$. Because the $v_i$ are orthonormal, $\sum c_i^2 = \|w\|_2^2 = 1$. Applying $A$ sends each $v_i$ to $\sigma_i u_i$, and because the $u_i$ are orthonormal the squared length is $\sum \sigma_i^2 c_i^2$.

Each $\sigma_i$ with $i \leq k+1$ is at least $\sigma_{k+1}$ because the values are sorted, so the sum is at least $\sigma_{k+1}^2 \sum c_i^2 = \sigma_{k+1}^2$. The slide shows this as one two-row chain, each step labelled by the fact it uses.

Details

The two orthonormality facts play different roles: orthonormal $v_i$ give the coefficient identity $\sum c_i^2 = 1$; orthonormal $u_i$ give the Pythagorean identity for $\|Aw\|_2^2$. Sortedness gives the final inequality. The bound is tight at $w = v_{k+1}$.

This is one half of the min–max characterization of singular values (Courant–Fischer), which the Frobenius proof uses in full; it is stated and proved in the article for the Frobenius theorem.

Sources

Standard; Horn and Johnson [6], ch. 3 (variational characterization of singular values).

Message. Since $w$ lies in $W$, $\|Aw\|_2 \geq \sigma_{k+1}$, and therefore $\|A - B\|_2 \geq \sigma_{k+1}$ for every competitor $B$.

58. Step 5: Truncation Meets the Bound

Message. The residual $A - A_k$ is itself an SVD with largest singular value $\sigma_{k+1}$, so $A_k$ achieves the lower bound and the minimum is exactly $\sigma_{k+1}$.

Subtracting the first $k$ layers leaves $A - A_k = \sum_{i = k+1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$. This sum is already a valid SVD: the $u_i$ are orthonormal, the $v_i$ are orthonormal, and the $\sigma_i$ are positive and sorted. Its largest singular value is $\sigma_{k+1}$.

The spectral-norm identity $\|M\|_2 = \sigma_1(M)$ then gives $\|A - A_k\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}$. A lower bound for every competitor plus one competitor on the bound means the minimum equals $\sigma_{k+1}$, which completes the proof.

60. Eckart–Young–Mirsky: Frobenius Error

Message. In the Frobenius norm the best rank-$\leq k$ squared error is $\sum_{i \gt k} \sigma_i^2$, again attained by $A_k$; this is a separate theorem, stated here and proved only in this note.

The theorem box repeats the same setup and states $\min_{\operatorname{rank}(B) \leq k} \|A - B\|_F^2 = \sum_{i = k+1}^r \sigma_i^2$, attained by $B = A_k$.

The spectral proof does not give this. It bounds only the largest singular value of $A - B$, whereas the Frobenius norm sums all of them. The proof below needs Weyl's inequality for singular values, which rests on the min–max characterization. The lecture uses the statement only.

Details

7.2 Courant–Fischer for singular values.

Lemma 7.1 (min–max / max–min). For $1 \leq i \leq \min(m,n)$,

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_i(A) &= \max_{\substack{S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \\ \dim S = i}} \ \min_{\substack{x \in S \\ \|x\|_2 = 1}} \|Ax\|_2 \\ &= \min_{\substack{T \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \\ \dim T = n-i+1}} \ \max_{\substack{x \in T \\ \|x\|_2 = 1}} \|Ax\|_2 .\end{aligned}\]

Proof (first equality; the second is analogous). (At least $\sigma_i$ for the max.) Take $S_0 = \operatorname{span}\{v_1, \dots, v_i\}$. For unit $x \in S_0$, $x = \sum_{j \leq i} c_j v_j$ with $\sum c_j^2 = 1$, and $\|Ax\|_2^2 = \sum_{j \leq i} \sigma_j^2 c_j^2 \geq \sigma_i^2$. So the inner min over $S_0$ is at least $\sigma_i$ (indeed equal, at $x = v_i$). (At most $\sigma_i$ for every $S$.) Let $\dim S = i$ and $W' = \operatorname{span}\{v_i, v_{i+1}, \dots, v_n\}$, completing the $v$'s to an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $\sigma_j := 0$ beyond the rank; $\dim W' = n - i + 1$. By the intersection count, $\dim(S \cap W') \geq 1$, so there is a unit $x \in S \cap W'$. Writing $x = \sum_{j \geq i} c_j v_j$, $\|Ax\|_2^2 = \sum_{j \geq i} \sigma_j^2 c_j^2 \leq \sigma_i^2$. Hence the inner min over $S$ is at most $\sigma_i$. $\blacksquare$

7.4 The Frobenius case, proved.

Lemma 7.2 (Weyl, additive). For $X, Y \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ and $i, j \geq 1$ with $i + j - 1 \leq \min(m,n)$: $\sigma_{i+j-1}(X + Y) \leq \sigma_i(X) + \sigma_j(Y)$.

Proof. By the min–max form of Lemma 7.1 there are subspaces $T_X$ of dimension $n - i + 1$ and $T_Y$ of dimension $n - j + 1$ with $\max_{x \in T_X, \|x\| = 1} \|Xx\|_2 = \sigma_i(X)$ and likewise for $Y$. Put $T = T_X \cap T_Y$, so $\dim T \geq (n-i+1) + (n-j+1) - n = n - (i+j-1) + 1$. Applying the min–max characterization at index $i + j - 1$ (whose minimum runs over subspaces of exactly that dimension, and any larger subspace contains one) gives $\sigma_{i+j-1}(X+Y) \leq \max_{x \in T, \|x\| = 1} \|(X+Y)x\|_2 \leq \max_{x \in T, \|x\| = 1} (\|Xx\|_2 + \|Yx\|_2) \leq \sigma_i(X) + \sigma_j(Y)$. $\blacksquare$

Lemma 7.3. If $\operatorname{rank}(B) \leq k$ then $\sigma_{i+k}(A) \leq \sigma_i(A - B)$ for all $i \geq 1$ (with $\sigma_j := 0$ beyond $\min(m,n)$). Proof. Apply Lemma 7.2 with $X = A - B$, $Y = B$, indices $i$ and $k+1$: $\sigma_{i+k}(A) \leq \sigma_i(A - B) + \sigma_{k+1}(B)$, and $\operatorname{rank}(B) \leq k$ means $\sigma_{k+1}(B) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem (Eckart–Young–Mirsky, Frobenius norm). For every $B$ with $\operatorname{rank}(B) \leq k$, $\|A - B\|_F^2 \geq \sum_{i \gt k} \sigma_i(A)^2 = \|A - A_k\|_F^2$. Proof. By the Frobenius identity $\|M\|_F^2 = \sum_i \sigma_i(M)^2$ and Lemma 7.3, $\|A - B\|_F^2 = \sum_{i \geq 1} \sigma_i(A - B)^2 \geq \sum_{i \geq 1} \sigma_{i+k}(A)^2 = \sum_{j \gt k} \sigma_j(A)^2$, which is $\|A - A_k\|_F^2$ by the next article. $\blacksquare$

The same argument extends to the nuclear norm and, by Mirsky [2], to every unitarily invariant norm; those extensions are kept in the extended-edition note (../opt03-backup/opt01-svd-lowrank-full-note.html).

63. The Best Rank-One Error Is $\sqrt5$

Message. For the worked matrix with $k = 1$, the theorems certify that no matrix of rank at most one is closer than $\sqrt5$, in either norm, and $A_1$ attains it.

65. Worst Error and Total Error Can Differ

Message. With a residual of rank two, the spectral and Frobenius errors separate: for $\operatorname{diag}(5,3,1)$ truncated to rank one they are $3$ and $\sqrt{10}$.

Take the diagonal matrix $D = \operatorname{diag}(5, 3, 1)$, whose singular values are $5, 3, 1$ with standard basis vectors as singular vectors. Keeping the top layer gives $D_1 = \operatorname{diag}(5, 0, 0)$, and the residual is $\operatorname{diag}(0, 3, 1)$.

The spectral error is the largest dropped singular value, $3$, the worst-case stretch. The Frobenius error counts all dropped values, $\sqrt{3^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$. A rank-two residual has two nonzero singular values, so the two norms measure different things.

Details

In the $2 \times 2$ worked example the residual had one nonzero singular value, so the two errors matched. In general $\|M\|_2 \leq \|M\|_F \leq \sqrt{\operatorname{rank}(M)}\, \|M\|_2$, with equality on the left exactly when $M$ has rank at most one.

Both numbers are optimal for their own norm by the two theorems with $k = 1$.

66. What the Guarantee Covers

Message. The theorems answer one precise question: given every entry of $A$ and a rank budget $k$, which rank-$\leq k$ matrix is closest in unweighted spectral or Frobenius error.

A three-box flow reads left to right: given every entry of $A$, choose a rank budget $k$, get $A_k$ with optimal error. The optimal error is $\sigma_{k+1}$ in the spectral norm and $\sum_{i \gt k} \sigma_i^2$ in squared Frobenius norm, and every entry counts equally.

A box below lists what the theorem does not cover: matrices with missing entries, the downstream accuracy of a model built on $A_k$, and statistically optimal denoising of a noisy matrix. Each of these changes the problem, so the closed-form answer no longer applies as stated.

Details

Other limits of this lecture, for the record: the existence of the SVD rests on the spectral theorem, which was not proved here; everything was over the real numbers (the complex case is parallel, with conjugate transposes); and how the SVD is computed numerically is a separate subject (Golub and Van Loan [8], ch. 8).

Weighted errors, where some entries count more than others, and observing only some entries, are not covered: the unweighted Eckart–Young–Mirsky guarantee does not apply to general weighted or missing-entry objectives, and truncating the given matrix’s SVD is not generally guaranteed to solve them.
