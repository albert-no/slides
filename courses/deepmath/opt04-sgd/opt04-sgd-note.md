## 1 What This Lecture Actually Claims

### 1.1 The problem

Every result today concerns the **finite-sum** or empirical-risk objective introduced in opt02 [25],

$ \min_{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d} \; \mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{L}_i(\theta), \qquad \mathcal{L}_i(\theta) = \ell\big(h_\theta(x^{(i)}), y^{(i)}\big), $

and its algorithm is one line: draw an index, take one gradient, step.

$ i_k \sim \mathrm{Unif}\{1,\dots,N\}, \qquad g_k = \nabla \mathcal{L}_{i_k}(\theta_k), \qquad \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \alpha_k\, g_k. $

The motivation is a cost argument, and it is worth stating quantitatively because it is the entire reason the rest of the lecture exists. Gradient descent needs $O(1/\varepsilon)$ steps for an $\varepsilon$-accurate convex objective (opt03, Theorem 8), and each step costs $N$ gradient evaluations, so $O(N/\varepsilon)$ in total. SGD needs $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ steps, by Theorem 2 below, at one gradient each: $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ total, *independent of $N$*. SGD wins whenever $N \gg 1/\varepsilon$. With $N$ in the millions and $\varepsilon$ the modest accuracy a training run actually needs, this is not close.

**The structural point.** The $N$-independence is not an artefact of loose analysis. Theorem 2's bound involves only $G$, $\sigma$, $D$ and $K$; the number of terms $N$ appears nowhere. What replaces it is $\sigma^2$, which measures how much the individual $\nabla \mathcal{L}_i$ disagree with each other. A dataset of a million near-identical points has a tiny $\sigma^2$ and SGD is nearly as good as gradient descent at $1/N$ the price; a dataset of a million wildly heterogeneous points has a large $\sigma^2$ and pays for it in the constant, not in the rate.

### 1.2 The four theorems and what each one needs

The lecture is careful to keep the hypotheses of the four results separate, and the list below makes that explicit. Note that *none* of them assumes smoothness, meaning an $L$-Lipschitz gradient; this is the sharpest contrast with opt03, where every rate began with the descent lemma. The price is that the rates are stated for an **averaged** iterate rather than the last one, and are $O(1/\sqrt{K})$ rather than $O(1/K)$. Each entry gives the result, its hypotheses, its conclusion, and the section where it is proved.

- **Theorem 1, unbiasedness.** Hypotheses: uniform sampling, independent of the past. Conclusion: $\mathbb{E}_k[g_k] = \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$. Proved in Section 2.2.
- **Theorem 2, the convex rate.** Hypotheses: $\mathcal{L}$ convex and $G$-Lipschitz; unbiased gradients; conditional variance at most $\sigma^2$; constant $\alpha$ tuned to $K$. Conclusion: $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\bar\theta_K)-\mathcal{L}^\star] \le \dfrac{\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}\,D}{\sqrt{K+1}}$. Proved in Section 5.
- **Theorem 3, mini-batching.** Hypotheses: $b$ indices drawn independently and uniformly, with replacement. Conclusion: the batch-gradient variance equals $\sigma^2/b$. Proved in Section 6.
- **Theorem 4, the strongly convex rate.** Hypotheses: $F = f + \frac\mu2\lVert\cdot\rVert^2$ with $f$ convex and $G$-Lipschitz; schedule $\alpha_k = \frac{1}{\mu(k+1)}$; start at $x_0=0$. Conclusion: $\mathbb{E}[F(\bar x_k)-F^\star] \le \dfrac{2(G^2+\sigma^2)}{\mu}\cdot\dfrac{1+\log(k+1)}{k+1}$. Proved in Section 7.

Three things the lecture states as slogans and that this note turns into statements. First, "the noise never sleeps": at $\theta^\star$ the *average* pull vanishes but individual pulls do not, so a constant step size cannot converge, and Section 4 computes the exact floor. Second, "balance progress against noise": the $\sqrt{K}$ in Theorem 2 is born from minimizing an expression of the form $a/\alpha + b\alpha$, and Section 5.4 proves that this is the best a constant step can do. Third, "shrinking steps kill the floor": Section 7 shows that the specific schedule $\alpha_k = 1/(\mu(k+1))$ does something stronger than shrink the floor, namely it makes the telescope's head term vanish identically, which is why $\lVert x_0 - x^\star\rVert$ is absent from Theorem 4.

### 1.3 What is *not* claimed

- **Nothing about the last iterate.** Theorems 2 and 4 bound the loss at $\bar\theta_K$. The last iterate satisfies a weaker bound in general (Sections 5.8 and 7.7); the lecture flags this honestly as a theory-practice gap.
- **Nothing almost surely.** Both are in-expectation bounds. Almost-sure convergence needs the Robbins-Monro conditions and a supermartingale argument (Section 8); high-probability bounds need tail assumptions on the noise (Section 8.3).
- **Nothing nonconvex.** Convexity is used in exactly one place in each proof, the tangent-line floor, and it is essential. The nonconvex analogue bounds $\min_k \mathbb{E}\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)\rVert^2$, needs smoothness back, and is stated in Section 8.4.
- **Nothing about generalization.** Everything here is about minimizing the *empirical* risk. Why the minimizer generalizes is prob05 and prob06 territory, and why SGD's particular noise helps it generalize is open research (Section 9.6).

## 2 The Stochastic Gradient: Sampling and Unbiasedness

### 2.1 The probability space, and what $\mathbb{E}_k$ means

The lecture writes $\mathbb{E}_k[\,\cdot\,] = \mathbb{E}[\,\cdot \mid \theta_0,\dots,\theta_k]$ and moves on. That is the right picture, but the object needs a moment's care, because every subsequent proof consists of taking a *deterministic* inequality and putting $\mathbb{E}_k$ in front of it.

Fix a horizon and let $i_0, i_1, i_2, \dots$ be independent random variables, each uniform on $\{1,\dots,N\}$, defined on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$. Define the **filtration**

$ \mathcal{F}_k \;=\; \sigma(i_0, i_1, \dots, i_{k-1}), \qquad \mathcal{F}_0 = \{\emptyset, \Omega\}\ \text{(trivial)}, $

the information available *before* the $k$-th draw is made. Write $\mathbb{E}_k[\,\cdot\,] = \mathbb{E}[\,\cdot \mid \mathcal{F}_k]$.

**Lemma 2.1 (measurability).** If $\theta_0$ is deterministic and $\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \alpha_k \nabla\mathcal{L}_{i_k}(\theta_k)$, then $\theta_k$ is $\mathcal{F}_k$-measurable for every $k$.

*Proof.* Induction. $\theta_0$ is constant, hence measurable with respect to the trivial sigma-algebra $\mathcal{F}_0$. If $\theta_k$ is $\mathcal{F}_k$-measurable, then $\theta_{k+1}$ is a measurable function of the pair $(\theta_k, i_k)$, and the sigma-algebra generated by $\mathcal{F}_k$ together with $i_k$ is $\mathcal{F}_{k+1}$. **End of proof.**

Lemma 2.1 is what licenses the two moves used constantly below.

- **Pull out what is known.** $\mathbb{E}_k[\langle \theta_k - \theta^\star, g_k\rangle] = \langle \theta_k - \theta^\star, \mathbb{E}_k[g_k]\rangle$, because $\theta_k$ is $\mathcal{F}_k$-measurable. Without Lemma 2.1 this step is simply false, and it is the step that makes unbiasedness useful: the inner product is linear in $g_k$, so the noise integrates away exactly. Contrast $\mathbb{E}_k\lVert g_k\rVert^2$, which is *not* $\lVert \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)\rVert^2$: the square is not linear, and the discrepancy is precisely the variance; see Section 3.2.
- **Tower.** $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_k[X]] = \mathbb{E}[X]$, from prob07. Every proof establishes a one-step inequality under $\mathbb{E}_k$, then applies the tower property to get an inequality between total expectations, which is what can be summed over $k$.

Also note that $i_k$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_k$: the algorithm samples the index without looking at the current iterate. This is a modelling assumption and it is what the lecture means by "the draw does not look at the iterate". Curriculum learning, importance sampling with adaptive weights, and hard-example mining all violate it, and all need a separate analysis.

### 2.2 Theorem 1, in full

**Theorem 2.2 (unbiasedness; Theorem 1 of the lecture).** Let $i_k \sim \mathrm{Unif}\{1,\dots,N\}$ be independent of $\mathcal{F}_k$, and suppose each $\mathcal{L}_i$ is differentiable at $\theta_k$. Then

$ \mathbb{E}_k\big[\nabla \mathcal{L}_{i_k}(\theta_k)\big] \;=\; \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k) \qquad \text{almost surely.} $

*Proof.* Because $i_k$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_k$ and $\theta_k$ is $\mathcal{F}_k$-measurable, conditioning on $\mathcal{F}_k$ freezes $\theta_k$ at its realised value and leaves the law of $i_k$ untouched. So for a fixed point $\theta$,

$ \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla\mathcal{L}_{i_k}(\theta)\big] \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{P}(i_k = i)\, \nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\theta) \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\theta) \;=\; \nabla\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathcal{L}_i\Big)(\theta) \;=\; \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta), $

the last-but-one equality by linearity of the derivative on a *finite* sum. Substituting $\theta = \theta_k$ gives the claim. **End of proof.**

Three remarks the two-line proof in the presentation cannot make room for.

- **The finiteness matters.** Exchanging the gradient with the average is trivial for a finite sum. For a population risk $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim P}[\ell(h_\theta(x),y)]$ the same statement is an interchange of derivative and integral, and needs a dominated-convergence hypothesis, for example a local integrable Lipschitz envelope for the map $\theta \mapsto \ell(h_\theta(x),y)$. It is true in every setting of practical interest, but it is a theorem, not an identity.
- **Non-differentiable losses.** ReLU networks and hinge losses are not differentiable everywhere. The correct object is then a stochastic *subgradient*: the claim $\mathbb{E}_k[g_k] \in \partial\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$ requires $\mathbb{E}[\partial\mathcal{L}_i] = \partial\mathcal{L}$, which holds for the finite sum by Moreau-Rockafellar (opt03 Section 9.3). Everything in Section 5 goes through verbatim with subgradients, since the only property used is the tangent-line floor $\langle g, \theta-\theta^\star\rangle \ge \mathcal{L}(\theta)-\mathcal{L}^\star$ in expectation.
- **Unbiasedness is designed, not discovered.** The uniform weight $1/N$ was chosen to match the weight $1/N$ in the objective. Change one and you must change the other, which is exactly what Section 2.3 does.

### 2.3 Non-uniform sampling and importance weights

Nothing forces uniform sampling. Let $p = (p_1,\dots,p_N)$ be any distribution with $p_i > 0$ for all $i$, draw $i_k \sim p$, and use the **reweighted** gradient

$ g_k \;=\; \frac{1}{N p_{i_k}}\, \nabla \mathcal{L}_{i_k}(\theta_k). $

**Proposition 2.3 (importance sampling).** $g_k$ is unbiased for every such $p$, and its variance is minimized by taking $p_i$ proportional to $\lVert \nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\theta_k)\rVert$, at which choice

$ \mathbb{E}_k\lVert g_k\rVert^2 \;=\; \Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\theta_k)\rVert\Big)^{2} \;\le\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\theta_k)\rVert^2, $

the right-hand side being the uniform-sampling value.

*Proof.* Unbiasedness: $\mathbb{E}_k[g_k] = \sum_i p_i \cdot \frac{1}{Np_i}\nabla\mathcal{L}_i = \frac1N\sum_i \nabla\mathcal{L}_i = \nabla\mathcal{L}$, where the $p_i$ cancels, which is the whole trick. Second moment: writing $a_i = \lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}_i\rVert$,

$ \mathbb{E}_k\lVert g_k \rVert^2 \;=\; \sum_i p_i \frac{a_i^2}{N^2p_i^2} \;=\; \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_i \frac{a_i^2}{p_i}. $

By Cauchy-Schwarz, $\big(\sum_i a_i\big)^2 = \big(\sum_i \frac{a_i}{\sqrt{p_i}}\sqrt{p_i}\big)^2 \le \big(\sum_i \frac{a_i^2}{p_i}\big)\big(\sum_i p_i\big) = \sum_i \frac{a_i^2}{p_i}$, with equality if and only if $a_i/\sqrt{p_i}$ is proportional to $\sqrt{p_i}$, that is $p_i$ proportional to $a_i$. So the minimum of $\frac{1}{N^2}\sum_i a_i^2/p_i$ over distributions $p$ equals $\frac{1}{N^2}(\sum_i a_i)^2 = (\frac1N\sum_i a_i)^2$. The final inequality is Jensen, or Cauchy-Schwarz again, applied to the average of the $a_i$: the square of a mean is at most the mean of the squares. **End of proof.**

Since $\mathbb{E}_k\lVert g_k\rVert^2$ is the only place the noise enters the proof of Theorem 2, reducing it reduces the bound directly. The catch is practical rather than mathematical: the optimal $p$ depends on all $N$ gradient norms at the *current* $\theta_k$, which costs a full pass to compute, precisely what SGD was invented to avoid. Cheap surrogates, such as a fixed $p_i$ proportional to $\lVert x^{(i)}\rVert$ for linear models, or stale norms refreshed occasionally, recover part of the gain, and this is the standard theory of importance-sampling SGD [1, Section 5.4].

### 2.4 With replacement, without replacement, and epochs

The lecture is explicit that its analysis samples *with replacement*, by independent draws, while practice shuffles the data and makes a pass, an **epoch**, before reshuffling. The gap is real and was open for a surprisingly long time.

Under random reshuffling, $i_k$ is *not* independent of the past: having drawn point 7 this epoch, the conditional probability of drawing it again is zero. Consequently $\mathbb{E}_k[g_k] \ne \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$ in general and Theorem 2.2 fails at the level of an individual step. What survives is a statement per epoch: over a full sweep, every gradient is used exactly once, so the *sum* of the sampled gradients over one epoch equals $N$ times the full gradient evaluated at $N$ *different* points, which is $N \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ plus an error controlled by how far the iterate moves within the epoch, that is by $\alpha$.

This is why reshuffling helps: the within-epoch errors are of order $\alpha$ and partially cancel, giving rates like $O(1/K^2)$ per-epoch behaviour under strong convexity against $O(1/K)$ for with-replacement sampling, once $K$ exceeds a threshold. The clean modern reference is Mishchenko, Khaled and Richtarik [19], which also explains why the older analyses needed the noncommutative arithmetic-geometric-mean conjecture and how to avoid it. For this lecture the honest summary is: *with replacement is the analysable case and is never better than what practice does*.

### 2.5 The running example, computed completely

With $\mathcal{L}_i(\theta) = \tfrac12(\theta-c_i)^2$ and $c = (0,3,6)$: $\nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\theta) = \theta - c_i$ and $\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \theta - \bar c = \theta - 3$. The sampled gradient at any $\theta$ takes the three values $\theta$, $\theta-3$ and $\theta-6$ with probability $1/3$ each; their mean is $\theta - 3$ for every $\theta$, which is the worked check at $\theta \in \{0,3,6\}$.

Two features of this example are worth naming because they are *not* generic and it is easy to over-generalise from them.

- **The noise is state-independent.** Setting $n_k := g_k - \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k) = (\theta_k - c_{i_k}) - (\theta_k - 3) = 3 - c_{i_k} \in \{3,0,-3\}$, with no $\theta_k$ in it at all. This happens because all three $\mathcal{L}_i$ have the *same* second derivative, so the individual gradients differ from the average by a constant offset. Generic finite sums have $\sigma^2(\theta)$ varying with $\theta$, often growing with $\lVert\theta\rVert$; the bounded-variance assumption then becomes a genuine restriction (Section 3.3).
- **The noise does not vanish at $\theta^\star$.** The variance of $n$ is $\frac{9+0+9}{3} = 6$ at $\theta = 3$ as much as anywhere else. This is the "unbiased is not zero" point, and it is the entire reason a constant step size cannot converge. The opposite regime, $\sigma^2(\theta^\star) = 0$, meaning every $\mathcal{L}_i$ is minimized at the same point, is the *interpolation* regime, and it changes the theory qualitatively (Section 3.3).

## 3 Noise: Variance, Second Moments, and What Can Fail

### 3.1 The second-moment identity

The lecture asserts a decomposition of the second moment and attributes it to prob07. Stated carefully:

**Lemma 3.1 (bias-variance split of the second moment).** For any random vector $g$ with $\mathbb{E}\lVert g\rVert^2 < \infty$ and any sub-sigma-algebra $\mathcal{G}$,

$ \mathbb{E}\big[\lVert g\rVert^2 \mid \mathcal{G}\big] \;=\; \big\lVert \mathbb{E}[g\mid\mathcal{G}]\big\rVert^2 \;+\; \mathbb{E}\big[\lVert g - \mathbb{E}[g\mid\mathcal{G}]\rVert^2 \mid \mathcal{G}\big]. $

*Proof.* Write $m = \mathbb{E}[g\mid\mathcal{G}]$ and expand $\lVert g\rVert^2 = \lVert (g-m) + m\rVert^2 = \lVert g-m\rVert^2 + 2\langle g-m, m\rangle + \lVert m\rVert^2$. Condition on $\mathcal{G}$: the middle term is $2\langle \mathbb{E}[g-m\mid\mathcal{G}], m\rangle = 0$ because $m$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable and $\mathbb{E}[g-m\mid\mathcal{G}] = 0$. **End of proof.**

Applied with $\mathcal{G} = \mathcal{F}_k$ and $g = g_k$, and using Theorem 2.2 for $m = \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$, equation (3.1) reads

$ \mathbb{E}_k\big[\lVert g_k\rVert^2\big] \;=\; \lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)\rVert^2 \;+\; \operatorname{Var}_k(g_k) \;\le\; G^2 + \sigma^2, \tag{3.1} $

where the first term is at most $G^2$ if $\mathcal{L}$ is $G$-Lipschitz and the second is at most $\sigma^2$ by assumption.

This single inequality is the only thing about the noise that Theorem 2's proof ever uses. It is why the theorem's constant is $\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}$ and not, say, $G+\sigma$: what enters is a second moment, and second moments add under the orthogonal decomposition of Lemma 3.1.

**Check on the running example at $\theta = 9$.** The three sampled gradients are $9$, $6$ and $3$, with second moment $(81+36+9)/3 = 42$. The decomposition predicts $\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}(9)\rVert^2 + \sigma^2 = 6^2 + 6 = 42$. Exact, as it must be: (3.1) is an identity before the two bounds are applied, and $\theta = 9$ is where the Lipschitz bound $G = 6$ on $[-3,9]$ is attained.

### 3.2 Three inequivalent meanings of "$\sigma^2$"

The literature uses one symbol for several conditions of increasing strength, and mixing them up is a common source of confusion when comparing rate statements. Each is given as: condition, statement, who needs it.

- **Uniform bounded variance.** $\operatorname{Var}_k(g_k) \le \sigma^2$ for all $\theta$. Needed by Theorems 2 and 4 as stated in the lecture.
- **Variance at the optimum.** $\sigma_\star^2 = \frac1N\sum_i \lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\theta^\star)\rVert^2$. Needed by modern smooth analyses; $\sigma_\star^2 = 0$ is interpolation.
- **Expected smoothness, or growth condition.** $\mathbb{E}_k\lVert g_k\rVert^2 \le A(\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)-\mathcal{L}^\star) + B\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}\rVert^2 + C$. Needed by analyses that must cover unbounded domains.

The first is the strongest and the least realistic. It is genuinely satisfied in the running example, where $\sigma^2 = 6$ everywhere (Section 2.5); it is not satisfied by, for example, least squares with heterogeneous inputs, where the per-sample gradient $(\langle a_i,\theta\rangle - y_i)a_i$ has variance growing quadratically in $\lVert\theta\rVert$. In that setting the honest statement is the third item, which reduces to bounded variance on a bounded domain, and the theorems survive with modified constants [1, Section 4.3].

### 3.3 When the assumption fails, and what replaces it

The lecture flags that bounded variance is an assumption, not a theorem, and can fail, then leaves it there. Three ways it fails, and the repair in each case:

- **Unbounded domain.** A quadratic is not globally Lipschitz, so neither $G$ nor $\sigma^2$ is globally finite, which is exactly the lecture's own caveat that its constants $G = 6$ and $D = 3$ are valid on the interval $[-3,9]$ where the run lives. The clean repairs are either to project onto a compact convex set (projected SGD, same proof with the nonexpansiveness of the projection inserted; opt03 Section 9.4) or to drop Lipschitzness and assume smoothness instead, which lets $\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}\rVert^2 \le 2L(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}^\star)$ absorb the growth (opt03's Theorem 3 sandwich).
- **Interpolation.** If the model can fit every training point exactly, the overparameterized regime of deep learning, then $\nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\theta^\star) = 0$ for every $i$, so $\sigma_\star^2 = 0$ and the noise *does* sleep at the solution. Then constant-step SGD converges linearly, at a rate governed by $L/\mu$ rather than by $\sigma^2$, with no averaging and no schedule. This is the theory of Ma, Bassily and Belkin [18], and it is a much better model of a modern training run's final phase than anything in this lecture.
- **Heavy tails.** Gradient noise in transformer training is empirically heavy-tailed; the variance may be infinite, with only a $\kappa$-th moment finite for some $\kappa \in (1,2]$. Then no bounded-variance analysis applies, and the standard fix is **gradient clipping**, which trades a controlled bias for a bounded second moment. Zhang et al. [20] give a matching theory and use it to explain why adaptive methods outperform plain SGD on attention models.

### 3.4 Why $\sigma^2$ is the right price tag

It is worth pausing on why a variance, of all things, is what SGD pays for. Both headline rates have the shape

$ \text{(progress term, shrinking in } \alpha) \;+\; (\text{noise term, growing in } \alpha), $

and the noise term is always $\alpha^2 \cdot \mathbb{E}\lVert g\rVert^2$, coming from the $\alpha^2\lVert g_k\rVert^2$ produced when the update is squared. A first-order method that only used $g_k$ linearly would pay nothing for noise; it is the *quadratic* in the distance-to-optimum potential $\lVert\theta_k-\theta^\star\rVert^2$ that converts noise into cost. This is the same phenomenon as in prob05: sums of independent zero-mean terms cancel in mean and accumulate in variance.

## 4 Constant Step Size: the Noise Floor

### 4.1 The running example, solved exactly

The lecture derives the error recursion and its fixed point. Here is the derivation with the signs tracked and the independence hypothesis made explicit.

Let $e_k = \theta_k - \theta^\star = \theta_k - 3$. With $g_k = \theta_k - c_{i_k}$ and $n_k = 3 - c_{i_k}$, so that $g_k = \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k) + n_k$ as in Section 2.5,

$ e_{k+1} \;=\; \theta_k - \alpha g_k - 3 \;=\; e_k - \alpha(e_k + n_k) \;=\; (1-\alpha)\,e_k \;-\; \alpha\, n_k. \tag{4.1} $

The original writes $+\alpha n_k$; since $n_k$ is symmetric about $0$, taking the values $3$, $0$ and $-3$ with probability $\tfrac13$ each, the two recursions have identical laws and identical variances, so nothing downstream changes.

**Proposition 4.1 (exact mean and variance).** With $\theta_0$ deterministic, $m_k = \mathbb{E}[e_k]$ and $v_k = \operatorname{Var}(e_k)$ satisfy

$ m_{k+1} = (1-\alpha)m_k, \qquad v_{k+1} = (1-\alpha)^2 v_k + \alpha^2\sigma^2, \qquad v_0 = 0, $

hence, for $0 < \alpha < 2$,

$ m_k = (1-\alpha)^k e_0 \to 0, \qquad v_k \;=\; \frac{\alpha\sigma^2}{2-\alpha}\Big(1 - (1-\alpha)^{2k}\Big) \;\longrightarrow\; v_\infty = \frac{\alpha\,\sigma^2}{2-\alpha}. $

Consequently $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{L}(\theta_k) - \mathcal{L}^\star\big] = \tfrac12\mathbb{E}[e_k^2] = \tfrac12(m_k^2 + v_k) \to \dfrac{\alpha\sigma^2}{2(2-\alpha)}$.

*Proof.* Take expectations in (4.1) and use $\mathbb{E}[n_k] = 0$ together with the independence of $n_k$ from $\mathcal{F}_k$, hence from $e_k$: the mean recursion is immediate. For the variance, $e_{k+1} - m_{k+1} = (1-\alpha)(e_k - m_k) - \alpha n_k$ is a sum of two *independent* zero-mean terms, so variances add (prob05): $v_{k+1} = (1-\alpha)^2 v_k + \alpha^2 \operatorname{Var}(n_k) = (1-\alpha)^2 v_k + \alpha^2\sigma^2$. This is an affine recursion $v_{k+1} = r v_k + s$ with $r = (1-\alpha)^2 \in [0,1)$ and $s = \alpha^2\sigma^2$; its unique fixed point is $v_\star = s/(1-r) = \alpha^2\sigma^2/(2\alpha - \alpha^2) = \alpha\sigma^2/(2-\alpha)$, and $v_k - v_\star = r^k(v_0 - v_\star)$ gives the closed form. The final display is the gap identity $\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\mathcal{L}^\star = \tfrac12(\theta-3)^2$ together with $\mathbb{E}[e^2] = (\mathbb{E}e)^2 + \operatorname{Var}(e)$. **End of proof.**

This is worth dwelling on, because it is an *exact* statement, not a bound: with a constant step, SGD on this problem converges in distribution to a stationary random variable centred at $\theta^\star$ with variance $\alpha\sigma^2/(2-\alpha)$, and it never does better. Everything the general theory says about noise floors is visible here without a single inequality.

### 4.2 The floor, numerically

With $\sigma^2 = 6$ and $e_0 = -3$, that is $\theta_0 = 0$, Proposition 4.1 gives $\mathbb{E}[e_k^2] = m_k^2 + v_k$ exactly. The two runs below are given as: step size, then the values at $k = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8$, then the floor $v_\infty$.

- $\alpha = 0.5$: $9$, $3.75$, $2.44$, $2.11$, $2.03$, $2.002$, $2.000$; floor $2.0$.
- $\alpha = 0.1$: $9$, $7.35$, $6.01$, $4.93$, $4.05$, $2.77$, $1.93$; floor $0.316$.

This is the "racing to different floors" picture with numbers attached. The large step is at its floor after about four steps and stays there; the small step is still far above its floor after eight and will need roughly $\log(0.316/9)/\log(0.81) \approx 16$ more steps to approach it. The corresponding expected gaps are $\tfrac12 v_\infty = 1.0$ and $0.158$.

Compare with the hand-run at $\alpha = 1/2$, which produced iterates $3$, $1.5$, $2.25$, $4.125$ with gaps $0$, $1.125$, $0.281$, $0.633$. Those are one realisation; Proposition 4.1 describes the ensemble the realisation is drawn from, and the observed gaps oscillating around $1$ is exactly the predicted floor $\tfrac12 v_\infty = 1$. The deterministic gradient-descent run with the same step size gives $1.5$, $2.25$, $2.625$, $2.8125$ with gaps $1.125$, $0.281$, $0.070$, $0.018$, decreasing geometrically forever: the noiseless comparison.

### 4.3 The general statement

The exact solvability above is special to a one-dimensional quadratic. The general phenomenon is a theorem:

**Theorem 4.2 (constant-step SGD converges to a neighbourhood).** Let $\mathcal{L}$ be $L$-smooth and $\mu$-strongly convex, let $g_k$ be unbiased with $\operatorname{Var}_k(g_k) \le \sigma^2$, and let $0 < \alpha \le 1/L$. Then

$ \mathbb{E}\big[\lVert\theta_k - \theta^\star\rVert^2\big] \;\le\; (1-\alpha\mu)^k\,\lVert\theta_0-\theta^\star\rVert^2 \;+\; \frac{\alpha\,\sigma^2}{\mu}. $

*Sketch.* Expand the square as in Lemma 5.1 below, use the strong-convexity floor (Lemma 7.3) instead of the plain tangent floor to obtain a contraction factor $(1-\alpha\mu)$, and bound the noise term by $\alpha^2\sigma^2$; then unroll the affine recursion $u_{k+1} \le (1-\alpha\mu)u_k + \alpha^2\sigma^2$ and sum the geometric series, whose limit is $\alpha^2\sigma^2/(\alpha\mu) = \alpha\sigma^2/\mu$. **End of proof.**

The structure matches Proposition 4.1: a geometrically decaying transient plus a floor proportional to $\alpha\sigma^2$. In the running example $\mu = L = 1$, and the general bound's floor $\alpha\sigma^2/\mu = 3$ at $\alpha = 0.5$ is a factor $1.5$ above the exact value $2$, the usual price of an inequality-based argument. The sharp constants and the general treatment are in Bottou, Curtis and Nocedal [1, Theorem 4.6]; see also Moulines and Bach [22] for the non-asymptotic analysis in the machine-learning setting.

### 4.4 Three ways out

Theorem 4.2 organises the rest of the lecture. The floor is proportional to $\alpha\sigma^2/\mu$, so there are exactly three dials:

- **Tune $\alpha$ to the horizon and average.** Choose $\alpha$ as a function of $K$ so that the transient has died by step $K$ and the floor is at the accuracy you want. This is Theorem 2 (Section 5), and the optimal trade-off produces $1/\sqrt{K}$.
- **Shrink $\sigma^2$.** Average $b$ samples per step: the floor drops by $b$. This is Theorem 3 (Section 6), and it is nearly free on parallel hardware.
- **Shrink $\alpha_k$ over time.** Drive the floor to zero as the iteration proceeds, slowly enough that the transient still dies. This is Theorem 4 (Section 7), and the classical conditions for "slowly enough" are Robbins and Monro's (Section 8.1).

## 5 Theorem 2, the Convex $O(1/\sqrt{K})$ Rate

### 5.1 Statement and hypotheses

**Theorem 5.1 (Theorem 2 of the lecture).** Let $\mathcal{L}$ be convex and $G$-Lipschitz on a convex set containing the iterates, with a minimizer $\theta^\star$ and $D = \lVert\theta_0-\theta^\star\rVert$. Run $\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \alpha g_k$ with $\mathbb{E}_k[g_k] = \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$ and $\operatorname{Var}_k(g_k) \le \sigma^2$, for $k = 0,\dots,K$, and set $\bar\theta_K = \frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^K \theta_k$. Then for any constant step $\alpha > 0$,

$ \mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{L}(\bar\theta_K) - \mathcal{L}^\star\big] \;\le\; \frac{D^2}{2\alpha(K+1)} \;+\; \frac{\alpha}{2}\big(G^2+\sigma^2\big), \tag{5.1} $

and the choice $\alpha = \dfrac{D}{\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}\,\sqrt{K+1}}$ minimizes the right-hand side, giving

$ \mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{L}(\bar\theta_K) - \mathcal{L}^\star\big] \;\le\; \frac{\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}\;D}{\sqrt{K+1}}. \tag{5.2} $

Note what is *not* assumed: no smoothness, no strong convexity, no bound on $N$, nothing about the geometry of the $\mathcal{L}_i$ individually. Note also that (5.1) is the substantive statement and (5.2) is a corollary of the one-variable minimization in Section 5.4; separating them is useful because (5.1) is what you use when $\alpha$ is fixed by other considerations.

### 5.2 The one-step inequality

Everything rests on a single lemma, which is Steps 1 through 4 of the lecture assembled.

**Lemma 5.2 (one step).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 5.1,

$ \mathbb{E}_k\big[\lVert\theta_{k+1}-\theta^\star\rVert^2\big] \;\le\; \lVert\theta_k-\theta^\star\rVert^2 \;-\; 2\alpha\big(\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)-\mathcal{L}^\star\big) \;+\; \alpha^2\big(G^2+\sigma^2\big). \tag{5.3} $

*Proof.* Four steps.

*(i) Expand.* Purely algebraic, no probability yet:

$ \lVert\theta_{k+1}-\theta^\star\rVert^2 = \lVert\theta_k - \alpha g_k - \theta^\star\rVert^2 = \lVert\theta_k-\theta^\star\rVert^2 - 2\alpha\langle g_k, \theta_k-\theta^\star\rangle + \alpha^2\lVert g_k\rVert^2. $

*(ii) Condition.* Apply $\mathbb{E}_k$. The first term is $\mathcal{F}_k$-measurable and passes through. In the second, $\theta_k - \theta^\star$ is $\mathcal{F}_k$-measurable (Lemma 2.1), so it comes out of the conditional expectation and unbiasedness (Theorem 2.2) replaces $g_k$ by the true gradient:

$ \mathbb{E}_k\big[\langle g_k, \theta_k-\theta^\star\rangle\big] = \big\langle \mathbb{E}_k[g_k], \theta_k-\theta^\star\big\rangle = \big\langle \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k), \theta_k-\theta^\star\big\rangle. $

This is the *only* place unbiasedness is used in the whole proof. The third term is handled by (3.1): $\mathbb{E}_k\lVert g_k\rVert^2 \le G^2+\sigma^2$.

*(iii) Convexity floor.* By the first-order characterization of convexity (opt03, Theorem 2.2) applied at $\theta_k$ with $y = \theta^\star$,

$ \mathcal{L}^\star = \mathcal{L}(\theta^\star) \;\ge\; \mathcal{L}(\theta_k) + \langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k), \theta^\star - \theta_k\rangle, $

which is equivalent to $\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k), \theta_k-\theta^\star\rangle \ge \mathcal{L}(\theta_k) - \mathcal{L}^\star$. Convexity enters here and nowhere else. Because the coefficient $-2\alpha$ is negative, lower-bounding the inner product upper-bounds the whole expression: the direction matters and is easy to get backwards.

*(iv) Combine.* Substituting (ii) and (iii) into (i) gives (5.3). **End of proof.**

### 5.3 Tower, telescope, and the averaged bound

Take total expectations in (5.3) using the tower property $\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_k[\cdot]] = \mathbb{E}[\cdot]$ (prob07), and write $u_k = \mathbb{E}\lVert\theta_k-\theta^\star\rVert^2$ and $\delta_k = \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)-\mathcal{L}^\star] \ge 0$:

$ u_{k+1} \;\le\; u_k - 2\alpha\,\delta_k + \alpha^2(G^2+\sigma^2), $

equivalently $2\alpha\,\delta_k \le u_k - u_{k+1} + \alpha^2(G^2+\sigma^2)$.

Sum over $k = 0,\dots,K$. The $u_k - u_{k+1}$ terms telescope (opt03, Section 7):

$ 2\alpha\sum_{k=0}^K \delta_k \;\le\; u_0 - u_{K+1} + (K+1)\alpha^2(G^2+\sigma^2) \;\le\; D^2 + (K+1)\alpha^2(G^2+\sigma^2), $

where the last step discards $-u_{K+1} \le 0$, a small but genuine loss, and the reason the bound says nothing about where the iterates end up. Dividing by $2\alpha(K+1)$:

$ \frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\delta_k \;\le\; \frac{D^2}{2\alpha(K+1)} + \frac{\alpha}{2}(G^2+\sigma^2). \tag{5.4} $

Finally, Jensen. The function $\mathcal{L}$ is convex and $\bar\theta_K$ is an average of the $\theta_k$, so $\mathcal{L}(\bar\theta_K) \le \frac{1}{K+1}\sum_k \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$ pointwise (prob01, Theorem 2); subtracting $\mathcal{L}^\star$ and taking expectations, and using linearity of expectation on the finite sum,

$ \mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{L}(\bar\theta_K)-\mathcal{L}^\star\big] \;\le\; \frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^K \delta_k, $

which combined with (5.4) is exactly (5.1). **End of proof.**

**Why the average, and not the last iterate?** The telescoped sum controls the *average* of the gaps $\delta_k$, and nothing in the argument says $\delta_K$ is any smaller than the others; indeed Section 4 shows that with a constant step the gaps hover at a floor rather than decreasing. Jensen is the device that converts "the average of the gaps is small" into a statement about a single point you can actually output. Without convexity, Jensen fails and there is no averaged-iterate theorem at all, which is one reason the nonconvex results in Section 8.4 take the different form "the best gradient norm along the trajectory is small".

### 5.4 The balance lemma

**Lemma 5.3.** For $a, b > 0$, $\varphi(\alpha) = \dfrac{a}{\alpha} + b\alpha$ on $\alpha > 0$ is strictly convex, attains its minimum at $\alpha^\star = \sqrt{a/b}$, and $\varphi(\alpha^\star) = 2\sqrt{ab}$; at the optimum the two terms are equal.

*Proof.* $\varphi'(\alpha) = -a/\alpha^2 + b$ vanishes if and only if $\alpha^2 = a/b$; $\varphi''(\alpha) = 2a/\alpha^3 > 0$ on the positive axis, so the critical point is the global minimum. Then $\varphi(\alpha^\star) = a\sqrt{b/a} + b\sqrt{a/b} = 2\sqrt{ab}$, and each term equals $\sqrt{ab}$. Equivalently, by the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality, $\frac{a}{\alpha}+b\alpha \ge 2\sqrt{ab}$ with equality if and only if $\frac{a}{\alpha} = b\alpha$. **End of proof.**

Apply it to (5.1) with $a = \dfrac{D^2}{2(K+1)}$ and $b = \dfrac{G^2+\sigma^2}{2}$:

$ \alpha^\star = \sqrt{\frac{D^2}{2(K+1)}\cdot\frac{2}{G^2+\sigma^2}} = \frac{D}{\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}\sqrt{K+1}}, \qquad \varphi(\alpha^\star) = 2\sqrt{\frac{D^2(G^2+\sigma^2)}{4(K+1)}} = \frac{\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}\,D}{\sqrt{K+1}}, $

which is (5.2). The $\sqrt{K+1}$ is born exactly here: the progress term falls like $1/(\alpha K)$ and the noise term grows like $\alpha$, so the best a constant step can do is their geometric mean, $1/\sqrt{K}$. The lecture plots this U-shaped curve for $K = 99$ in the running example: $\varphi(\alpha) = 0.045/\alpha + 21\alpha$, minimized at $\alpha^\star = \sqrt{0.045/21} = 0.0463$ with value $2\sqrt{0.045\cdot 21} = 1.944$.

### 5.5 Numbers, and how loose the bound is

The constants for the running example are $G = 6$, the Lipschitz constant on $[-3,9]$, and $\sigma^2 = 6$, so $G^2+\sigma^2 = 42$, and $D = 3$; the bound is $\sqrt{42}\cdot 3/\sqrt{K+1} = 19.44/\sqrt{K+1}$. Since this problem is a one-dimensional quadratic, the truth can be computed exactly from the linear recursion (4.1): $\bar\theta_K$ is a linear functional of the independent draws, and its mean and variance follow from summing the covariances $\operatorname{Cov}(e_k,e_l) = \alpha^2\sigma^2\sum_{j<\min(k,l)}(1-\alpha)^{k+l-2-2j}$.

The comparison, each row given as: $K$, tuned $\alpha$, the Theorem 5.1 bound, the exact $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\bar\theta_K)-\mathcal{L}^\star]$, and the exact value for the last iterate.

- $K = 99$: $\alpha = 0.0463$; bound $1.944$; exact averaged $0.227$; exact last iterate $0.072$.
- $K = 999$: $\alpha = 0.0146$; bound $0.615$; exact averaged $0.024$; exact last iterate $0.022$.
- $K = 9999$: $\alpha = 0.0046$; bound $0.194$; exact values not computed.

The bound is loose by roughly an order of magnitude, for identifiable reasons: $G = 6$ is the worst-case gradient norm over the whole interval while the run spends its time near $\theta^\star$ where $\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}\rVert \approx 0$; the discarded $-u_{K+1}$ costs something; and Jensen is not tight. What the bound gets exactly right is the *rate*: the exact values fall by a factor of about $\sqrt{10}$ per decade of $K$, matching $K^{-1/2}$. That is the correct way to read a bound of this kind: the constant is a warranty, the exponent is the physics.

### 5.6 The Lipschitz caveat, taken seriously

The lecture notes honestly that a quadratic is not globally Lipschitz, but that the run stayed in $[0, 4.125]$, and takes $G = 6$ on $[-3,9]$. Strictly, Theorem 5.1's hypothesis is that $\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)\rVert \le G$ *at every iterate*, which is a statement about a random trajectory, not about a fixed interval. Three ways to make this airtight, in increasing order of honesty:

- **Project.** Replace the update by $\theta_{k+1} = \Pi_C(\theta_k - \alpha g_k)$ for a compact convex $C$ containing $\theta^\star$ on which $\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}\rVert \le G$. The proof is unchanged: the projection is nonexpansive and $\Pi_C(\theta^\star) = \theta^\star$, so $\lVert\Pi_C(z)-\theta^\star\rVert \le \lVert z-\theta^\star\rVert$ and step (i) of Lemma 5.2 only improves. This is the standard statement of the theorem [2], [4, Section 6.1].
- **Assume smoothness instead.** Drop Lipschitzness and use $L$-smoothness: the analogous result is $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\bar\theta_K)-\mathcal{L}^\star] \le \frac{D^2}{2\alpha(K+1)} + \frac{\alpha\sigma^2}{2}$ for $\alpha \le 1/(2L)$, giving $O(1/K + \sigma/\sqrt{K})$, the fast gradient-descent rate plus a noise term, which reduces to opt03's $O(1/K)$ when $\sigma = 0$.
- **Verify a posteriori.** Bound the iterates first: Lemma 5.2 with the crude floor $\delta_k \ge 0$ already gives $u_{k+1} \le u_k + \alpha^2(G^2+\sigma^2)$, hence $u_k \le D^2 + k\alpha^2(G^2+\sigma^2)$. Then take $G$ to be the Lipschitz constant on the resulting ball. With the tuned $\alpha$ this ball has radius $O(D)$, so the argument closes.

### 5.7 Horizon-free step sizes

The tuned $\alpha$ needs $K$, $D$, $G$ and $\sigma$ in advance, which the lecture correctly calls a theory dial. The standard fix is the decaying schedule $\alpha_k = \dfrac{D}{\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}\sqrt{k+1}}$. Redoing Section 5.3 with $\alpha_k$ inside the sum gives

$ \sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_k \delta_k \;\le\; \frac{D^2}{2} + \frac{G^2+\sigma^2}{2}\sum_{k=0}^K \alpha_k^2, $

and with $\sum_{k=0}^K \alpha_k \ge c_1 D\sqrt{K}/\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}$ and $\sum_{k=0}^K\alpha_k^2 \le c_2 D^2\log K/(G^2+\sigma^2)$, the $\alpha_k$-weighted average of the iterates satisfies a bound of order $D\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}\,\log K/\sqrt{K}$: the same rate up to a logarithm, valid at every $K$ simultaneously. The logarithm is removable by **suffix averaging**, meaning averaging only the last half of the iterates, or by a doubling trick; see Rakhlin, Shamir and Sridharan [7] and Lacoste-Julien, Schmidt and Bach [6].

### 5.8 Is $1/\sqrt{K}$ the best possible?

Yes, and this is worth knowing before concluding that the analysis is crude. For the class of convex $G$-Lipschitz functions on a ball of radius $D$, accessed through a stochastic first-order oracle with variance $\sigma^2$, *every* algorithm has worst-case error $\Omega\big(\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}\,D/\sqrt{K}\big)$ after $K$ oracle calls. The information-theoretic lower bound is due to Nemirovski and Yudin and is given in modern form, with matching constants, by Agarwal, Bartlett, Ravikumar and Wainwright [10]. So Theorem 5.1 is optimal in $K$, $D$ and the noise level, not merely the best we could prove. The gap with opt03's gradient-descent rate $O(1/K)$ is therefore not a proof artefact: it is the intrinsic price of a noisy oracle, and it is paid back in the $N$-fold cheaper steps.

**The accounting, once more.** To reach expected gap $\varepsilon$: gradient descent needs $O(1/\varepsilon)$ steps times $N$ gradients, that is $O(N/\varepsilon)$; SGD needs $O\big((G^2+\sigma^2)D^2/\varepsilon^2\big)$ steps times one gradient. Crossover at $N \approx 1/\varepsilon$. For $N = 10^6$ and $\varepsilon = 10^{-2}$, SGD is $10^4$ times cheaper. And a training run is not trying to reach $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$: the empirical risk is itself only a proxy for the population risk, whose statistical error is $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ (prob05, prob06), so optimizing far below that is wasted effort.

## 6 Mini-Batching

### 6.1 The update, and why it is still unbiased

The mini-batch update averages $b$ sampled gradients per step:

$ \theta_{k+1} \;=\; \theta_k - \alpha\,\frac{1}{b}\sum_{j=1}^{b} \nabla\mathcal{L}_{i_{k,j}}(\theta_k), \qquad i_{k,1},\dots,i_{k,b} \ \text{independent and uniform on } \{1,\dots,N\}. $

Unbiasedness is immediate from linearity: $\mathbb{E}_k\big[\frac1b\sum_j \nabla\mathcal{L}_{i_{k,j}}\big] = \frac1b\sum_j \mathbb{E}_k[\nabla\mathcal{L}_{i_{k,j}}] = \frac1b \cdot b\,\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k) = \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$. The average of unbiased estimators is unbiased regardless of dependence; independence is needed only for the variance.

### 6.2 Theorem 3, with replacement

**Theorem 6.1 (Theorem 3 of the lecture).** If $g^{(1)},\dots,g^{(b)}$ are independent copies of the single-sample stochastic gradient at $\theta$, with $\operatorname{Var}(g^{(j)}) = \sigma^2$, meaning $\mathbb{E}\lVert g^{(j)} - \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\rVert^2 = \sigma^2$, then

$ \operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac1b\sum_{j=1}^b g^{(j)}\Big) \;=\; \frac{\sigma^2}{b}. $

*Proof.* Write $n^{(j)} = g^{(j)} - \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)$, so the $n^{(j)}$ are independent, identically distributed, zero-mean, with $\mathbb{E}\lVert n^{(j)}\rVert^2 = \sigma^2$. Then

$ \mathbb{E}\Big\lVert \frac1b\sum_j n^{(j)}\Big\rVert^2 = \frac{1}{b^2}\sum_{j}\sum_{l} \mathbb{E}\big\langle n^{(j)}, n^{(l)}\big\rangle = \frac{1}{b^2}\Big(\sum_j \mathbb{E}\lVert n^{(j)}\rVert^2 + \sum_{j\ne l}\big\langle \mathbb{E} n^{(j)}, \mathbb{E} n^{(l)}\big\rangle\Big) = \frac{b\sigma^2}{b^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{b}, $

the cross terms vanishing because independence lets the expectation factor and each factor is zero. **End of proof.**

This is prob05's variance-of-averages applied verbatim to vectors: variances add for independent terms, and the $1/b$ in the average squares to $1/b^2$, leaving $1/b$.

### 6.3 Without replacement, the correction the lecture gestures at

The lecture notes that the source material treats subset sampling, where the variance is even smaller, and proves only the independent case. Here is the exact statement, which also explains the claim about the full-batch corner.

**Proposition 6.2 (finite-population correction).** Let $I \subseteq \{1,\dots,N\}$ be a uniformly random subset of size $b$, sampled *without* replacement, and let $\sigma^2 = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\rVert^2$. Then the batch gradient is unbiased and

$ \operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac1b\sum_{i\in I}\nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\theta)\Big) \;=\; \frac{\sigma^2}{b}\cdot\frac{N-b}{N-1}. $

*Proof.* Put $n_i = \nabla\mathcal{L}_i(\theta) - \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)$, so $\sum_{i=1}^N n_i = 0$ and $\frac1N\sum_i \lVert n_i\rVert^2 = \sigma^2$. Let $Z_i = \mathbf{1}\{i \in I\}$, so $\mathbb{P}(Z_i=1) = b/N$ and, for $i \ne l$, $\mathbb{P}(Z_i = Z_l = 1) = \frac{b(b-1)}{N(N-1)}$. Unbiasedness: $\mathbb{E}[\frac1b\sum_i Z_i n_i] = \frac1b\cdot\frac bN \sum_i n_i = 0$. For the second moment,

$ \mathbb{E}\Big\lVert\frac1b\sum_i Z_i n_i\Big\rVert^2 = \frac{1}{b^2}\Big( \frac{b}{N}\sum_i \lVert n_i\rVert^2 + \frac{b(b-1)}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i\ne l}\langle n_i, n_l\rangle \Big). $

Now $\sum_{i \ne l}\langle n_i,n_l\rangle = \lVert\sum_i n_i\rVert^2 - \sum_i\lVert n_i\rVert^2 = -N\sigma^2$, since the $n_i$ sum to zero. Substituting and using $\sum_i\lVert n_i\rVert^2 = N\sigma^2$,

$ \frac{1}{b^2}\Big(\frac{b}{N}N\sigma^2 - \frac{b(b-1)}{N(N-1)}N\sigma^2\Big) = \frac{\sigma^2}{b}\Big(1 - \frac{b-1}{N-1}\Big) = \frac{\sigma^2}{b}\cdot\frac{N-b}{N-1}. $

**End of proof.**

The correction factor $\frac{N-b}{N-1}$ is $1$ at $b=1$, no difference, since one draw is one draw, and $0$ at $b = N$, where the batch *is* the whole dataset, so the batch gradient is the full gradient and has no variance. That is precisely the claim that gradient descent is the $b=N$, no-repeats corner of mini-batch SGD, now with a formula.

**Check on the running example**, with $N=3$, $\sigma^2 = 6$, at $\theta = 0$ where the three sampled gradients are $0$, $-3$ and $-6$. Each entry is given as: batch size $b$, the with-replacement variance $\sigma^2/b$, the without-replacement formula, and the without-replacement value by direct enumeration.

- $b = 1$: with replacement $6$; formula $6\cdot\frac22 = 6$; by enumeration the values are $0,-3,-6$, variance $6$.
- $b = 2$: with replacement $3$; formula $3\cdot\frac12 = 1.5$; by enumeration the batch means are $-1.5,-3,-4.5$, variance $1.5$.
- $b = 3$: with replacement $2$; formula $2\cdot\frac02 = 0$; by enumeration the only batch has mean $-3$, variance $0$.

### 6.4 The histogram, derived

For $b = 3$ *with* replacement at $\theta = 0$, the batch gradient is $\frac13(u_1+u_2+u_3)$ with the $u_j$ independent and uniform on $\{0,-3,-6\}$. Writing $u_j = -3 s_j$ with $s_j \in \{0,1,2\}$, the batch gradient is $-(s_1+s_2+s_3)$, and the number of triples with $s_1+s_2+s_3 = s$ is the coefficient of $x^s$ in $(1+x+x^2)^3$, namely

$ (1+x+x^2)^3 = 1 + 3x + 6x^2 + 7x^3 + 6x^4 + 3x^5 + x^6, $

which gives the counts $1, 3, 6, 7, 6, 3, 1$ out of $27$ that the lecture displays as a histogram. The mean is $-3$ and the variance is $\operatorname{Var}(s_1+s_2+s_3) = 3\operatorname{Var}(s_1) = 3\cdot\frac{(0-1)^2+(1-1)^2+(2-1)^2}{3} = 2$, matching Theorem 6.1: $\sigma^2/b = 6/3 = 2$.

### 6.5 What $b$ actually buys

Substituting $\sigma^2 \to \sigma^2/b$ into Theorem 5.1 gives the bound $\dfrac{\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2/b}\;D}{\sqrt{K+1}}$, and the honest reading is the lecture's: *batching attacks only the noise part of the bill*. In the running example $G^2 = 36$ dominates $\sigma^2 = 6$, so $b = 3$ improves $\sqrt{42} = 6.48$ to $\sqrt{38} = 6.16$, a five percent gain for three times the work per step. Batching pays when $\sigma^2 \gg G^2$, that is when the disagreement among data points dwarfs the size of the average gradient, which is the situation early in training and near a noisy optimum.

The systems argument is the one that actually decides $b$ in practice:

- **Sequentially, batching is close to a wash.** Variance divided by $b$ at compute multiplied by $b$. In the $\sigma$-dominated regime the bound improves like $1/\sqrt{b}$ while the cost grows like $b$: a losing trade.
- **On parallel hardware it is nearly free.** The $b$ gradients are independent and are computed simultaneously; wall-clock per step is roughly constant until memory or interconnect saturates. Mini-batching is variance reduction that the hardware gives away, which is why it exists.
- **There is a critical batch size.** Past the point where $\sigma^2/b \ll G^2$, equivalently where the gradient's signal dominates its noise, further increases in $b$ buy essentially nothing in steps-to-target while still costing compute. McCandlish et al. [21] formalise this as the *gradient noise scale* and predict the observed knee in large-batch training curves; Goyal et al. [24] give the companion empirical recipe, linear scaling of $\alpha$ with $b$ plus warmup, that makes large batches trainable in the first place.

Note that the linear scaling rule has a theoretical shadow in Theorem 5.1: the optimal $\alpha$ is proportional to $1/\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2/b}$, which for $\sigma$-dominated noise is proportional to $\sqrt{b}$, not $b$. The discrepancy between $\sqrt{b}$ and the empirically successful $b$ is one of the honest open gaps between this theory and practice.

## 7 Strongly Convex SGD, Theorem 4

### 7.1 The setting

The lecture changes notation here, from $\theta$ to $x$, and changes problem, from a general finite sum to an explicitly regularized one:

$ \min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^d} \; F(x) \;=\; f(x) + \frac{\mu}{2}\lVert x\rVert^2, \qquad f \ \text{convex and } G\text{-Lipschitz}, $

with the update

$ x_{k+1} \;=\; x_k - \alpha_k\big(g_k + \mu x_k\big), \qquad \alpha_k = \frac{1}{\mu(k+1)}, \qquad x_0 = 0, \tag{7.1} $

where $g_k$ is an unbiased estimate of $\nabla f(x_k)$ with $\operatorname{Var}_k(g_k)\le\sigma^2$. Three design choices deserve comment.

- **Only $f$ is sampled.** The regularizer $\frac\mu2\lVert x\rVert^2$ is differentiated exactly; it costs nothing, since it does not depend on the data. So $\mathbb{E}_k[g_k + \mu x_k] = \nabla f(x_k) + \mu x_k = \nabla F(x_k)$ and the *whole* step is an unbiased estimate of $\nabla F(x_k)$, with the same variance $\sigma^2$ as $g_k$.
- **$F$ is $\mu$-strongly convex** by opt03 Section 5.1: a convex function plus $\frac\mu2\lVert\cdot\rVert^2$ is $\mu$-strongly convex, so $x^\star$ exists and is unique (opt03, Proposition 5.2). This is a ridge-regularized objective, exactly opt02's regularized least squares in shape.
- **The schedule is anytime.** $\alpha_k = 1/(\mu(k+1))$ needs $\mu$ but not $K$, so unlike Theorem 5.1 there is no horizon to guess. It also blows up as $\mu \to 0$, which is the correct signal: without strong convexity no rate of this kind holds, and the theorem degenerates rather than lying.

**The running example, rewritten.** Completing the square in the finite sum,

$ \mathcal{L}_i(\theta) = \tfrac12(\theta-c_i)^2 = \Big(\tfrac{c_i^2}{2} - c_i\theta\Big) + \tfrac12\theta^2, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \mathcal{L}(\theta) = (7.5 - 3\theta) + \tfrac12\theta^2, $

with $f(\theta) = 7.5-3\theta$ and $\mu = 1$, since $\frac13\sum_i c_i^2/2 = (0+4.5+18)/3 = 7.5$ and $\frac13\sum_i c_i = 3$. Now $f$ is *linear*, hence convex with $G = 3$ globally, with no interval caveat, unlike Section 5.6. The sampled gradient is $g_k = \nabla f_{i_k}(\theta) = -c_{i_k} \in \{0,-3,-6\}$, with mean $-3 = \nabla f$ and variance $6 = \sigma^2$, so $G^2+\sigma^2 = 9+6 = 15$; and here (3.1) holds with equality, since $\mathbb{E}\lVert g\rVert^2 = (0+9+36)/3 = 15$.

### 7.2 Statement

**Theorem 7.1 (Theorem 4 of the lecture).** Under the setting of Section 7.1, with $\bar x_k = \frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{i=0}^k x_i$,

$ \mathbb{E}\big[F(\bar x_k) - F(x^\star)\big] \;\le\; \frac{2(G^2+\sigma^2)}{\mu}\cdot\frac{1+\log(k+1)}{k+1} \qquad \text{for every } k \ge 0. $

Two features to notice before the proof. First, $\lVert x_0 - x^\star\rVert$ is **absent**: the bound does not depend on where you started. Section 7.5 shows exactly why, namely that the schedule puts weight $0$ on the initial condition in a weighted telescope. Second, the rate $\log k / k$ beats $1/\sqrt{k}$ eventually but not immediately; Section 7.7 computes the crossover.

### 7.3 Step 1, unrolling, and why the weights are uniform

Rewrite (7.1) as a convex blend. Since $\alpha_k \mu \cdot \frac{-g_k}{\mu} = -\alpha_k g_k$,

$ x_{k+1} \;=\; (1-\alpha_k\mu)\,x_k \;+\; \alpha_k\mu\cdot\Big(\frac{-g_k}{\mu}\Big), $

so each step moves the iterate a fraction $\alpha_k\mu$ of the way toward the "target" $-g_k/\mu$. Unrolling the affine recursion $y_{k+1} = \beta_k y_k + z_k$ (opt03 Section 6.2, the same move) gives

$ x_{k+1} \;=\; \Big(\prod_{s=0}^{k}(1-\alpha_s\mu)\Big)x_0 \;+\; \sum_{i=0}^{k} w_i^{(k)}\Big(\frac{-g_i}{\mu}\Big), \qquad w_i^{(k)} = \alpha_i\mu\prod_{s=i+1}^{k}(1-\alpha_s\mu), $

and $x_0 = 0$ kills the head term. These weights are what the original note calls $\theta_i$; they are renamed $w_i$ here to avoid colliding with the parameter $\theta$ of Sections 1 to 6.

**Lemma 7.2 (uniform weights).** With $\alpha_i = \frac{1}{\mu(i+1)}$ one has $\alpha_i\mu = \frac{1}{i+1}$ and $1-\alpha_s\mu = \frac{s}{s+1}$, hence

$ w_i^{(k)} \;=\; \frac{1}{i+1}\prod_{s=i+1}^{k}\frac{s}{s+1} \;=\; \frac{1}{i+1}\cdot\frac{i+1}{k+1} \;=\; \frac{1}{k+1} \quad\text{for every } i = 0,\dots,k, $

so $\sum_{i=0}^k w_i^{(k)} = 1$: the iterate $x_{k+1}$ is a genuine **convex combination** of the targets $-g_i/\mu$, indeed their plain average.

*Proof.* The product $\prod_{s=i+1}^k \frac{s}{s+1}$ telescopes to $\frac{i+1}{k+1}$, every numerator cancelling the previous denominator; for $i = k$ the empty product is $1$ and $w_k^{(k)} = \frac{1}{k+1}$ as well. **End of proof.**

This is a striking identity, and it is the reason the schedule $1/(\mu(k+1))$ is chosen rather than any other decaying sequence: it makes SGD compute a *running average* of the targets. In the running example $\mu = 1$ and $-g_j/\mu = c_{i_j}$, so

$ x_{k+1} \;=\; \frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{j=0}^{k} c_{i_j}, $

the sample mean of the drawn targets, which the lecture checks against the draws $6, 0, 3, 6$: $x_1 = 6$, $x_2 = 3$, $x_3 = 3$, $x_4 = 3.75$. The optimizer has rediscovered prob05's law of large numbers.

### 7.4 Step 2, the second moment of the step

**Lemma 7.3.** For every $k$, $\mathbb{E}\lVert x_k\rVert^2 \le \dfrac{G^2+\sigma^2}{\mu^2}$, and consequently $\mathbb{E}\lVert g_k+\mu x_k\rVert^2 \le 4(G^2+\sigma^2)$.

*Proof.* For $k = 0$ the first claim is trivial, since $x_0=0$. For $k \ge 1$, Lemma 7.2 writes $x_k = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} w_i(-g_i/\mu)$ with $w_i \ge 0$ summing to $1$; since $\lVert\cdot\rVert^2$ is convex, Jensen gives $\big\lVert \sum_i w_i \frac{-g_i}{\mu}\big\rVert^2 \le \sum_i w_i \frac{\lVert g_i\rVert^2}{\mu^2}$, hence

$ \mathbb{E}\lVert x_k\rVert^2 \le \sum_i w_i \frac{\mathbb{E}\lVert g_i\rVert^2}{\mu^2} \le \frac{G^2+\sigma^2}{\mu^2}, $

using $\mathbb{E}\lVert g_i\rVert^2 \le G^2+\sigma^2$ from (3.1), where here $G$ bounds $\lVert\nabla f\rVert$. For the second claim, convexity of $\lVert\cdot\rVert^2$ again gives $\lVert a+b\rVert^2 \le 2\lVert a\rVert^2+2\lVert b\rVert^2$: apply Jensen to $\lVert\frac{a+b}{2}\rVert^2 \le \frac12\lVert a\rVert^2+\frac12\lVert b\rVert^2$ and multiply by $4$. So

$ \mathbb{E}\lVert g_k + \mu x_k\rVert^2 \le 2\,\mathbb{E}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 + 2\mu^2\,\mathbb{E}\lVert x_k\rVert^2 \le 2(G^2+\sigma^2) + 2(G^2+\sigma^2) = 4(G^2+\sigma^2). $

**End of proof.**

**A gap in the presented version, closed.** The lecture writes this bound with $\mathbb{E}_k$ in Step 3a, as though $\mathbb{E}_k\lVert g_k+\mu x_k\rVert^2 \le 4(G^2+\sigma^2)$ held conditionally. It does not: $\lVert x_k\rVert^2$ is $\mathcal{F}_k$-measurable and can be arbitrarily large on a bad trajectory, so only the *total* expectation is bounded. The repair is to take total expectations at the end of Step 3a and apply Lemma 7.3 there, which is what Section 7.5 does. Nothing about the final statement changes, only the order in which the expectations are taken.

Note also why Lemma 7.2 was needed *before* Lemma 7.3: without knowing that the $w_i$ form a convex combination, there is no reason for $\mathbb{E}\lVert x_k\rVert^2$ to be bounded at all. The uniform-weight identity is doing structural work, not just decoration. On the running example the bound $\mathbb{E}\lVert x_k\rVert^2 \le 15$ is attained exactly at $k=1$, where $x_1 = c_{i_0}$ and $\mathbb{E}[c^2] = 15$, and is loose thereafter, since $\mathbb{E}[x_k^2] = 9 + 6/k$.

### 7.5 Steps 3 to 5: the strong floor, the schedule, the telescope

**The floor.** Since $F$ is $\mu$-strongly convex (opt03, Theorem 5.1), for all $x$ and $y$ we have $F(y) \ge F(x) + \langle\nabla F(x), y-x\rangle + \frac\mu2\lVert y-x\rVert^2$. Taking $x = x_k$, $y = x^\star$ and rearranging,

$ \big\langle \nabla F(x_k),\, x_k - x^\star\big\rangle \;\ge\; F(x_k) - F(x^\star) + \frac{\mu}{2}\lVert x_k-x^\star\rVert^2. \tag{7.2} $

This is step (iii) of Lemma 5.2 with an extra term $\frac\mu2\lVert x_k-x^\star\rVert^2$, and that extra term is the entire source of the improved rate.

**Expand and condition.** Exactly as in Lemma 5.2, with the step $g_k+\mu x_k$ in place of $g_k$, using $\mathbb{E}_k[g_k+\mu x_k] = \nabla F(x_k)$:

$ \mathbb{E}_k\lVert x_{k+1}-x^\star\rVert^2 = \lVert x_k-x^\star\rVert^2 - 2\alpha_k\big\langle\nabla F(x_k), x_k-x^\star\big\rangle + \alpha_k^2\,\mathbb{E}_k\lVert g_k+\mu x_k\rVert^2. $

Now take total expectations by the tower property, insert the floor (7.2), legitimately, since the coefficient $-2\alpha_k$ is negative, and apply Lemma 7.3 to the last term:

$ \mathbb{E}\lVert x_{k+1}-x^\star\rVert^2 \;\le\; (1-\alpha_k\mu)\,\mathbb{E}\lVert x_k-x^\star\rVert^2 \;-\; 2\alpha_k\,\mathbb{E}\big[F(x_k)-F(x^\star)\big] \;+\; 4\alpha_k^2(G^2+\sigma^2). \tag{7.3} $

The term $\frac\mu2\lVert x_k-x^\star\rVert^2$ from the floor, multiplied by $-2\alpha_k$, became $-\alpha_k\mu\lVert x_k-x^\star\rVert^2$ and turned the leading coefficient into a **contraction** $(1-\alpha_k\mu)$.

**Plug the schedule.** With $\alpha_k = \frac{1}{\mu(k+1)}$ we have $1-\alpha_k\mu = \frac{k}{k+1}$ and $\frac{1}{2\alpha_k} = \frac{\mu(k+1)}{2}$. Dividing (7.3) by $2\alpha_k$ and rearranging,

$ \mathbb{E}\big[F(x_k)-F(x^\star)\big] \;\le\; a_k - a_{k+1} \;+\; \frac{2(G^2+\sigma^2)}{\mu(k+1)}, \qquad a_i := \frac{\mu i}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\lVert x_i-x^\star\rVert^2, \tag{7.4} $

because $\frac{\mu(k+1)}{2}\cdot\frac{k}{k+1} = \frac{\mu k}{2}$ and $2\alpha_k\cdot 2(G^2+\sigma^2) = \frac{2(G^2+\sigma^2)}{\mu(k+1)}$.

**The trick.** The schedule was chosen so that the coefficients in (7.4) are *exactly* $a_k$ and $a_{k+1}$: a weighted telescope. Summing over $i = 0,\dots,k$,

$ \sum_{i=0}^{k}(a_i - a_{i+1}) \;=\; a_0 - a_{k+1} \;\le\; a_0 \;=\; \frac{\mu\cdot 0}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\lVert x_0-x^\star\rVert^2 \;=\; 0. $

The weight $\frac{\mu i}{2}$ vanishes at $i=0$. That is why the initial distance is absent from Theorem 7.1: not because the analysis was clever about it, but because the schedule assigns it weight zero. A constant step size cannot do this, since its telescope head is $\frac{1}{2\alpha}D^2$, exactly the term that forced Theorem 5.1 to trade $\alpha$ against $D$.

**The harmonic sum.** What remains is the sum of the noise rents.

**Lemma 7.4.** $\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac1j \le 1 + \log n$ for every $n \ge 1$.

*Proof.* For $j \ge 2$ and $x \in [j-1,j]$ we have $\frac1j \le \frac1x$, so $\frac1j \le \int_{j-1}^{j}\frac{dx}{x}$. Summing from $j=2$ to $n$ gives $\sum_{j=2}^n \frac1j \le \int_1^n\frac{dx}{x} = \log n$; adding the $j=1$ term gives the claim. **End of proof.**

Summing (7.4) over $i = 0,\dots,k$ and using $\sum_{i=0}^k \frac{1}{i+1} = \sum_{j=1}^{k+1}\frac1j \le 1+\log(k+1)$:

$ \sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}\big[F(x_i)-F(x^\star)\big] \;\le\; 0 \;+\; \frac{2(G^2+\sigma^2)}{\mu}\big(1+\log(k+1)\big). $

**Jensen.** $F$ is convex and $\bar x_k$ is the average of $x_0,\dots,x_k$, so dividing by $k+1$,

$ \mathbb{E}\big[F(\bar x_k)-F(x^\star)\big] \;\le\; \frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}\big[F(x_i)-F(x^\star)\big] \;\le\; \frac{2(G^2+\sigma^2)}{\mu}\cdot\frac{1+\log(k+1)}{k+1}. $

**End of proof.**

The whole skeleton, in one line: unroll, then tame the iterates, then the strong floor, which gives a contraction, then the schedule, which gives $a_0 = 0$, then the harmonic sum, which gives the logarithm, then Jensen, which gives $\bar x_k$. Note that the logarithm enters at exactly one place: the rents shrink like $1/i$, and $\sum 1/i$ grows like $\log$.

### 7.6 The bound against the truth

The running example is again exactly solvable, and unusually informative here because Lemma 7.2 identifies the iterate as a sample mean. With $\mu=1$ and $G^2+\sigma^2 = 15$, the bound is $30\,\frac{1+\log(k+1)}{k+1}$. The truth:

- **Last iterate.** $x_k$ is the mean of $k$ independent draws from $\{0,3,6\}$, so $\mathbb{E}[F(x_k)-F^\star] = \tfrac12\operatorname{Var}(x_k) = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{6}{k} = \frac{3}{k}$.
- **Averaged iterate.** Writing $x_i - 3 = S_i/i$ with $S_i = \sum_{j<i}(c_{i_j}-3)$ and using $\mathbb{E}[S_iS_l] = 6\min(i,l)$, one gets $\sum_{i,l\ge1}\frac{6\min(i,l)}{il} = 6\sum_{i,l}\frac{1}{\max(i,l)} = 6(2k-H_k)$, and with the $x_0 = 0$ term contributing $(-3)^2$,

$ \mathbb{E}\big[F(\bar x_k)-F^\star\big] \;=\; \frac{9 + 12k - 6H_k}{2(k+1)^2} \;\approx\; \frac{6}{k}. $

The comparison, each row given as: $k$, the Theorem 7.1 bound, the exact value for the averaged $\bar x_k$, the exact value for the last iterate $x_k$, and the Theorem 5.1 bound on the same problem.

- $k = 99$: bound $1.68$; averaged $0.0583$; last $0.0303$; Theorem 5.1 bound $1.16$.
- $k = 999$: bound $0.237$; averaged $0.0060$; last $0.0030$; Theorem 5.1 bound $0.367$.
- $k = 9999$: bound $0.031$; averaged $0.0006$; last $0.0003$; Theorem 5.1 bound $0.116$.

The last column is Theorem 5.1 applied to the *rewritten* problem, where $G = 3$ honestly, so the constant is $\sqrt{15}\cdot 3 = 11.62$ rather than the $19.44$ of Section 5.5: the same objective, but a legitimate global Lipschitz constant for $f$ once the quadratic part is peeled off. This is why the two comparisons carry different numbers for what looks like the same problem.

Two readings. First, **the averaged iterate is genuinely worse than the last** here, by an asymptotic factor of exactly $2$, namely $6/k$ against $3/k$, because averaging drags in the early, high-variance iterates and the deterministic $x_0 = 0$. This is a concrete instance of the honest remark that theory averages while practice keeps the last iterate: practice is not being sloppy, it is exploiting something the proof technique cannot see. Second, **the last iterate here attains the statistical limit**: $3/k = \sigma^2/(2\mu k)$, which is the best any estimator of the mean from $k$ samples can do. SGD with this schedule is not merely converging; it is the sample mean, and no algorithm can beat it on this problem.

### 7.7 Removing the log, and the crossover

The lecture states, without proof, that the optimal rate for this problem class is $O(1/k)$ and that sharper averaging removes the logarithm. Precisely:

- **Weighted averaging.** Replacing the uniform average by weights proportional to $i+1$, that is $\bar x_k = \frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}\sum_{i=0}^k (i+1)x_i$, removes the logarithm and gives $\mathbb{E}[F(\bar x_k)-F^\star] \le \frac{2(G^2+\sigma^2)}{\mu(k+1)}$. This is Lacoste-Julien, Schmidt and Bach [6], and the proof is the same weighted telescope with a different weight sequence.
- **Suffix averaging.** Averaging only the last half of the iterates also removes the logarithm; Rakhlin, Shamir and Sridharan [7].
- **Last iterate.** For the last iterate the logarithm is *not* removable: Shamir and Zhang [8] prove an $O(\log k / k)$ upper bound and Harvey, Liaw, Plan and Randhawa [9] prove a matching $\Omega(\log k/k)$ lower bound for nonsmooth strongly convex SGD. So the honest scoreboard, that this proof is one logarithm away from optimal, is exactly right for the averaged iterate and exactly wrong to try to fix for the last one.
- **Lower bound.** No algorithm can beat $\Omega\big(\sigma^2/(\mu k)\big)$ for this class [10], and Section 7.6 exhibits a problem where SGD attains it.

**Crossover with Theorem 5.1.** The comparisons show Theorem 2's constant winning at $k=99$ and Theorem 4's rate winning by $k = 999$. Solving $30\frac{1+\log(k+1)}{k+1} = \frac{11.62}{\sqrt{k+1}}$ numerically gives $k+1 \approx 300$, so the exact crossover is $k \approx 299$; saying "near $10^3$" is a statement about the resolution of the table, not the true switch point. In general the crossover sits where $\frac{2(G^2+\sigma^2)}{\mu}\frac{\log k}{k} \approx \frac{\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}D}{\sqrt{k}}$, that is at $k \approx \frac{(G^2+\sigma^2)\log^2 k}{\mu^2D^2}$: later when the problem is badly conditioned, meaning $\mu$ small, which is the familiar warning that asymptotically better rates can be irrelevant at realistic horizons.

## 8 Beyond the Four Theorems

The lecture's closing sections name-drop step-size schedules, momentum, RMSprop and Adam, and the Robbins-Monro line, deliberately without math. This section supplies the statements behind the names, at the level a reader of these notes can now verify.

### 8.1 Robbins-Monro: which schedules work at all

Sections 4 and 7 exhibited two schedules: constant, which converges to a neighbourhood, and $1/(\mu(k+1))$, which converges. The classical characterization of which decaying schedules work predates all of the above by four decades.

**The Robbins-Monro conditions [3].** A step-size sequence $\alpha_k > 0$ is admissible if

$ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_k = \infty \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_k^2 < \infty. $

The first says the steps must be able to travel an unbounded distance; otherwise $\sum_k\alpha_k\lVert g_k\rVert \le G'\sum_k\alpha_k < \infty$ bounds the total movement and the iterates simply cannot reach $x^\star$ from a far enough start. The second says the accumulated noise, whose contribution to the squared error enters as $\alpha_k^2$ (Section 3.4), must be summable so the variance does not blow up.

The canonical family is $\alpha_k$ proportional to $k^{-p}$ with $p \in (\tfrac12, 1]$: $\sum k^{-p} = \infty$ requires $p \le 1$, and $\sum k^{-2p} < \infty$ requires $p > \tfrac12$. Note where this lecture's own schedules land. A constant $\alpha_k \equiv \alpha$ fails the second condition, hence the noise floor of Section 4. The schedule $\alpha_k$ proportional to $1/\sqrt{k+1}$ from Section 5.7 is exactly on the excluded boundary $p = \tfrac12$, which is why it earned only an $O(\log K/\sqrt{K})$ *average*-iterate rate and not almost-sure convergence. And $\alpha_k = 1/(\mu(k+1))$ is $p = 1$: admissible, at the aggressive end of the window.

Robbins and Monro's theorem [3], strengthened to the modern supermartingale form by Robbins and Siegmund [23], delivers something the four theorems of this lecture never do: **almost-sure** convergence, $x_k \to x^\star$ with probability one, rather than convergence of an expectation. The gap matters. The statement $\mathbb{E}[F(\bar x_k)-F^\star]\to0$ leaves open, for instance, that a vanishing fraction of runs diverges; the almost-sure statement does not. The tool is the Robbins-Siegmund lemma: if $V_{k+1} \le (1+a_k)V_k - b_k + c_k$ with $V_k, a_k, b_k, c_k \ge 0$ adapted, $\sum a_k < \infty$ and $\sum c_k < \infty$, then $V_k$ converges almost surely and $\sum b_k < \infty$. Applied with $V_k = \lVert x_k-x^\star\rVert^2$, inequality (7.3) is already in this shape, with $c_k = \alpha_k^2$ times a constant, summable exactly under the second Robbins-Monro condition.

### 8.2 Polyak-Ruppert averaging

Section 7.6 showed averaging *hurting*. That is an artifact of averaging from step $0$ under an aggressive schedule. The Polyak-Juditsky theorem [17] shows that the opposite combination, a *slow* schedule $\alpha_k$ proportional to $k^{-p}$ with $p \in (\tfrac12,1)$ plus averaging, is asymptotically optimal: $\sqrt{k}(\bar x_k - x^\star)$ converges in distribution to a Gaussian whose covariance matches the Cramer-Rao and maximum-likelihood limit, that is the same covariance a second-order method with exact Hessian information would achieve. The slogan: *take sloppy long steps, then average away the sloppiness*. The averaging that looked wasteful in Section 7.6 is, with the right schedule, how a first-order method recovers second-order efficiency for free.

### 8.3 Variance reduction: escaping the $1/\sqrt{K}$ floor

The lower bound of Section 5.8, $\Omega(\sqrt{G^2+\sigma^2}\,D/\sqrt{K})$, applies to algorithms that see only unbiased gradient samples. A finite sum is more than that: the same $N$ functions recur, so past gradients remain *relevant*, not merely historical. Exploiting this breaks the lower bound; it does not contradict it, because the algorithm is outside the class.

- **SVRG** [12] keeps a snapshot $\tilde x$ with its full gradient $\nabla\mathcal{L}(\tilde x)$, recomputed every few epochs, and uses the estimator $g_k = \nabla\mathcal{L}_{i_k}(x_k) - \nabla\mathcal{L}_{i_k}(\tilde x) + \nabla\mathcal{L}(\tilde x)$. It is unbiased by the same one-line argument as Theorem 2.2, since the two extra terms have equal conditional means, and its variance tends to $0$ as $x_k$ and $\tilde x$ approach $x^\star$, because the two sampled gradients are then evaluated at nearby points.
- **SAGA** [13] does the same with a stored table of the most recent gradient of each $\mathcal{L}_i$, trading $O(N)$ memory for the periodic full-gradient pass.

For smooth strongly convex finite sums both achieve *linear* convergence: accuracy $\varepsilon$ in $O\big((N+\kappa)\log\frac1\varepsilon\big)$ gradient evaluations, against SGD's $O(\sigma^2/(\mu\varepsilon))$ and full gradient descent's $O(N\kappa\log\frac1\varepsilon)$. This is the honest counterpoint to Section 1.1: SGD's $N$-independence is not the last word on finite sums. In deep learning these methods are nonetheless rarely used, because $N$ is enormous, snapshots are expensive, the objective is nonconvex, and, per Section 3.3, large models often sit close to interpolation, where $\sigma_\star^2 \approx 0$ makes plain SGD already fast [18].

### 8.4 The descendants: momentum, RMSprop, Adam

The lecture names these three and stops. What each adds to $x_{k+1}=x_k-\alpha g_k$:

- **Momentum** replaces the step by an exponential moving average of past gradients, $m_k = \beta m_{k-1} + g_k$ and $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha m_k$. On a quadratic this damps oscillation across the high-curvature directions and accumulates progress along the low-curvature ones, the same disease opt03 Section 7 diagnosed as ill-conditioning. In the deterministic smooth convex case Nesterov's variant provably improves $O(1/k)$ to $O(1/k^2)$ [5]; in the stochastic case the improvement is to the transient, not to the $\sigma^2$-dominated asymptotic term, which the lower bound of Section 5.8 forbids improving.
- **RMSprop** tracks a running average of squared gradient coordinates, $v_k = \beta_2 v_{k-1} + (1-\beta_2)g_k^{\odot2}$, and divides coordinatewise: $x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\, g_k / (\sqrt{v_k}+\epsilon)$. This is a diagonal preconditioner, a cheap surrogate for the Hessian scaling of opt03, giving each coordinate its own effective step size.
- **Adam** [14] is both at once, plus bias correction for the fact that $m_0=v_0=0$ biases the early averages toward zero. It is the default optimizer for deep learning.

Two honest caveats worth knowing. Adam's original convergence proof is *wrong*: Reddi, Kale and Kumar [15] exhibit a simple convex problem on which Adam does not converge, and propose AMSGrad. And weight decay as usually implemented in Adam is not $\ell_2$ regularization: adding $\lambda\lVert x\rVert^2$ to the loss interacts with the coordinatewise rescaling, so the effective penalty is not the intended one. AdamW [16] decouples them, and is what modern training actually uses. Both facts illustrate the lecture's own closing point: the guarantees are a compass, not a warranty.

### 8.5 Nonconvex SGD: what survives

The lecture's ledger lists "convex loss" against "deep nets: nonconvex" and leaves it there. Something does survive, provided the objective is $L$-smooth, which the lecture never assumed but deep-learning theory generally does.

**Theorem (Ghadimi and Lan [11], informal).** For $L$-smooth, possibly nonconvex $\mathcal{L}$ bounded below, with $\mathbb{E}_k[g_k]=\nabla\mathcal{L}(x_k)$ and $\operatorname{Var}_k(g_k)\le\sigma^2$, SGD with $\alpha$ of order $\min\{1/L, \varepsilon^2/(L\sigma^2)\}$ satisfies $\min_{k<K} \mathbb{E}\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}(x_k)\rVert^2 \le \varepsilon^2$ after

$ K = O\!\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^4} + \frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}\right) \ \text{iterations.} $

Read the statement carefully, because every weakening is deliberate. The target is $\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}\rVert$, not $\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}^\star$: without convexity, a small gradient does not certify a small gap, so the guarantee is **stationarity, not optimality**. It is the *minimum* over iterates, so one cannot say *which* iterate is good, only that one of them is. And the rate is $\varepsilon^{-4}$, not $\varepsilon^{-2}$, a real degradation, and known to be unimprovable in this class. It is a much weaker guarantee than Theorems 5.1 or 7.1, and it is nonetheless the guarantee under which essentially all of modern deep learning operates.

## 9 What the Lecture Leaves Out

A short inventory, so that the boundary of the four theorems is explicit.

- **No smoothness anywhere.** Uniquely among the optimization lectures, all four theorems assume only convexity plus a Lipschitz and variance budget. That is what makes them apply to hinge loss and ReLU networks, and also what caps the rates at $1/\sqrt{K}$ and $\log k/k$. Adding $L$-smoothness gives strictly better constants and enables the interpolation and nonconvex results of Sections 3.3 and 8.5.
- **High-probability bounds.** Everything here bounds a mean. Markov's inequality converts a mean bound into a $1/\delta$-dependence on the confidence, which is weak; genuine $\log(1/\delta)$ bounds require sub-Gaussian noise and martingale concentration, namely prob05's Azuma-Hoeffding inequality applied to $\sum\alpha_k\langle n_k, x_k-x^\star\rangle$.
- **Last-iterate guarantees.** Both convergence theorems bound $\bar x$; practice reports $x_K$. Sections 5.3 and 7.7 give the state of the art, $O(\log k/k)$ for the strongly convex last iterate, with a matching lower bound [9].
- **Without-replacement sampling.** Every proof uses independent draws; every training loop shuffles. Section 2.4 and [19] cover the gap, which is favourable in practice and analytically harder.
- **Generalization.** The four theorems bound the *empirical* risk $\mathcal{L}$ over the given $N$ points. Whether small $\mathcal{L}$ implies small population risk is prob06's question, entirely separate; Section 5.8 notes the consequence, that driving $\varepsilon$ far below the $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ statistical error is wasted computation.
- **Constrained and composite problems.** Projected SGD (Section 5.6) is stated but not analysed; proximal SGD, which handles a nonsmooth regularizer such as $\ell_1$ exactly rather than by subgradient, is standard [1, Section 6].
- **Adaptivity to unknown parameters.** Theorem 5.1's tuned step size needs $D$, $G$, $\sigma$ and $K$; Theorem 7.1 needs $\mu$. Parameter-free and AdaGrad-style methods attain nearly the same rates while estimating these on the fly.

Read as a whole, this lecture's arc is a single accounting identity applied four times: one step buys $2\alpha(\mathcal{L}(\theta_k)-\mathcal{L}^\star)$ of progress and pays $\alpha^2\mathbb{E}\lVert g_k\rVert^2$ in noise. Theorem 1 makes the purchase honest; Theorem 2 balances the two sides once; Theorem 3 lowers the price; Theorem 4 rebalances at every step. Every knob on a training run, batch size, learning-rate schedule, warmup, averaging, is a move in that one ledger.
