Randomized Response

A 1965 survey trick with a privacy dial.

Asking an Embarrassing Question

Recall (reconstruction). Accurate aggregates leak the dataset; protection needs deliberate randomness.

Asked directly, respondents lie or refuse.

→

→
report $Y_i$
→
curator

Every answer is deniable: "my coin flipped it."

Warner, "Randomized Response: A Survey Technique for Eliminating Evasive Answer Bias", JASA 1965.

Randomized Response — the Mechanism

Each of $n$ users holds a bit $D_i \in \{0,1\}$ and reports

$$Y_i \;=\; \begin{cases} D_i & \text{w.p. } \tfrac12 + \gamma \\ 1 - D_i & \text{w.p. } \tfrac12 - \gamma \end{cases} \qquad 0 \lt \gamma \le \tfrac12.$$
$D_i$ w.p. $\tfrac12+\gamma$ (truth) w.p. $\tfrac12-\gamma$ (flip) $Y_i = D_i$ $Y_i = 1 - D_i$

The Privacy Dial $\gamma$

$\gamma = 0$ uniform coin: perfect privacy $0 \lt \gamma \lt \tfrac12$ $\gamma = \tfrac12$ truth released: no privacy
  • $\gamma = \tfrac12$: $Y_i = D_i$ always, full utility, zero privacy.
  • $\gamma = 0$: $Y_i$ is a fair coin, zero utility, perfect privacy.
One parameter prices privacy against accuracy.

What the Curator Receives

The report is a Bernoulli tilted by the true bit:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[Y_i] &= D_i\bigl(\tfrac12+\gamma\bigr) + (1-D_i)\bigl(\tfrac12-\gamma\bigr) \\[4pt] &= 2\gamma D_i + \tfrac12 - \gamma. \end{aligned}$$
  • Affine in $D_i$ with slope $2\gamma$: biased, but invertibly so.
  • Any $\gamma \gt 0$ leaves a recoverable signal.

Debiasing — Solve for the Bit

Invert the affine map $\mathbb{E}[Y_i] = 2\gamma D_i + \tfrac12 - \gamma$; check unbiasedness directly:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat D_i \;&=\; \frac{Y_i - \tfrac12 + \gamma}{2\gamma}, \\[4pt] \mathbb{E}[\hat D_i] \;&=\; \frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_i] - \tfrac12 + \gamma}{2\gamma} \;=\; \frac{2\gamma D_i}{2\gamma} \;=\; D_i. \end{aligned}$$
Trick: bias removal. Undo the known tilt first; noise remains, bias does not.

Estimating the Fraction $p$

Target: the population fraction. Average the debiased bits:

$$p \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n D_i, \qquad \hat p \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \hat D_i \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{Y_i - \tfrac12 + \gamma}{2\gamma}.$$

Unbiased by linearity of expectation:

$$\mathbb{E}[\hat p] \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[\hat D_i] \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n D_i \;=\; p.$$

Variance of $\hat p$

Users randomize independently, so variances add:

$$\mathrm{Var}[\hat p] \;=\; \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}[\hat D_i] \;=\; \frac{1}{4 n^2 \gamma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}[Y_i] \;\le\; \frac{1}{4 n^2 \gamma^2}\cdot\frac{n}{4} \;=\; \frac{1}{16 n \gamma^2}.$$
Trick: Bernoulli variance. $\mathrm{Var}[Y_i] = P(1-P) \le \tfrac14$, whatever $D_i$ is.

Standard deviation $\le 1/(4\gamma\sqrt n)$: shrinks with $n$, blows up as $\gamma \to 0$.

Recall — Chebyshev's Inequality

Lemma (Chebyshev).

If $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X) = \sigma^2$, then for all $k \gt 0$, $\;\Pr\bigl[|X - \mu| \gt k\sigma\bigr] \le \dfrac{1}{k^2}$.

Apply to $\hat p$ with $\mu = p$ and $\sigma \le 1/(4\gamma\sqrt n)$:

$$\Pr\!\left[|\hat p - p| \gt \frac{k}{4\gamma\sqrt n}\right] \;\le\; \frac{1}{k^2}.$$

Sample Complexity — the $(\alpha, \beta)$ Guarantee

Claim.

$\Pr[|\hat p - p| \gt \alpha] \le \beta$ holds once $\;n = \dfrac{1}{16\,\gamma^2 \beta\, \alpha^2}$.

Proof: pick $k = 1/\sqrt{\beta}$, then force the deviation under $\alpha$:

$$\frac{k}{4\gamma\sqrt n} \;=\; \frac{1}{4\gamma\sqrt{\beta n}} \;\le\; \alpha \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n \;\ge\; \frac{1}{16\,\gamma^2 \beta\,\alpha^2}. \;\blacksquare$$
Trick: two moves. Debias, bound the variance; Chebyshev does the rest.

Pricing the Three Knobs

Accuracy $\alpha \downarrow$

Tighter band around $p$ costs $n \propto 1/\alpha^2$.

Confidence $\beta \downarrow$

Rarer failures cost $n \propto 1/\beta$.

Privacy $\gamma \downarrow$

More flipping costs $n \propto 1/\gamma^2$.

$\gamma = 0.1$, $\alpha = \beta = 0.05$:  $n = \dfrac{1}{16 \cdot 0.01 \cdot 0.05 \cdot 0.0025} = 50{,}000$ respondents.

Definition (replace neighbors).

$D = (X_1, \dots, X_i, \dots, X_n)$ and $D' = (X_1, \dots, X_i', \dots, X_n)$ for some $i$.

Variant: add/delete one record; replace = delete + insert, equivalent up to a factor.

$\varepsilon$-Differential Privacy

Definition ($\varepsilon$-DP).

A randomized $\mathcal{M}: \mathcal{X}^n \to \mathcal{Y}$ is $\varepsilon$-DP if for all neighbors $D, D'$ and events $O \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$,

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in O] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\, \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in O].$$

Changing one person's data barely changes the distribution of outputs.

Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, Smith, "Calibrating Noise to Sensitivity in Private Data Analysis", TCC 2006. Test-of-time TCC 2016; Gödel Prize 2017.

Reading the Definition

  • All neighbors: protects every individual, including outliers; no "average-case" escape.
  • All events $O$: rules out every test an adversary could run.
  • Multiplicative: small probabilities stay small in ratio, not just in difference.

Swapping the roles of $D$ and $D'$ gives the two-sided form:

$$e^{-\varepsilon} \;\le\; \frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in O]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in O]} \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}.$$

Smaller $\varepsilon$ means stronger privacy; $\varepsilon = 0$ means the output ignores the change.

Why Randomization Is Necessary

Example (deterministic mean).

Take $f(D) = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i$ and release $\mathcal{M}(D) = f(D)$ exactly. Then

$$\mathcal{M}(D') \;=\; f(D) + \frac{X_i' - X_i}{n},$$

so the output pins down $X_i' - X_i$ and identifies which dataset produced it.

Deterministic outputs have disjoint supports: some $O$ makes the ratio infinite. DP forces randomization.

How Strong Is $e^{\varepsilon}$?

$e^{\varepsilon} \approx 1 + \varepsilon$ only while $\varepsilon$ is small:

$\varepsilon$$e^{\varepsilon}$Reading
$0.1$$1.105$output probabilities within $\approx 11\%$
$1$$2.72$within a factor of $e$: still meaningful
$10$$22{,}026$guarantee essentially vacuous

Deployed systems typically choose $\varepsilon$ between $0.1$ and $10$.

The Adversary as Hypothesis Tester

Adversary tests $H_0$: data $= D$, vs. $H_1$: data $= D'$; declare $H_1$ iff output $\in O$.

Errors: $q = \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in O]$ (false positive), $p = \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \notin O]$ (false negative).

$$\begin{aligned} 1 - p \;=\; \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in O] \;&\le\; e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in O] \;=\; e^{\varepsilon} q, \\[4pt] 1 - q \;=\; \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in O^{c}] \;&\le\; e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in O^{c}] \;=\; e^{\varepsilon} p. \end{aligned}$$
$1 \le p + e^{\varepsilon} q$ and $1 \le q + e^{\varepsilon} p$: both errors cannot be small.
Wasserman & Zhou, "A Statistical Framework for Differential Privacy", JASA 2010.

The Forbidden Corner

$q$ (false positive) $p$ $1$ $1$ forbidden achievable tests $p = 1 - e^{\varepsilon} q$ $p = e^{-\varepsilon}(1-q)$
  • Perfect test $(q,p) = (0,0)$ excluded.
  • Every test sits above both lines.
  • $\varepsilon \to 0$: dashed line, $p + q \ge 1$, blind guessing.

Aside — False Positive vs False Negative

Example (Covid test).

Patient temperature: $T \sim \mathrm{Lap}(39, b)$ if sick, $T \sim \mathrm{Lap}(38, b)$ if healthy.

Best rule: declare sick when $T \gt 38.5$.

  • FP: healthy person flagged as sick.
  • FN: sick patient declared healthy.

Moving the threshold shrinks one error and inflates the other.

DP forces exactly this dilemma on every adversary, for every rule $O$.

RR Is Differentially Private

Theorem (Warner 1965; DP reading).

Randomized response $\mathcal{M}(D) = (Y_1, \dots, Y_n)$ is $\varepsilon_\gamma$-DP with

$$\varepsilon_\gamma \;=\; \ln\frac{\tfrac12+\gamma}{\tfrac12-\gamma}.$$

Small $\gamma$: $\varepsilon_\gamma = 4\gamma + O(\gamma^3)$, the familiar "$4\gamma$-DP" reading.

  1. Ratio for one output vector $y$: unchanged users cancel.
  2. Worst case over the two possible bits at index $i$.
  3. Sum the pointwise bound over any event $O$.

Proof — Only One Coordinate Differs

Fix an output $y = (y_1, \dots, y_n)$. Users flip independent coins, so probabilities factor:

$$\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) = y]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') = y]} \;=\; \frac{\prod_{j=1}^n \Pr[Y_j = y_j \mid D_j]}{\prod_{j=1}^n \Pr[Y_j = y_j \mid D_j']} \;=\; \frac{\Pr[Y_i = y_i \mid D_i]}{\Pr[Y_i = y_i \mid D_i']}.$$
Trick: cancellation. Users $j \ne i$ contribute identical factors; the ratio lives on user $i$ alone.

Proof (continued) — Worst Case over the Two Bits

Neighbors differ at $i$, so $D_i' = 1 - D_i$. Two cases for the observed $y_i$:

Case$\Pr[Y_i = y_i \mid D_i]$$\Pr[Y_i = y_i \mid D_i']$Ratio
$y_i = D_i$$\tfrac12+\gamma$$\tfrac12-\gamma$$\dfrac{1/2+\gamma}{1/2-\gamma}$
$y_i = 1 - D_i$$\tfrac12-\gamma$$\tfrac12+\gamma$$\dfrac{1/2-\gamma}{1/2+\gamma} \le 1$
$$\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) = y]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') = y]} \;\le\; \frac{\tfrac12+\gamma}{\tfrac12-\gamma} \;=\; e^{\varepsilon_\gamma}, \qquad \text{with equality when } y_i = D_i.$$

Proof (continued) — From Points to Events

DP quantifies over events $O$, not single outputs. Sum the pointwise bound:

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in O] \;&=\; \sum_{y \in O} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) = y] \;\le\; \sum_{y \in O} e^{\varepsilon_\gamma} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') = y] \\[2pt] &=\; e^{\varepsilon_\gamma} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in O]. \;\blacksquare \end{aligned}$$
Trick: pointwise to setwise. A uniform ratio bound survives summation (and integration).

Exercise: where would the argument break if the per-$y$ bound held only for most $y$?

Discussion — How Private Is RR?

  • $\gamma \to 0$: $\varepsilon_\gamma \to 0$, but sample cost grows as $1/\gamma^2$.
  • Each user's own report is private: no trusted curator needed (local DP).

Laplace Mechanism

Noise calibrated to sensitivity.

$\ell_1$ Sensitivity

How much can one person move the query answer?

Definition ($\ell_1$ sensitivity).

For $f : \mathcal{X}^n \to \mathbb{R}^k$,

$$\Delta_1 \;=\; \max_{D,\, D' \text{ neighbors}} \|f(D) - f(D')\|_1.$$
  • Property of $f$ alone: computed before seeing any data.
  • Large $\Delta_1$: one record has large influence, so hiding it costs more noise.

Worked Examples — Counting and Mean

Counting query.

$f(D) = |\{i : X_i \text{ has the property}\}|$.

Changing one record moves the count by at most $1$:

$$\Delta_1 = 1.$$

Mean query.

$f(D) = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i$ with $X_i \in [a,b]$:

$$|f(D) - f(D')| = \frac{|X_i' - X_i|}{n} \le \frac{b-a}{n}.$$

Counting stays $1$ at any $n$; the mean's sensitivity shrinks like $1/n$.

Recall — the Laplace Distribution

$$\mathrm{Lap}(\mu, b): \quad f_Y(y) \;=\; \frac{1}{2b}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{|y-\mu|}{b}\Bigr).$$
$\mu$ tails $\propto e^{-|y-\mu|/b}$
  • Mean $\mu$, variance $2b^2$; sharper peak, heavier tails than a Gaussian.
  • Tail: $\Pr[|Y - \mu| \gt t b] = e^{-t}$ for $t \ge 0$.

The Laplace Mechanism

Theorem (Dwork–McSherry–Nissim–Smith 2006).

For $f$ with $\ell_1$ sensitivity $\Delta_1$, release

$$\mathcal{M}(D) \;=\; f(D) + (Y_1, \dots, Y_k), \qquad Y_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Lap}\bigl(0,\, \Delta_1/\varepsilon\bigr).$$

Then $\mathcal{M}$ is $\varepsilon$-DP.

Noise scale $b = \Delta_1/\varepsilon$: exactly enough to hide any single-record change.
Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, Smith, "Calibrating Noise to Sensitivity in Private Data Analysis", TCC 2006.

Picture — Two Shifted Densities

$f(D)$ $f(D')$ $\le \Delta_1$ $z$ height ratio $\le e^{\varepsilon}$

Scale $b = \Delta_1/\varepsilon$ makes the two output densities close everywhere.

Proof Setup — the Density Ratio Target

Independent coordinates give a product density, $b = \Delta_1/\varepsilon$:

$$f_{\mathcal{M}(D)}(z) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^k \frac{1}{2b}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{|z_i - f(D)_i|}{b}\Bigr), \qquad z \in \mathbb{R}^k.$$
  1. Bound the ratio $f_{\mathcal{M}(D)}(z) / f_{\mathcal{M}(D')}(z)$ by $e^{\varepsilon}$ for every $z$.
  2. Integrate the bound over any event $T$.

Proof — Triangle Inequality Kills $z$

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{f_{\mathcal{M}(D)}(z)}{f_{\mathcal{M}(D')}(z)} &= \prod_{i=1}^k \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{|z_i - f(D')_i| - |z_i - f(D)_i|}{b}\Bigr) \\[4pt] &\le\; \prod_{i=1}^k \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{|f(D)_i - f(D')_i|}{b}\Bigr) \;=\; \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{\|f(D) - f(D')\|_1}{b}\Bigr). \end{aligned}$$
Trick: triangle inequality. $|z - a'| - |z - a| \le |a - a'|$: the observation $z$ drops out entirely.

Proof (continued) — Calibrate, Then Integrate

Sensitivity plus the calibration $b = \Delta_1/\varepsilon$ close the chain:

$$\frac{f_{\mathcal{M}(D)}(z)}{f_{\mathcal{M}(D')}(z)} \;\overset{\text{triangle}}{\le}\; e^{\|f(D)-f(D')\|_1/b} \;\overset{\text{sensitivity}}{\le}\; e^{\Delta_1/b} \;\overset{b = \Delta_1/\varepsilon}{=}\; e^{\varepsilon}.$$

For any event $T \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$:

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in T] = \int_T f_{\mathcal{M}(D)}(z)\, dz \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon} \int_T f_{\mathcal{M}(D')}(z)\, dz = e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in T]. \;\blacksquare$$

Accuracy — How Loud Is the Noise?

Per coordinate: variance $2\Delta_1^2/\varepsilon^2$, and the Laplace tail gives

$$\Pr\!\Bigl[\,|\mathcal{M}(D)_i - f(D)_i| \gt \frac{\Delta_1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\beta}\Bigr] \;=\; \beta.$$
Counting query, $\Delta_1 = 1$, $\varepsilon = 1$: with probability $99\%$ the error is $\le \ln 100 \approx 4.6$ counts, at any dataset size $n$.

More sensitivity or less $\varepsilon$: linearly louder noise.

Discussion — Central Noise Beats Local

Same target, the fraction $p$ with $X_i \in \{0,1\}$:

Who is trustednobody: users randomizecurator sees raw data

04

Exponential Mechanism

Private selection by reweighting.

Selection Problems

Not every query is numeric; often we must pick an option.

Voting.

$d$ candidates; user $i$ approves ${X_i \subseteq \{1, \dots, d\}}$.

$$q(j; D) = |\{i : j \in X_i\}|.$$

Pricing.

User $i$ buys iff price $p \le X_i$; revenue

$$q(p; D) = p \cdot |\{i : p \le X_i\}|.$$

Non-private answer: $\operatorname*{arg\,max}_y q(y; D)$. How do we privatize an argmax?

Why Noising the Output Fails

Pricing with $X_1 = \dots = X_n = 7$: optimal price $p^\star = 7$, revenue $7n$.

revenue $q(p) = p \cdot n$ $p^\star = 7$ noisy $\hat p \gt 7$: revenue $0$ price $p$
Utility is discontinuous in the output. Fix: discretize to $\{1, \dots, p_{\max}\}$ and select privately.

The Exponential Mechanism

Definition (McSherry–Talwar 2007).

Utility $q : \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{X}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ with sensitivity $\Delta = \max_y \max_{D \sim D'} |q(y; D) - q(y; D')|$. Output $y \in \mathcal{Y}$ with

$$\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{EM}}(D) = y] \;=\; \frac{\exp\bigl(\frac{\varepsilon\, q(y; D)}{2\Delta}\bigr)}{\sum_{y' \in \mathcal{Y}} \exp\bigl(\frac{\varepsilon\, q(y'; D)}{2\Delta}\bigr)}.$$

Higher utility: exponentially more likely. Never outputs anything "wrong", only suboptimal.

McSherry & Talwar, "Mechanism Design via Differential Privacy", FOCS 2007.

EM Is $\varepsilon$-DP — Proof Plan

Theorem.

$\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{EM}}$ is $\varepsilon$-DP.

The output probability is a fraction; both parts move when $D$ changes:

$$\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{EM}}(D) = y]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{EM}}(D') = y]} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\exp\bigl(\frac{\varepsilon q(y;D)}{2\Delta}\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(\frac{\varepsilon q(y;D')}{2\Delta}\bigr)}}_{\text{score factor}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{\sum_{y'}\exp\bigl(\frac{\varepsilon q(y';D')}{2\Delta}\bigr)}{\sum_{y'}\exp\bigl(\frac{\varepsilon q(y';D)}{2\Delta}\bigr)}}_{\text{normalizer factor}}.$$

Plan: each factor pays at most $e^{\varepsilon/2}$.

Proof — the Score Factor Pays $e^{\varepsilon/2}$

Sensitivity bounds every single utility move: $q(y; D) \le q(y; D') + \Delta$. Hence

$$\frac{\exp\bigl(\frac{\varepsilon\, q(y;D)}{2\Delta}\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(\frac{\varepsilon\, q(y;D')}{2\Delta}\bigr)} \;=\; \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon\,(q(y;D) - q(y;D'))}{2\Delta}\Bigr) \;\le\; \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon \Delta}{2\Delta}\Bigr) \;=\; e^{\varepsilon/2}.$$

One record moves each score by $\le \Delta$; the $2\Delta$ exponent halves the cost.

Proof (continued) — the Normalizer Pays $e^{\varepsilon/2}$

Apply the per-term bound inside the sum, then multiply the two factors:

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{y'} \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon\, q(y';D')}{2\Delta}\Bigr) \;&\le\; e^{\varepsilon/2} \sum_{y'} \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon\, q(y';D)}{2\Delta}\Bigr), \\[6pt] \frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{EM}}(D) = y]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{EM}}(D') = y]} \;&\le\; e^{\varepsilon/2} \cdot e^{\varepsilon/2} \;=\; e^{\varepsilon}. \;\blacksquare \end{aligned}$$
Trick: factor of 2. Score and normalizer each spend half the budget; hence the $2\Delta$.

EM Utility — High Probability

Theorem.

Let $q_{\max} = \max_y q(y; D)$, $d = |\mathcal{Y}|$, and $Y = \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{EM}}(D)$. For $t \ge 0$,

$$\Pr\!\Bigl[q(Y; D) \lt q_{\max} - \frac{2\Delta(\log d + t)}{\varepsilon}\Bigr] \;\le\; e^{-t}.$$

Reading: the utility gap exceeds $\tfrac{2\Delta}{\varepsilon}(\log d + t)$ with probability at most $e^{-t}$.

  1. Collect the bad outputs into a set $B_t$.
  2. Compare each bad output against the best one.
  3. Count: $|B_t| \le d$.

Proof — Bad Outputs Lose to the Best

Bad set: $B_t = \bigl\{y : q(y; D) \lt q_{\max} - \tfrac{2\Delta(\log d + t)}{\varepsilon}\bigr\}$; let $y^\star$ attain $q_{\max}$.

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr[Y \in B_t] \;\le\; \frac{\Pr[Y \in B_t]}{\Pr[Y = y^\star]} &= \sum_{y \in B_t} \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon\,(q(y;D) - q_{\max})}{2\Delta}\Bigr) \\[4pt] &\lt\; |B_t|\, e^{-(\log d + t)} \;\le\; d \cdot \frac{e^{-t}}{d} \;=\; e^{-t}. \;\blacksquare \end{aligned}$$
Trick: benchmark against $y^\star$. Dividing by $\Pr[Y = y^\star] \le 1$ cancels the unknown normalizer.

EM Utility — Expectation

Theorem.

$\mathbb{E}\bigl[q_{\max} - q(Y; D)\bigr] \;\le\; \dfrac{2\Delta}{\varepsilon}\,(\log d + 1)$.

Proof: set $Z = \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2\Delta}(q_{\max} - q(Y; D)) \ge 0$; the tail bound reads $\Pr[Z \gt \log d + t] \le e^{-t}$:

$$\mathbb{E}[Z] \;=\; \int_0^\infty \Pr[Z \gt z]\, dz \;\le\; \log d + \int_0^\infty \Pr[Z \gt \log d + t]\, dt \;\le\; \log d + 1. \;\blacksquare$$
Trick: integrate the tail. Spend $\log d$ on the flat part, $\int_0^\infty e^{-t} dt = 1$ on the rest.

Numbers — Voting with 100 Candidates

  • Sensitivity $\Delta = 1$: one voter moves one count by one.
  • $d = 100$, $\varepsilon = 0.5$, target failure $e^{-t} = 0.01$, so $t = \ln 100 \approx 4.6$.
$$\frac{2\Delta}{\varepsilon}(\log d + t) \;=\; 4 \times (4.6 + 4.6) \;\approx\; 36.8, \qquad \Pr\bigl[q_{\max} - q(Y;D) \gt 36.8\bigr] \le 0.01.$$
If the winner leads the runner-up by more than $36.8$ votes, the true winner is announced with probability $\ge 99\%$.
05

Noisy Max

Private selection by noisy scores.

Noisy Max — the Algorithm

  1. For each candidate $y \in \mathcal{Y}$, draw $Z_y \sim \mathrm{Exp}(2\Delta/\varepsilon)$, independently.
  2. Form the noisy score $s_y = q(y; D) + Z_y$.
  3. Output $\;y^{\mathrm{out}} = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} s_y$.

Same goal as EM, different randomness: perturb scores, then take a plain argmax.

$\mathrm{Exp}(b)$: density $\tfrac1b e^{-z/b}$ on $z \ge 0$, tail $\Pr[Z \ge K] = e^{-K/b}$.

Noisy Max Is $\varepsilon$-DP — Proof Strategy

Theorem.

$\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{NM}}$ with $Z_y \sim \mathrm{Exp}(2\Delta/\varepsilon)$ is $\varepsilon$-DP.

  1. Fix an output $y$; condition on all other noises $Z_{-y} = z_{-y}$.
  2. The event "output is $y$" becomes a one-dimensional threshold for $Z_y$.
  3. Swapping $D \to D'$ moves the threshold by at most $2\Delta$.
  4. The exponential tail absorbs a $2\Delta$ shift at cost $e^{\varepsilon}$.
  5. Integrate the conditioning back out.

Intuition — Freeze the Other Noises

With $z_{-y}$ frozen, $y$ wins iff its own noise clears one number $K$:

$K - 2\Delta$ $K$ $\Pr[Z_y \ge K]$ a $2\Delta$ shift adds mass: factor $\le e^{\varepsilon}$
Trick: condition and compare. The other noises are identical under $D$ and $D'$; only the threshold moves.

Proof — Condition on the Other Noises

Fix $y$ and condition on $Z_{-y} = z_{-y}$. Then $y$ wins exactly when

$$q(y; D) + Z_y \;\ge\; q(j; D) + z_j \quad \forall j \ne y,$$

which is a single threshold event for $Z_y$:

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{NM}}(D) = y \mid Z_{-y} = z_{-y}] \;&=\; \Pr\bigl[Z_y \ge K(D)\bigr], \\[4pt] K(D) \;&=\; \max_{j \ne y}\, \bigl(q(j; D) - q(y; D) + z_j\bigr). \end{aligned}$$

Ties happen with probability zero: the noises are continuous.

Proof (continued) — the Threshold Moves by at Most $2\Delta$

Compare thresholds term by term: for every $j$,

$$q(j; D) - q(y; D) \;\ge\; \bigl(q(j; D') - \Delta\bigr) - \bigl(q(y; D') + \Delta\bigr) \;=\; q(j; D') - q(y; D') - 2\Delta.$$

The max over $j$ preserves it:

$$K(D) \;\ge\; K(D') - 2\Delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Pr[Z_y \ge K(D)] \;\le\; \Pr\bigl[Z_y \ge K(D') - 2\Delta\bigr].$$
Trick: both scores move. Each utility moves by $\le \Delta$, opposite directions at worst.

Proof (continued) — the Exponential Tail Pays $e^{\varepsilon}$

Claim: for $Z_y \sim \mathrm{Exp}(b)$ with $b = 2\Delta/\varepsilon$ and any $K$,

$$\Pr[Z_y \ge K - 2\Delta] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\, \Pr[Z_y \ge K].$$
$$\begin{aligned} K \ge 2\Delta:&\quad \frac{\Pr[Z_y \ge K - 2\Delta]}{\Pr[Z_y \ge K]} = \frac{e^{-(K-2\Delta)/b}}{e^{-K/b}} = e^{2\Delta/b} = e^{\varepsilon} \quad \text{(exact)}, \\[4pt] K \lt 2\Delta:&\quad \Pr[Z_y \ge K - 2\Delta] \le 1 = e^{\varepsilon} e^{-\varepsilon} \le e^{\varepsilon} e^{-K/b} = e^{\varepsilon}\, \Pr[Z_y \ge K]. \end{aligned}$$

Memorylessness in disguise: shifting an exponential tail rescales it by a constant.

Proof (continued) — Integrate Out the Conditioning

The other noises have the same, data-independent density $f(z_{-y})$ under $D$ and $D'$:

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{NM}}(D) = y] \;&=\; \int \Pr[Z_y \ge K(D)]\, f(z_{-y})\, dz_{-y} \\[2pt] &\le\; e^{\varepsilon} \!\int\! \Pr[Z_y \ge K(D')]\, f(z_{-y})\, dz_{-y} \;=\; e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{NM}}(D') = y]. \;\blacksquare \end{aligned}$$

Recap of the chain (events $O$ follow by summing over $y \in O$, as for RR):

$$\Pr[Z_y \ge K(D)] \;\overset{\text{shift} \le 2\Delta}{\le}\; \Pr[Z_y \ge K(D') - 2\Delta] \;\overset{\text{tail}}{\le}\; e^{\varepsilon}\, \Pr[Z_y \ge K(D')].$$

Noisy Max Utility — Statement and Key Step

Theorem.

With $Y = \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{NM}}(D)$ and $c = \tfrac{2\Delta(\log d + t)}{\varepsilon}$:   $\Pr\bigl[q_{\max} - q(Y; D) \gt c\bigr] \le e^{-t}$.

Key step: $Y$ won the noisy comparison against the true best $y^\star$:

$$q(Y; D) + Z_Y \;\ge\; q(y^\star; D) + Z_{y^\star} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q_{\max} - q(Y; D) \;\le\; Z_Y - Z_{y^\star}.$$

A large utility gap forces a large noise gap.

Proof (continued) — Union Bound over Candidates

If the gap exceeds $c$, some candidate's noise does too, since $Z_{y^\star} \ge 0$:

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr[q_{\max} - q(Y; D) \gt c] \;&\le\; \Pr[\exists j :\; Z_j - Z_{y^\star} \gt c] \;\le\; \sum_{j=1}^d \Pr[Z_j \gt c] \\[4pt] &= d\, e^{-\varepsilon c / 2\Delta} \;=\; d\, e^{-(\log d + t)} \;=\; e^{-t}. \;\blacksquare \end{aligned}$$
Trick: union bound over bad outcomes. The $d$ candidates cost a $\log d$ term, nothing more.
