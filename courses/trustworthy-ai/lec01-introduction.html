Bias in the Courtroom

  • COMPAS: predicts who will re-offend
  • Used by US judges for bail, sentencing
  • Higher false-alarm rate for Black defendants
  • Labeled "high risk", then never re-offended

A score nobody could see inside or question

Angwin, Larson, Mattu, and Kirchner, "Machine Bias", ProPublica 2016.

A Tool That Learned to Discriminate

  • Amazon built a résumé-ranking model
  • Trained on a decade of past hires
  • Past hires: mostly men
  • Model penalized the word "women's"
  • Scrapped in 2018

Biased data in, biased decisions out

The Lawyer Who Trusted ChatGPT

  • 2023 case: Mata v. Avianca
  • Lawyer asked ChatGPT for precedent
  • Got six confident case citations
  • All six were fabricated
  • Court sanctioned the lawyer

Fluent, authoritative, and simply wrong

A Chatbot That Made Promises

Air Canada, 2024

Bot invented a bereavement refund. A tribunal held the airline liable.

Car dealership, 2023

Bot talked into agreeing a "$1 car, no takesies-backsies."

When the assistant can act, its mistakes bind you

Models That Memorize People

  • 2021: real names, phones, addresses pulled from GPT-2
  • 2023: "repeat the word poem forever"
  • ChatGPT spilled chunks of training data
  • Some of it was personal

The model remembers more than it should

Carlini et al., "Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models", USENIX Security 2021.

Cannot trust that a voice or face is real

How AI Fails Differently

Learned systems break in new ways

Software vs Learned Models

Classical software

Bug = a line of code. Reproducible. Patchable. Someone wrote the rule.

Learned models

Behavior sits in the weights. Probabilistic. No single line to fix.

Nobody wrote the rule the model follows

Failure Lives in the Weights

  • Billions of numbers
  • No comments, no variable names
  • No stack trace when it goes wrong
  • Cannot just open and read the code

The behavior is learned, not written

A Tiny Push Fools It

Add noise invisible to you: "panda" 58% → "gibbon" 99%.

Goodfellow, Shlens, and Szegedy, "Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples", ICLR 2015 — Figure 1.

Three Sources of Failure

Data

Biased, private, or poisoned inputs

Training

Objective misses what we meant

Deployment

New inputs, real adversaries

A failure can enter at any stage

Bigger Models, New Surprises

  • Scale brings new, unplanned abilities
  • Capabilities appear without warning
  • So do failure modes
  • Hard to predict before deployment

We discover behavior after we ship

03

Threat-Model Thinking

The one habit that unifies the course

What Is a Threat Model?

  • Who is the adversary, and their goal?
  • What do they know about the system?
  • What can they actually do?

Answer these before "is it safe?"

An Everyday Analogy

  • Same front-door lock on your house
  • Casual passerby: more than enough
  • Determined burglar: maybe not
  • Locksmith you hired: opens it anyway

Same lock, different threat, different verdict

Who Is the Adversary?

Curious user

Pokes at limits for fun

Fraudster

Wants money or identity

Competitor

Wants to steal the model

Troll

Wants to abuse or harm

Careless insider

No malice, still leaks

Regulator

Audits for compliance

What They Know

White-box

Sees weights and gradients. Open models, insiders, audits.

Black-box

Sees only the API: inputs and outputs.

Gray-box: partial knowledge, somewhere between

When They Strike

Train-time

Poison the data before the model ships.

Inference-time

Manipulate inputs after deployment.

Timing changes what defense is possible

The Attack Map

Two axes, knowledge × timing, locate almost any attack.

Worked Example: A Medical LLM

  • Privacy: whose patient records trained it?
  • Memorization: will it recite a real note?
  • Reliability: will it invent a diagnosis?
  • Jailbreak: can safety be bypassed?

One system, four very different threats

Placing Them on the Map

Train-timeInference-time
White-boxPrivacy of training setMemorization probe
Black-box—Hallucination, jailbreak

The same system spans several threat models

No Threat Model, No Answer

"Is it secure?" means nothing until you fix who, what, and when.
  • Security is always relative to a threat
  • State it first, then evaluate

The Trust Stack & Course Map

15 weeks, four modules

The Trust Stack

Each failure lives at one layer. The course climbs the stack.

Four Modules, 15 Weeks

Each topic: definition → history → 2025–26 frontier

Module 1 — Privacy & Data

  • Differential privacy: a budget for leakage
  • Membership inference: "were you in the data?"
  • Memorization & extraction: GPT-2 leak
  • Unlearning: making a model forget

Module 2 — Reliability

  • Hallucination: confident fake citations
  • Calibration: does 90% mean 90%?
  • Interpretability: opening the black box

From "it sounds right" to "we can check"

Module 3 — Security

  • Adversarial examples: panda → gibbon
  • Data poisoning: corrupt before training
  • Jailbreaks: bypass the safety filter
  • Prompt injection: hijack the assistant

Module 4 — Provenance & Fairness

  • Watermarking: marking AI output
  • Deepfakes: detecting the fake
  • Fairness: definitions and mitigation
  • Governance: rules and accountability
