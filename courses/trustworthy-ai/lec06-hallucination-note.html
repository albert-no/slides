What Is a Hallucination

Message: A hallucination is output that reads fluently and confidently but is not grounded in facts or in the provided source: the grammar is perfect, the content is invented, and nothing in the text signals the difference.

Reading the slide. Follow the diagram left to right: the prompt “cite a paper” goes into the model, which predicts the next token and returns fluent output with perfect grammar. The dashed box for facts or a source sits off that path, and the red × marks that no grounding link connects it to the output. The slide cites the Ji et al. survey (linked below).

Script

A hallucination is output that reads fluently and confidently but is not grounded in facts or in the provided source. The grammar is perfect; the content is invented.

Definitions

Hallucination (working formalization). Fix a prompt $c$ and let $R_c$ be the set of possible responses, partitioned into a set of valid responses $V_c$ and a disjoint set of errors $E_c$ (Kalai, Nachum, Vempala & Zhang 2025 use exactly this valid/error partition). A model is a distribution $\hat p(\cdot \mid c)$ over responses, and its hallucination (error) rate on $c$ is $$\mathrm{err}(c) \;=\; \Pr_{r \sim \hat p(\cdot\mid c)}\bigl[r \in E_c\bigr].$$ What makes an error a hallucination rather than a mere mistake is that $r$ is fluent and plausible — it has high probability under the model even though its content is false or unsupported. The NLG survey of Ji et al. frames the phenomenon the same way: modern generation systems are "prone to hallucinate unintended text" that is fluent but not grounded in the input or in the world.

  • Kalai, Nachum, Vempala & Zhang, "Why Language Models Hallucinate" (2025) — the valid/error formalization used throughout this lecture's theory entries.

Fluency Fools Us

Message: Readers take confident, well-formatted prose as true, but style is not evidence: a real and an invented citation look identical on the page, and only a lookup tells them apart.

Reading the slide. The slide is a test. Two citations in identical format: Vaswani et al. Script

We instinctively read confident, well-formatted prose as true prose. The signal we trust is style, not verified truth.

Definitions

Fluency vs. truth (formalization: course notes). Fluency is an internal quantity: text $x$ is fluent when it has high likelihood under the language model itself, i.e. low perplexity $\exp\bigl(-\tfrac1T\sum_t \log \hat p(x_t \mid x_{<t})\bigr)$. Truth is an external predicate: whether the claims in $x$ hold in the world (or follow from a given source). Nothing in the definition of likelihood mentions the external predicate, so the two can disagree arbitrarily — a maximally fluent string can be entirely false. The reader's heuristic "fluent $\Rightarrow$ true" is exactly the gap a hallucinating model exploits.

Two Flavors of Wrong

Message: Wrong comes in two flavors: a factuality error contradicts the world; a faithfulness error contradicts the provided source, for example a summary asserting something the document never said. The two are checked against different references.

Reading the slide. The output sits in the middle with two “contradicts?” checks: one against the world (factuality), one against the given source (faithfulness). The two checks are independent: an output can pass one and fail the other.

Script

A factuality error contradicts the world. A faithfulness error contradicts the provided source, for example a summary asserting something the document never said.

Definitions

Factuality vs. faithfulness (formalization: course notes). Let $y$ be a generated output, decomposed into atomic claims $a_1,\dots,a_k$, let $W$ denote world knowledge, and let $d$ be a provided source document (when one exists).

  • $y$ is factual iff every claim $a_i$ is true in $W$.
  • $y$ is faithful (to $d$) iff every claim $a_i$ is entailed by $d$: $d \models a_i$ for all $i$.

The two predicates are independent, giving four quadrants: a summary can be faithful but unfactual (the source itself is wrong), or factual but unfaithful (true in the world, never said in the document — still a failure for summarization). Faithfulness is decidable from $(d, y)$ alone via entailment, which is why it is the easier property to audit automatically; factuality requires an external reference. This distinction returns in the RAG entries: retrieval converts a factuality problem into a faithfulness problem with respect to the retrieved evidence.

Not the Same as a Bug

Message: Hallucination is not a bug: a plausible falsehood is the model working as designed, predicting likely text rather than checking truth. Under the conditions of the next section’s theorems some errors are even forced, so no code patch removes them without changing the objective or its incentives.

Reading the slide. Two columns. Left, a bug: an ⚠ Error with a stack trace, visible and fixable, a defect in the code. Right, a hallucination: a fluent claim (“The study found a 23% increase...”) with no error signal, the model working as designed, a property of the objective.

Script

A bug is a fixable defect with a crash or error. Hallucination is structural: the model is working as designed, predicting likely text rather than checking truth.

Definitions

Bug vs. structural behavior (course notes). A bug is a deviation between a program's behavior and its specification. A language model's specification is its training objective: minimize cross-entropy against the corpus distribution. A model that emits a plausible falsehood is meeting that specification — the falsehood can even be required by it, as the theorems in the next section make precise (a well-calibrated model provably must hallucinate at a rate tied to the rare-fact structure of its training data). So "hallucination" names a property of the objective's optimum, not an implementation error, and no patch to the code removes it without changing the objective or the incentives.

02 — Why Models Hallucinate

Script

The Training Objective

Message: A language model is trained to predict the next token from the words so far, so it ranks continuations by likelihood; nothing in that objective checks whether the continuation is true.

Reading the slide. The formula Pr[next word ∣ words so far] is the whole objective. The bar chart applies it to the prefix “The 2019 paper by Smith ...”: candidate next tokens ranked by probability (et 0.41, and 0.22, “,” 0.15, showed 0.09, (2019) 0.07). The caption makes the point: ranked by likelihood; nothing here checks the fact.

Script

A language model is trained to predict the next token given the words so far. It maximizes likely text, not true text.

Definitions

Autoregressive language modeling (derivation: course notes). A language model defines a distribution over token sequences by the chain rule, $$p_\theta(x_{1:T}) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta\bigl(x_t \mid x_{<t}\bigr),$$ and training minimizes the cross-entropy to the data distribution $p_{\mathrm{data}}$: $$\mathcal L(\theta) \;=\; -\,\mathbb E_{x \sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})\right] \;=\; H(p_{\mathrm{data}}) + \mathrm{KL}\bigl(p_{\mathrm{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta\bigr).$$ Since $H(p_{\mathrm{data}})$ is a constant, the unique population minimizer is $p_\theta = p_{\mathrm{data}}$: the objective's ideal is to reproduce the corpus distribution, truths and falsehoods alike. No term in $\mathcal L$ references an external truth predicate, so truthfulness enters only insofar as the corpus happens to be truthful.

No Truth Grounding

Message: Nothing in the loss demands a factual output. Kalai et al. (2025) show that generating valid text is at least as hard as classifying text as valid or error, so where that classification fails, errors are forced, up to the theorem’s correction terms.

Reading the slide. The title states the reduction. The figure is Kalai et al. (2025), Fig. 1: the Is-It-Valid reduction, valid outputs (+) and errors (−) in three settings. Spelling has a clean boundary; counting has a boundary that a poor model misses; birthdays have no pattern at all, only memorization. A hallucination is a classification error on a pattern that may not exist. The formal statement for that reduction is in the notes for slide S19; this entry gives the earlier Kalai–Vempala theorem.

Script

Nothing in the loss demands that an output be factual. The corpus mixes truth and fiction, so the model learns the shape of answers, and a plausible shape can be false.

Definitions

The Kalai–Vempala setting (arXiv 2311.14648, STOC 2024). Documents $x$ carry factoids $y = f(x)$; the world picks a true distribution $D_L$ over documents, inducing a factoid distribution $p := f \circ D_L$. The facts are $F := \mathrm{supp}(p) \cup \{\perp\}$ and the hallucinations are $H := Y \setminus F$ — plausible factoids with zero true probability. The learner sees $n$ training documents (observed factoids $O \subseteq F$), outputs a model $D_{LM}$ with factoid distribution $g := f \circ D_{LM}$, and its hallucination rate is $g(H)$. The monofact estimate is $$\widehat{MF} \;:=\; \frac{\#\{\text{factoids appearing exactly once in training}\}}{n},$$ the classical Good–Turing estimate of the unseen mass $p(U)$, $U := Y\setminus O$. The model $g$ is calibrated to $p$ if $g = p^{\Pi}$ for some partition $\Pi$ (each factoid's probability equals the average true probability of its probability-bucket); $\mathrm{Mis}_b(g,p)$ is the total-variation distance to the nearest such coarsening over $b$ buckets, so $\mathrm{Mis}_b = 0$ means perfectly calibrated. $D_{\text{world}}$ is $s$-sparse if $|F| \le e^{-s}|H|$ (facts are rare among plausible factoids) and regular if unobserved factoids are a priori exchangeable.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (Kalai–Vempala, Corollary 1). For any $\delta > 0$, any number of buckets $b$, any $n$, any $s$-sparse regular world distribution, and any learning algorithm, with probability at least $1-\delta$ over the true distribution and the $n$ training documents: $$g(H) \;\ge\; \widehat{MF} \;-\; \mathrm{Mis}_b(g,p) \;-\; \frac{3e^{-s}}{\delta} \;-\; \sqrt{\frac{6\ln(6/\delta)}{n}}.$$ Informally (the paper's 99%-confidence version): hallucination rate $\gtrsim$ monofact rate $-$ miscalibration $- \;300\,|F|/|H| \; - \;7/\sqrt n$. A model that stays calibrated must hallucinate at least at the rate at which facts appear only once in its training data.

Proof sketch (verified against the paper; full proof in arXiv 2311.14648, Section 3 and appendix). Three steps.

  1. Calibration forces mass onto unseen factoids. The paper's Theorem 1 bounds $\mathbb E\bigl[(p(U) - \|p^{\Pi}-g\|_{TV} - g(H))_+\bigr]$ by $\max_{y\in U}\Pr[y \in F] + |O|\cdot\max_{y\in U}\mathbb E[p(y)]$: if $g$ is close to a coarsening of $p$, it cannot avoid placing roughly $p(U)$ probability on factoids never seen in training, because calibration ties its bucket averages to $p$'s.
  2. Almost all of that mass is hallucination. By $s$-sparsity and regularity, an unobserved factoid is a fact with probability at most $\approx e^{-s}$ — so up to the small additive terms, the unseen mass $g$ is forced to spend lands on $H$, i.e. contributes to $g(H)$.
  3. The unseen mass is estimable. Good–Turing concentration (McAllester–Schapire 2000; McAllester–Ortiz 2003; Berend–Kontorovich 2013) gives $|\widehat{MF} - p(U)| = \tilde O(1/\sqrt n)$ with high probability. Combining the three via Markov's inequality yields the stated bound. $\blacksquare$

The contrapositive is the slide's message run in reverse: a model that hallucinates less than its monofact rate must be miscalibrated — the paper notes this matches practice, where post-training reduces hallucinations precisely by sacrificing calibration.

Message: If no model in a family can tell valid from invalid outputs, generation must err. Proved case: two equally likely prompts ending in “out of”, two options, no abstention; every trigram model is then wrong at least half the time. V3’s miscounts are a hint, not a proof.

Reading the slide. Two prompts, “She lost it and was completely out of” (should end “her mind”) and “He lost it and was completely out of” (“his mind”), share their last two words, so a trigram model makes the same prediction for both. Under the stipulated task, two options, no abstention, both prompts equally likely, at least one prompt is answered wrongly, so the error is at least one half. Script

The paper's key reduction: producing only valid text is at least as hard as telling valid text from errors. So if no model in the family can tell them apart, generation must make errors. The cleanest example is a trigram model, which predicts each word from the previous two only. "She lost it and was completely out of" should end with "her mind", "He lost it and was completely out of" with "his mind". Both end in "out of", so the model cannot tell them apart. In this stipulated two-option task, with no abstention and both prompts equally likely, every trigram model is wrong at least half the time. V3's miscounts on the previous slide are only a hint: one model failing does not show that its whole family must fail.

Definitions

(Kalai et al. 2025, §3.3.2.) For each prompt $c$, $\mathcal V_c$ and $\mathcal E_c$ are the valid and error responses. $\mathrm{err} := \hat p(\mathcal E) = \sum_{(c,r)\in\mathcal E}\mu(c)\,\hat p(r\mid c)$ is the probability the model generates an error response, with prompts $c \sim \mu$. The distribution $D$: with probability $1/2$ draw $x = (c,r) \sim p$ (a valid training dialogue), and with probability $1/2$ draw $c \sim \mu$ and $r$ uniformly from $\mathcal E_c$; the target is $f(x) = +$ iff $x$ is valid. The term $\delta$: $\delta := |\hat p(A) - p(A)|$ for $A := \{(c,r) : \hat p(r\mid c) > 1/\min_c|\mathcal E_c|\}$, the gap between how much mass the model and the training distribution put on responses the model rates above that threshold (a calibration-type quantity, small for a calibrated base model). The classifier family thresholds a language model's probability: $g_{\theta,t}(c,r) = +$ if $\hat p_\theta(r\mid c) > t$, else $-$, over all $\theta \in \Theta$, $t \in [0,1]$. Then $\mathrm{opt}(\mathcal G) = \min_{g\in\mathcal G}\Pr_{x\sim D}[g(x)\ne f(x)]$ is the best error of any thresholded model in the family.

Theorem & proof

Statement (the paper's §3.3.2, following from its Theorem 1): $$\mathrm{err} \;\ge\; 2\,\mathrm{opt}(\mathcal G) \;-\; \frac{\max_c |\mathcal V_c|}{\min_c |\mathcal E_c|} \;-\; \delta.$$ Proof in the paper (Theorem 1 reduces generation to the Is-It-Valid classification problem). The bound is conditional. It forces errors only when $2\,\mathrm{opt}(\mathcal G) > \max_c|\mathcal V_c|/\min_c|\mathcal E_c| + \delta$; a large $\mathrm{opt}(\mathcal G)$ alone is not enough, since the two correction terms are not automatically small. $\mathrm{opt}(\mathcal G)$ is a statement about every model in the family. The letter-counting example is the paper's motivation ("this suggests"), not a proved instance: one model's mistakes do not show the family's incapacity. The proved instance is the trigram example below.

The proved case: trigram models

(Kalai et al. 2025, §3.3.2.) Prompts $c_1, c_2$ as above, $r_1$ = "her mind", $r_2$ = "his mind", with $\mathcal V_{c_1} = \mathcal E_{c_2} = \{r_1\}$ and $\mathcal V_{c_2} = \mathcal E_{c_1} = \{r_2\}$. Theorem 3 (pure multiple-choice). If $|\mathcal V_c| = 1$ for all $c$ and $C = \min_c|\mathcal E_c| + 1$, then $\mathrm{err} \ge 2(1 - 1/C)\,\mathrm{opt}(\mathcal G)$; no calibration term is needed. Corollary 2. With $\mu$ uniform over $\{c_1, c_2\}$, any trigram model has generation error at least $1/2$: here $C = 2$, and $\mathrm{opt}(\mathcal G) = 1/2$ because a classifier built from a trigram model cannot distinguish $c_1$ from $c_2$. So $\mathrm{err} \ge 2 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac12$. Proofs: the paper's Section C.

Definitions

Why citations are the worst case (course notes, in the Kalai–Vempala vocabulary). Citations are a domain where the plausible set is astronomically larger than the fact set: syntactically valid (author, year, venue, title) strings vastly outnumber real papers, so the sparsity ratio $|F|/|H|$ is tiny — which strengthens the Kalai–Vempala lower bound stated in the notes for slide S14 rather than weakening it. Meanwhile each real citation is a long-tail item typically seen once or never in training, so the monofact rate $\widehat{MF}$ on this slice is high. High $\widehat{MF}$ plus extreme sparsity is exactly the regime where the theorem forces a calibrated model to emit citation-shaped strings with no referent.

Definitions

Binary graders. Kalai, Nachum, Vempala & Zhang (2025) model an evaluation as a grader $g_c : R_c \to \{0,1\}$ per prompt $c$, with abstentions ("I don't know") scored $0$ — the dominant format across mainstream benchmarks.

Theorem & proof

Claim (guessing dominates abstaining under binary grading). If the best guess available to the model succeeds with probability $q$, its expected score is $q \cdot 1 + (1-q)\cdot 0 = q$, while abstaining scores exactly $0$. Hence guessing is weakly optimal always, and strictly optimal whenever $q > 0$. (Derivation: course notes — one line, but it is the paper's central socio-technical point.) Kalai et al. argue this creates an "epidemic" of penalized uncertainty: models are optimized to be good test-takers, and a good test-taker under 0/1 scoring never leaves a blank. Their proposed mitigation is not a new benchmark but changed scoring of existing ones — see the "Teaching I Don't Know" and "A Scoring Rule" entries (slides S61–S62).

An Exam-Taking Analogy

Script

Definitions

The Is-It-Valid (IIV) reduction (Kalai–Nachum–Vempala–Zhang 2025). Responses split into valid $V$ and errors $E$; the model's error rate is $\mathrm{err} := \hat p(E)$, and the training distribution $p$ satisfies $p(E) = 0$. Define the IIV classification problem: draw $x$ from the mixture $D(x) = \tfrac12 p(x)$ for $x \in V$ and $D(x) = \tfrac{1}{2|E|}$ for $x \in E$; the target label is $+$ iff $x \in V$. From any language model $\hat p$ build the plug-in classifier $\hat f(x) = +$ iff $\hat p(x) > 1/|E|$, with misclassification rate $\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{iiv}} := \Pr_{x\sim D}[\hat f(x) \neq f(x)]$.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (KNVZ Corollary 1). $$\mathrm{err} \;\ge\; 2\,\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{iiv}} \;-\; \frac{|V|}{|E|} \;-\; \delta, \qquad \delta := \bigl|\hat p(A) - p(A)\bigr|,\; A := \{x : \hat p(x) > 1/|E|\},$$ where $\delta$ is a one-set calibration gap (the paper shows it is small for any model near a local optimum of cross-entropy, since $\delta = |\tfrac{d}{ds}\mathcal L(\hat p_s)|_{s=1}$ for a one-parameter scaling family). The paper's Theorem 1 is the prompted version: $\mathrm{err} \ge 2\,\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{iiv}} - \max_c |V_c| / \min_c |E_c| - \delta$.

Proof sketch (verified against Appendix A of the paper). Partition the response space by the threshold: $A$ (mass above $1/|E|$) and $B$ (at or below). Write $\mathrm{err} = \hat p(A\setminus V) + \hat p(B \setminus V)$. Under $D$, errors are uniform on $E$ with total mass $\tfrac12$, so misclassified errors in $A$ contribute $|A \cap E|/(2|E|)$ to $\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{iiv}}$, while misclassified valid responses in $B$ contribute $\tfrac12 p(B \cap V)$. Bounding each piece of $\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{iiv}}$ by the corresponding piece of $\mathrm{err}$ — using $\hat p(x) > 1/|E|$ on $A$ and $\hat p(x) \le 1/|E|$ on $B$, and trading $p$ for $\hat p$ at cost $\delta$ on the set $A$ — yields $\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{iiv}} \le \tfrac12(\mathrm{err} + |V|/|E| + \delta)$, which rearranges to the claim. $\blacksquare$

Reading. Generating valid text is at least as hard as recognizing valid text: any statistical reason a binary classifier must err (an epistemically hard fact, a bad model family, garbage in training) forces the generator to err at up to twice the rate. The Kalai–Vempala 2024 result becomes a special case of this reduction. The constant 2 is shown to be essentially tight.

Definitions

Singleton rate (KNVZ 2025, Def. 2). Among $N$ training prompt–response pairs, a prompt is a singleton if it appears exactly once (with a substantive answer, not an abstention). The singleton rate is $sr = |S|/N$ — the supervised-learning twin of the Kalai–Vempala monofact rate.

Confidence as a Number

Message: A model can attach a probability to each answer, say 0.70 that it is correct; that number is useful for deciding when to trust the answer only if it is itself trustworthy, and calibration is the test.

Reading the slide. A gauge with its needle at 0.70, and the question “can we trust the needle?”. The next slides answer it.

Script

Definitions

Confidence. For a classifier with softmax output, the prediction and confidence are $\hat Y = \arg\max_k \hat p(k \mid x)$ and $\hat P = \max_k \hat p(k\mid x)$ (Guo et al. 2017). For a language model, the analogues are the sequence probability of the sampled answer, a self-evaluated probability such as P(True), or a verbalized confidence ("I am 90% sure") — three different estimators of the same target, treated in the following entries.

The Calibration Promise

Message: A model is calibrated if, among all answers given 70% confidence, about 70% are correct. Calibration ties stated confidence to observed accuracy, making it a promise you can audit; on its own it says nothing about accuracy.

Reading the slide. 100 dots stand for 100 answers given at 70% confidence: 70 correct, 30 wrong. When the split matches the stated confidence, the score is calibrated at that level.

Script

A model is calibrated if, among all answers it gives 70% confidence, about 70% are actually correct. Confidence then becomes a promise you can audit.

Definitions

Perfect calibration (Guo et al. 2017, Eq. 1). With prediction $\hat Y$ and confidence $\hat P$, $$\mathbb P\bigl(\hat Y = Y \,\bigm|\, \hat P = p\bigr) \;=\; p, \qquad \forall\, p \in [0,1],$$ the probability taken over the joint distribution of $(X,Y)$. Two caveats stated in the paper: perfect calibration is impossible in any practical setting, and the conditional probability cannot be computed from finitely many samples with continuous $\hat P$ — every empirical statement about calibration is a binned approximation of this definition. Note calibration says nothing about accuracy: a weather model that always predicts the base rate is perfectly calibrated and useless; calibration must be paired with sharpness.

  • Guo, Pleiss, Sun & Weinberger, "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks" (ICML 2017) — the reference for Eq. (1), ECE, and temperature scaling.

Definitions

Construction (Guo et al. 2017). Partition $[0,1]$ into $M$ equal-width bins; let $B_m$ be the indices of samples whose confidence falls in bin $m$. Plot, per bin, $$\mathrm{acc}(B_m) = \frac{1}{|B_m|}\sum_{i\in B_m} \mathbf 1[\hat y_i = y_i], \qquad \mathrm{conf}(B_m) = \frac{1}{|B_m|}\sum_{i\in B_m} \hat p_i .$$ Perfect calibration means $\mathrm{acc}(B_m) = \mathrm{conf}(B_m)$ for all $m$ — every bar on the diagonal. Measuring the Gap

Message: Expected Calibration Error averages the gap between confidence and accuracy over confidence bins, weighted by bin size; zero is perfect calibration. It summarizes the whole reliability diagram in one number, and the number depends on the binning.

Reading the slide. Five bins. In each, the red box is the gap between confidence and accuracy; one is labeled “gap 0.22”. Confidence exceeds accuracy in every bin, so this model is overconfident. ECE adds up the gaps, each weighted by its bin’s share of answers.

Script

Expected Calibration Error averages, over confidence bins, the absolute gap between confidence and accuracy, weighted by bin size. Zero is perfect calibration. On the slide each red box is one bin's gap; here confidence exceeds accuracy in every bin, so the model is overconfident.

Definitions

Expected calibration error. The population quantity is $\mathbb E_{\hat P}\bigl[\,\bigl|\mathbb P(\hat Y = Y \mid \hat P = p) - p\bigr|\,\bigr]$ (Guo et al., Eq. 2); ECE approximates it with $M$ equal-width bins (Eq. 3): $$\mathrm{ECE} \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n}\,\bigl|\mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m)\bigr|,$$ where $n$ is the number of samples. Guo et al. use $M = 15$ in their experiments.

Binning caveats (course notes). ECE is an estimator, and the binning choices matter: (i) the value depends on $M$ — too few bins smooth real miscalibration away, too many leave bins nearly empty and noisy; (ii) with equal-width bins and an overconfident model, most mass piles into the top bins, leaving the rest poorly estimated (equal-mass/adaptive binning is the common fix); (iii) within a bin, per-sample gaps of opposite sign cancel before the absolute value is taken, so ECE can understate miscalibration; (iv) $\mathrm{ECE} = 0$ does not certify a useful model — the always-predict-the-base-rate model achieves it. Treat ECE as a summary to be read alongside the diagram, not a replacement for it.

Background & further reading

  • Guo, Pleiss, Sun & Weinberger, "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks" (ICML 2017), Eqs. 2–3.

Reading ECE

Reading the slide. The formula is on top. In the picture each bar is as wide as its bin share (10, 10, 15, 25, 40%) and as tall as its gap (0.03, 0.07, 0.15, 0.20, 0.22), so the total red area is ECE; the worked sum gives 0.1705. A course illustration, not real data.

Script

The slide's example (course illustration, not real data). A Cheap Fix: Temperature

Message: Temperature scaling divides the model’s scores by one number T, tuned on held-out data, before the softmax. It leaves the predicted class, and so accuracy, unchanged, and often restores calibration: on ResNet-110 / CIFAR-100, ECE falls from 12.67 to 0.96.

Reading the slide. Guo et al. (2017), Fig. 4: reliability diagrams for ResNet-110 on CIFAR-100, uncalibrated and after recalibration. ECE (lower is better): uncalibrated 12.67, temperature scaling 0.96, histogram binning 2.46, isotonic regression 4.16. The one-parameter method does best here.

Script

Temperature scaling divides the model's scores by a single number T before the softmax, tuned on held-out data. Larger T softens the confidence and often restores calibration.

Definitions

Temperature scaling (Guo et al. 2017, Eq. 9). With logit vector $\mathbf z_i$, replace the confidence by $$\hat q_i \;=\; \max_k \, \sigma_{\mathrm{SM}}(\mathbf z_i / T)^{(k)}, \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{SM}}(\mathbf z)^{(k)} = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}},$$ with a single scalar $T > 0$ fitted by minimizing NLL on a validation set. Properties (all from the paper): $T > 1$ "softens" the softmax and raises output entropy; $T \to \infty$ drives $\hat q_i \to 1/K$ (maximum uncertainty); $T = 1$ recovers the original model; $T \to 0$ collapses to a point mass. Because dividing by $T$ does not change the arg-max of the softmax, the predicted class is untouched: accuracy is exactly preserved, only confidence moves. It is a single-parameter variant of Platt scaling, and in their benchmarks it was "surprisingly effective," often beating far more expressive recalibration maps.

The two figures of merit are coverage, $\Pr[Y \in C(X)]$, and efficiency, $\mathbb E\,|C(X)|$ — a useless predictor achieves perfect coverage with $C(x) \equiv \mathcal Y$, so the goal is guaranteed coverage with small sets, and an adaptive $|C(x)|$ that grows exactly on the hard inputs. Conformal prediction (next entries) achieves the coverage half by construction. Marginal coverage. Two Assumptions, None on the Model

Message: Coverage needs two assumptions: calibration and test data are exchangeable, and the score was fixed without looking at the calibration data. Nothing is assumed about the model’s accuracy: a bad model keeps the coverage and only gets larger sets.

Script

Definitions

(A1) Exchangeability. Random variables $Z_1,\dots,Z_{n+1}$ are exchangeable if their joint distribution is invariant under every permutation $\pi$: $(Z_1,\dots,Z_{n+1}) \overset{d}{=} (Z_{\pi(1)},\dots,Z_{\pi(n+1)})$. I.i.d. sampling implies exchangeability; the converse fails, so this is a strictly weaker assumption.

(A2) Fixed score. $s$ is chosen independently of the calibration set, e.g. $s(x,y) = 1-\hat f(x)_y$ with $\hat f$ trained on separate data. Then $s_i = s(X_i,Y_i)$ inherit exchangeability from (A1). Fitting $s$ on the calibration data can break this.

How It Works

Message: Split conformal: score held-out calibration pairs, set the threshold at the k-th smallest score with $k = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$, and at test time keep every candidate scoring at or below it. A held-out quantile turns scores into a guaranteed set.

Reading the slide. The bullets give the recipe. The worked example has nine sorted calibration scores and α = 0.2, so $k = 8$ and the threshold is the 8th smallest score, $\hat q = s_{(8)}$. For $n = 1000$ and α = 0.1, $k = 901$, just above the 90th percentile; the +1 is what makes the finite-sample proof work.

Script

Hold out a calibration set with known answers, score how surprised the model is on each, set the threshold at the $k$-th smallest score with $k = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$ (just above the 90th percentile), and at test time keep every candidate below the threshold.

Definitions

Split-conformal recipe (Angelopoulos & Bates 2021; also the lec06 technical supplement).

  1. Choose a nonconformity score $s(x,y)$ — larger = model more surprised that $y$ is the answer to $x$. The canonical classification choice is $s(x,y) = 1 - \hat p(y \mid x)$.
  2. On $n$ held-out calibration pairs compute $s_1,\dots,s_n$ and set $\hat q$ to their $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n$ quantile (for $\alpha = 0.1$, $n = 1000$: $k = \lceil 900.9\rceil = 901$, the 901st smallest score, slightly above the 90th percentile; the $+1$ is what makes the finite-sample proof work). If $k = n+1$, which happens only when $\alpha < 1/(n+1)$, set $\hat q = \infty$.
  3. At test time output $C(x) = \{\,y : s(x,y) \le \hat q\,\}$.

The model quality only affects efficiency: a bad model still gets valid coverage, just with big sets. This decoupling — validity by construction, sharpness from the model — is the design principle of the whole framework.

Two Kinds of Calibration

Message: “Calibration” means two things. Temperature scaling and the conformal threshold both tune one knob on held-out data, but temperature aims at confidence matching accuracy, without guarantee, while the conformal threshold guarantees the truth is in the set at least 1 − α of the time, even if probabilities are miscalibrated.

Reading the slide. Held-out data (labeled, unused in training) feeds two branches. Fit a temperature T: probability calibration, target confidence matches accuracy. Pick a threshold: conformal calibration, target truth in the set at least 1 − α of the time. The bullets answer “is conformal calibration?”: yes, it calibrates a cutoff, not the probabilities; the targets differ; coverage holds even if the probabilities are miscalibrated.

Script

We have now seen "calibration" twice, so let us separate them. Temperature scaling and the conformal threshold share one idea: hold out labeled data the model never trained on, and use it to tune a single knob without touching the model. They differ in what the knob is aiming for. Temperature scaling aims to improve probability calibration, so that answers labelled 80% are right closer to 80% of the time; exact calibration is not guaranteed. The conformal threshold makes the prediction set contain the truth at least one minus alpha of the time. Coverage holds even if the probabilities themselves are miscalibrated; a badly calibrated model just gets bigger sets.

Background & further reading

Temperature scaling (Guo et al., ICML 2017, §4.2) fits one scalar $T$ by minimizing negative log-likelihood on a validation set; it leaves the argmax, and so accuracy, unchanged, and is judged by ECE. Split conformal (Angelopoulos & Bates 2021, §1, Theorem 1) picks one quantile $\hat q$ of calibration scores; it is judged by marginal coverage and set size. The two can be combined if the data are kept separate: tune the temperature on one held-out split, then, with the score now fixed, run conformal calibration on a different split. The coverage guarantee does not depend on how well the probabilities are calibrated.

The Coverage Theorem

Message: Under exchangeability and a fixed score, split conformal covers the truth with probability at least 1 − α, for every α and every finite n, with no assumption on the distribution or model. If the scores are also almost surely distinct, coverage is at most 1 − α + 1/(n+1).

Reading the slide. The theorem has two lines. The lower bound, $\ge 1-\alpha$, always holds under (A1) and (A2). The upper bound, $\le 1-\alpha+\frac{1}{n+1}$, needs the extra condition of no ties; it says the sets are not wastefully large. The probability is over calibration and test data jointly: marginal, not per input.

Script

Under the two assumptions, for every alpha and every n, coverage is at least one minus alpha. If the scores are also almost surely distinct (no ties), it is also at most one minus alpha plus one over n plus one.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (conformal coverage; Vovk, Gammerman & Saunders 1999; as stated in Angelopoulos & Bates 2021, Theorem 1 and Appendix D). Under (A1) and (A2), with $k = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$, $\hat q = s_{(k)}$ ($\hat q = \infty$ if $k = n+1$) and $C(x) = \{y : s(x,y) \le \hat q\}$: $$\Pr\bigl[\,Y_{n+1} \in C(X_{n+1})\,\bigr] \;\ge\; 1-\alpha \quad\text{always};$$ $$\Pr\bigl[\,Y_{n+1} \in C(X_{n+1})\,\bigr] \;\le\; 1-\alpha+\frac{1}{n+1} \quad\text{if the scores are almost surely distinct (Theorem D.2)}.$$ The probability is over calibration and test data jointly.

Proof (verified against Appendix D of Angelopoulos & Bates). Assume distinct scores and $k \le n$; sort $s_{(1)} < \cdots < s_{(n)}$. By construction $\{Y_{n+1} \in C(X_{n+1})\} = \{s_{n+1} \le s_{(k)}\}$, which is the event that $s_{n+1}$ ranks at most $k$ among all $n+1$ scores. By (A1) and (A2) the scores $(s_1,\dots,s_{n+1})$ are exchangeable, so this rank is uniform on $\{1,\dots,n+1\}$ and $$\Pr[\,Y_{n+1} \in C(X_{n+1})\,] = \frac{k}{n+1} \;\ge\; \frac{(n+1)(1-\alpha)}{n+1} = 1-\alpha.$$ Upper bound: $k < (n+1)(1-\alpha)+1$ gives $k/(n+1) < 1-\alpha+1/(n+1)$. Ties: with random tie-breaking, rank $\le k$ still implies $s_{n+1} \le s_{(k)}$, so coverage is at least $k/(n+1)$; only the upper bound can fail. Edge case: $k = n+1$ only when $\alpha < 1/(n+1)$; then $\hat q = \infty$, $C = \mathcal Y$, coverage 1. Why (A2): a score fit on calibration data can make $s_{n+1}$ distributed differently from $s_1,\dots,s_n$, and then the uniform-rank step can fail. $\blacksquare$

Definitions

Marginal vs conditional. Marginal: $\Pr[Y \in C(X)] \ge 1-\alpha$. Conditional: $\Pr[Y \in C(X) \mid X = x] \ge 1-\alpha$ for every $x$. The example on the slide: $\tfrac23(0.99) + \tfrac13(0.72) = 0.66 + 0.24 = 0.90$.

The Abstention Idea

Reading the slide. Two branches. A singleton set {A} leads to “candidate for a validated policy”; a large or empty set leads to “defer to a human”. The guarantee bounds how often the truth is missed overall, not how often a singleton is wrong.

Script

Definitions

Set-size deferral rule (course notes). Fix a budget $k$ and act as: treat $C(x)$'s single element as a candidate answer for a separately validated decision policy if $|C(x)| = 1$; present the shortlist if $1 < |C(x)| \le k$; defer to a human if $C(x)$ is empty or $|C(x)| > k$. The original sets retain marginal coverage, $\Pr[\,Y \notin C(X)\,] \le \alpha$; the selected-answer error rate requires separate validation. The set being large is itself informative — it certifies that the model cannot separate candidates at the chosen confidence level; an empty set signals that no label is plausible enough.

Singletons are not certified. Write $S$ for the event $|C(X)| = 1$ and $W$ for "the singleton is wrong", i.e. $S \wedge Y \notin C(X)$. The guarantee gives $\Pr[W] \le \Pr[Y \notin C(X)] \le \alpha$: the joint probability of being wrong and singleton. The error rate among singletons is $\Pr[\,Y \notin C(X) \mid S\,] = \Pr[W]/\Pr[S]$, which need not be $\le \alpha$; if singletons are rare, it can be much larger. Acting automatically on singletons therefore needs its own validation of that conditional error rate.

Definitions

Monotonicity (derivation: course notes). Semantic Entropy

Message: Semantic entropy groups sampled answers by meaning rather than wording, so “Paris” and “It’s Paris” count as one; many distinct meanings give high entropy, which flags likely hallucination. Paraphrases no longer raise a false alarm.

Reading the slide. The bullets give the method. The figure is Kuhn, Gal and Farquhar (2023), Table 1: ‘Paris’ and ‘It’s Paris’ collapse into one semantic cluster, so the semantic entropy falls from 0.94 to 0.33 while naive entropy is unchanged. Script

Farquhar and colleagues group sampled answers by meaning rather than wording, so "Paris" and "It's Paris" count as one. Many distinct meanings give high entropy, which flags hallucination.

Definitions

Semantic entropy (Farquhar, Kossen, Kuhn & Gal, Nature 2024). Three steps. (1) Sample $M$ answers $s^{(1)},\dots,s^{(M)}$ to the question $x$. (2) Cluster by meaning: put two answers in the same cluster iff each entails the other (bidirectional entailment, checked with an NLI model or an LLM judge) — this makes clusters equivalence classes of meaning. (3) Take entropy over clusters. The cluster probability aggregates sequence probabilities, $$P(c \mid x) \;=\; \sum_{s \in c} P(s \mid x) \;=\; \sum_{s\in c}\prod_i P\bigl(s_i \mid s_{<i}, x\bigr),$$ and the semantic entropy is $$\mathrm{SE}(x) \;=\; -\sum_{c} P(c \mid x)\,\log P(c \mid x).$$ The paper's estimator is Rao–Blackwellized Monte Carlo over the sampled clusters $C_1,\dots,C_{|C|}$ with normalized cluster masses $\bar P(C_i \mid x) = P(C_i\mid x)/\sum_j P(C_j \mid x)$ (normalization is needed because length-normalized sequence "probabilities" need not sum to one). The discrete variant replaces model probabilities with empirical cluster fractions, $\bar P(C_i \mid x) \approx \tfrac1M\sum_{m} \mathbf 1[\,s^{(m)} \in C_i\,]$, which converges to the same estimator by the law of large numbers and needs no token probabilities at all — usable on black-box APIs. High SE = samples scatter across meanings = likely confabulation (the paper's term for hallucinations that are arbitrary and incorrect).

The Entropy Picture

Message: If repeated samples collapse to one meaning, entropy is low and the answer is likely reliable; if they scatter across several meanings, entropy is high and the answer is suspect.

Reading the slide. Two panels. Left: the samples land in one meaning, low entropy. Right: they scatter across several meanings, high entropy. In practice the detector flags an answer when entropy exceeds a threshold tuned on held-out data.

Script

Definitions

Limiting cases (derivation: course notes). If all $M$ samples land in one cluster, $P(c\mid x) = 1$ and $\mathrm{SE} = 0$ — meaning is certain even if wordings differ, which is exactly the case naive token-level entropy over-penalizes. If the samples spread uniformly over $K$ clusters, $\mathrm{SE} = \log K$, the maximum for $K$ meanings. In general $0 \le \mathrm{SE} \le \log(\#\text{clusters})$, so the score is a graded meaning-diversity measure: the decision rule thresholds it, flagging $x$ whenever $\mathrm{SE}(x)$ exceeds a value tuned on held-out data.

Definitions

RAG (Lewis et al., NeurIPS 2020) combines a retriever $p_\eta(z \mid x)$ — Dense Passage Retrieval, a BERT bi-encoder over query and passage, searched with maximum inner-product search — with a generator $p_\theta$ (BART-large, 400M parameters), marginalizing the answer over the top-$k$ retrieved passages $z$. Two decoding models: $$p_{\text{RAG-Sequence}}(y \mid x) \;\approx\; \sum_{z \in \mathrm{top}\text{-}k} p_\eta(z \mid x)\; \prod_{i=1}^{N} p_\theta\bigl(y_i \mid x, z, y_{1:i-1}\bigr)$$ (one document explains the whole answer), and $$p_{\text{RAG-Token}}(y \mid x) \;\approx\; \prod_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{z \in \mathrm{top}\text{-}k} p_\eta(z \mid x)\, p_\theta\bigl(y_i \mid x, z, y_{1:i-1}\bigr)$$ (each token may draw on a different document). The document index acts as non-parametric memory alongside the model's parametric memory, and retriever and generator are fine-tuned jointly.

Background & further reading

  • Lewis et al., "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (NeurIPS 2020).

Why RAG Helps

Message: Tying the answer to a retrieved source supplies facts from after the training cutoff and a citation the user can verify, leaving less room to invent: grounding shrinks the space of fabrications, though slide S60 shows it does not remove it.

Script

Tying the answer to a retrieved source supplies fresh facts past the cutoff and a citation the user can verify, leaving less room to invent.

Definitions

Factuality reduced to faithfulness (formalization: course notes). Recall the two predicates from "Two Flavors of Wrong" (slide S09). Without retrieval, correctness of $y$ is a factuality question against the whole world — unauditable at generation time. With retrieval, condition on the evidence $z$ and decompose: $y$ is correct if (i) the retrieved $z$ actually contains the needed fact (retrieval quality) and (ii) $y$ is entailed by $z$ (faithfulness). Both parts are checkable: (i) offline with retrieval metrics, (ii) at answer time with an entailment model over $(z, y)$ — and the citation shown to the user is exactly the auditable trace of (ii). Because $z$ can postdate the training cutoff, retrieval also repairs the knowledge-cutoff failure of Section 02: the fact enters through the context window rather than the frozen parameters.

Definitions

Error decomposition (derivation: course notes). Writing $G$ for the event "retrieval returned adequate evidence" and $\mathrm{err}$ for an incorrect final answer, a union bound gives $$\Pr[\mathrm{err}] \;\le\; \Pr[\lnot G] \;+\; \Pr[\mathrm{err} \mid G],$$ retrieval failure plus unfaithful generation given good evidence. RAG shrinks the second term (the generator can copy instead of recall) but neither term is zero: retrievers miss or fetch look-alike entities, and generators misread, over-summarize, or override the source with parametric memory. The practical consequence: grounded systems still need the detection tools of this section (consistency checks, entailment audits of answer vs. citation) — grounding and detection are layers, not substitutes.

Teaching "I Don't Know"

Message: Make abstention a rewarded behavior: let “I don’t know” score zero and make confident wrong answers cost something. With a penalty λ, answering pays only above confidence λ/(1+λ), so a well-placed refusal can beat a wrong answer.

Reading the slide. Two payoff rows for correct / I don’t know / wrong. No penalty: +1 / 0 / 0, so a wrong answer costs nothing and guessing always pays. Penalized: +1 / 0 / −2, so “I don’t know” now competes; with λ = 2 in the formula below, guessing pays only above 2/3 confidence.

Script

Make abstention a rewarded behavior: reward a correct "I don't know," penalize confident wrong answers more heavily, and shift the guessing incentive.

Theorem & proof

Threshold scoring (derivation: course notes). Score $+1$ for a correct answer, $0$ for "I don't know", $-\lambda$ for a wrong answer. A guess the model believes correct with probability $p$ has expected score $p - \lambda(1-p)$, so answering beats abstaining iff $$p \;>\; \frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda} \;=:\; t \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \lambda = \frac{t}{1-t}.$$ Choosing the penalty $\lambda$ therefore implements a confidence threshold $t$: under this rule the model's optimal policy is exactly "answer iff confidence exceeds $t$" — honest abstention becomes rational rather than penalized. Kalai, Nachum, Vempala & Zhang (2025) argue this is the right lever: hallucination persists because mainstream evaluations grade 0/1, and the fix is a socio-technical change to how existing benchmarks are scored (explicit penalties/confidence targets), not another hallucination benchmark. arXiv 2509.04664.

A Scoring Rule

Message: Score a correct answer +1, “I don’t know” 0 and a wrong answer −4: a guess at confidence p is then worth 5p − 4, positive only above 80% confidence. A wild guess is no longer worth the risk, and honest abstention becomes rational.

Reading the slide. The rule is in the formula. The plot puts confidence $p$ that the guess is right on the x-axis: abstaining is flat at 0, guessing is the line $5p-4$, and they cross at $p = 0.8$. Left of the crossing, abstain; right of it, guess.

Script

Score a correct answer +1, "I don't know" 0, and a wrong answer -4. Under this rule a wild guess is no longer worth the risk.

Theorem & proof

The slide's numbers, worked (derivation: course notes). With $\lambda = 4$: expected score of guessing at confidence $p$ is $p - 4(1-p) = 5p - 4$, which is positive iff $p > 4/5$. So the $+1 / 0 / -4$ rule is the threshold rule of the previous entry (slide S61) with $t = \lambda/(1+\lambda) = 0.8$: the model should answer only when at least 80% sure, and a "wild guess" ($p$ near chance) has strongly negative expected value. Compare the binary grader, $\lambda = 0$, where the threshold is $t = 0$ and guessing is always optimal — the whole difference between an exam that rewards bluffing and one that rewards honesty is this one constant.

Script

Definitions

Sycophancy (Sharma et al., ICLR 2024): the tendency of an assistant to tailor its responses toward the user's stated views or preferences at the expense of truthfulness. The paper documents consistent sycophancy in five state-of-the-art assistants across four varied free-form generation tasks, and traces the incentive to the training signal: in human preference data, a response that matches the user's views is more likely to be preferred, and both human raters and preference models prefer convincingly written sycophantic responses over correct ones a non-negligible fraction of the time. In the lecture's framing this is the "pressure to please" analogue of the pressure to answer: the reward channel itself pays for agreement.

Background & further reading

The GPT-4o episode. Factuality Evaluations

Reading the slide. Min et al. (2023), FActScore Fig. 1, top example: a ChatGPT biography of Bridget Moynahan split into nine atomic facts checked against Wikipedia; six are supported, a FActScore of 66.7%. The figure’s second example (StableLM, 10.0%) is omitted on the slide.

Script

Definitions

Atomic-claim factual precision (FActScore; Min et al. 2023). Decompose a generation $y$ into atomic facts $a_1,\dots,a_k$ — short claims each conveying one piece of information — and score $$\mathrm{FActScore}(y) \;=\; \frac{\#\{\,i : a_i \text{ supported by the knowledge source}\,\}}{k},$$ the percentage of atomic facts supported by a reliable knowledge source. The motivation is exactly the slide's: long-form generations mix supported and unsupported content, so binary judgments are inadequate. On people biographies, ChatGPT scored only 58%; the paper also builds an automated estimator (retrieval + a strong LM) within 2% of human judgment, making claim-level factuality cheap to measure at scale.
