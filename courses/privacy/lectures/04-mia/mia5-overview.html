MIA 5 · LLM Membership Inference — Overview

One page on the closing lecture of the membership-inference series. Full slides: mia5-llm.html (123 slides). Assumptions, provenance of every number, and delivery notes: mia5-llm-note.html.

One-line summary. Every LLM membership attack computes the same score, $s(x) = \log p_\theta(x) - \log q(x)$, and differs only in how it estimates the null $q$. A model-based signal-to-noise argument explains why the pre-training regime is intrinsically hard; the empirical record then shows that most published successes measure the benchmark's construction rather than the model, and that even a well-calibrated attack with good aggregate AUC decides individual records by something close to a coin flip.

§1 Why pre-training MIA is structurally hard

A deliberately simple model — one SGD epoch, frozen gradients, a fixed initialisation — is enough to see the shape of the problem. It is a statement about the model, not a theorem about real LLMs.

  • Effect size. One pass over $x$ lowers its loss by $\Delta(x) = \eta E\,\|g_x\|^2$.
  • Signal to noise. With Gaussian scores, $\mathrm{AUC} = \Phi(\rho/\sqrt2)$ and $\mathrm{AUC} \approx \tfrac12 + \rho/(2\sqrt\pi)$ for small $\rho$: AUC is linear in the ratio near chance.
  • Two nulls, two ceilings. Pooled: $\rho \propto \eta E$, so the signal dies with one epoch and a small step. Calibrated (per-example): $\rho = \|g_x\|^2/\sqrt{N v_x}$ — the learning rate cancels, and the ratio decays as $N^{-1/2}$.
  • Three regimes. Pre-training (hardest), fine-tuning (tractable — see §4), context and retrieval (a different problem, where the membership hypothesis is no longer well posed).

§2 Calibration is the whole subject

Fix the definition first, then read the literature as a list of estimators:

$$s(x) \;=\; \log p_\theta(x) - \log q(x), \qquad q \text{ = the null: what a model that never saw } x \text{ would assign.}$$

Under a difficulty-plus-noise model, calibration helps exactly when the null's estimation error is smaller than the difficulty spread it removes ($\sigma_\delta^2 < \sigma_a^2$). A bad reference model makes an attack worse — that inequality is the reason.

The information-theoretic identity closes the loop: averaging the log-ratio over the population gives $\log\frac{p(x\mid\theta)}{p(x)}$ plus a term that does not involve $x$. For a fixed model, the optimal ranking is the calibrated score with $q$ = the population marginal.

§3 The empirical wall

  • Pre-training attacks barely work. On MIMIR — seven Pile domains, members and non-members from one pool — no attack or target model exceeds AUC 0.6, except on GitHub (Duan et al., COLM 2024).
  • Benchmarks measure their own construction. On eight published evaluation datasets, blind attacks that never query the model beat state-of-the-art MIAs. WikiMIA: 83.9 → 99.0 AUC blind (Das, Zhang & Tramèr, DATA-FM at ICLR 2025). Date-controlled splits close the gap.
  • Why that is a validity failure, not a success. If members and non-members come from different laws $Q_1 \ne Q_0$, the achievable advantage is $\mathrm{TV}(Q_1, Q_0)$ and it is attained without the model. A random split forces expected blind advantage to zero — the only sound test.
  • Good AUC still means coin flips per record. Retraining a 302M target 127 times: LiRA reaches AUC 0.752 ± 0.007, yet about 15.4% of true positives at $10^{-3}$ FPR are statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip, and 42.2% once highly unstable records are counted (Hayes et al., NeurIPS 2025). Membership contributes $\rho^2/(\rho^2+4)$ of the score's variance — under 1% at $\rho = 0.2$.

§4 Fine-tuning, extraction, defenses

  • The regime reverses. Same model, different constants: pooled signal scales with epochs $E$, calibrated signal with $\sqrt{N/N'}$. Small dataset plus many epochs moves both the right way — which is why fine-tuning attacks report the numbers pre-training attacks cannot.
  • Extractable ⊊ inferable. Greedy extraction is a membership test with false-positive rate at most $2^{-h}$, so extraction implies inference. The converse fails: a record the model prefers but never emits is overwhelmingly inferable. Verbatim recovery is a decoding accident; membership evidence is a likelihood ratio.
  • DP is the only guarantee. The $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ ROC cap gives $\mathrm{AUC} \le 1 - (1-\delta)^2/(2e^\varepsilon)$: 0.697 at $\varepsilon = 0.5$, 0.816 at $\varepsilon = 1$, 0.932 at $\varepsilon = 2$, 0.9998 at $\varepsilon = 8$. Large budgets forbid nothing.
  • Everything else is a mechanism, not a bound. Deduplication removes the repeat count that acts as extra epochs; curation removes records rather than protecting them; output filters block emission and leave the likelihood interface — and hence the whole of §2 — untouched.

§5 Where the series lands

Homer's genomic attack (2008), the shadow-model paradigm (2017), the theoretical clarification (2018–2019), the per-example era (2021–2023) and the LLM era (2023–2025) are one template:

$$\text{score}(x) \;=\; \log \frac{\Pr[\text{observation} \mid x \in D]}{\Pr[\text{observation} \mid x \notin D]}.$$

What changes is the observation (allele frequencies, confidence vector, loss, logit, token sequence) and how the denominator is estimated. Optimality is Neyman–Pearson throughout; the research frontier has always been the denominator.

Three things to remember

  1. One score, many nulls. Every attack in the literature is $\log p_\theta(x) - \log q(x)$; the disagreement is entirely about $q$.
  2. Check the split before the attack. A benchmark whose members and non-members differ in distribution measures that difference. Run a blind baseline first; if it wins, the evaluation is invalid, not the attack impressive.
  3. Aggregate AUC licenses no accusation. Per-record decisions are unstable even when the curve looks good, and members are the more unstable side.

Open problems

  • A pre-training benchmark that is both realistic and provably unconfounded.
  • Per-record certificates — what would justify an accusation about one document?
  • Null estimation without exchangeability: code, mathematics, structured records.
  • Principled aggregation of token-level evidence, beyond averaging or min-$k$.
  • Defenses between deduplication and full DP that still bound something.
  • Continual training, synthetic data and retrieval, where "training data" stops being well defined.

Where this series ends

The series began with a genomic attack that needed no machine learning and ends with attacks on models too large to retrain. The constant is the likelihood ratio; the difficulty is always the denominator. The honest closing position: fine-tuning membership inference is real, pre-training membership inference is unresolved, and most published evidence to the contrary is a property of the benchmark. The methodological lesson — audit the split before you believe the curve — outlives any particular attack.
