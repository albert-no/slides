01 — Why the guarantee does not transfer

Slide 1Title ~0:30

Unlearning in Large Language Models

Part I ended with a theorem: a Newton step plus calibrated noise gives an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ certificate. Part II is the negative image of that result. The subtitle is the thesis — the certificate does not transfer, and what replaces it is measurement, not proof.

"Last time we proved we could delete a point. Today we prove we cannot — and then spend three sections on what people do instead."

Slide 2Contents ~0:45

Contents — Part II

Four sections, and the arc is deliberately downward-then-constructive. 01 proves the Part I machinery breaks (Proposition 4, four clauses). 02 asks what objective is safe to minimise once proof is gone, and answers with NPO. 03 asks what the benchmarks actually measure, and finds two validity failures. 04 names the gap: suppression versus deletion.

Flag the structure: every section ends in a numbered result, not a slogan. There are seventeen numbered results in the deck and every one has a proof on the following slide.

Slide 3Section divider 01 ~0:10

01 — Why the guarantee does not transfer

Four properties of LLM training

A breath. Announce the shape: four properties P1–P4 of how language models are trained, four assumptions A1–A5 they destroy, and one proposition collecting the damage.

Slide 4Framing ~2:00

What the Certificate Bought Us

The chain on the slide is the whole of Part I in six words: convex loss → unique $\theta^\star$ → Newton step → sensitivity → Gaussian noise → $(\varepsilon,\delta)$. Walk it left to right and name the hypothesis each arrow needs.

The point to plant: this is a chain, so it fails at its weakest link — and we will find four broken links, not one.

"Everything in Part I hung off convexity. Pull that one thread and the whole certificate comes apart — but it comes apart in four different places, which is the interesting part."

Slide 5Recall · definition ~1:30

Recall — Certified Unlearning

Re-read the definition slowly, because Section 01 attacks each of its quantifiers in turn. The critical object is the right-hand side: $\mathcal{A}(D\setminus\{z\})$. That is the reference, and the whole of Part II is about what happens when the reference cannot be computed, cannot be located as a point, and is not the thing anyone actually wants.

"Is this just DP?" — Same two-sided form, different quantifier. DP quantifies over neighbouring datasets for a single algorithm; this quantifies over a deletion request applied to an already-trained model. The bound looks identical; the operational meaning is not.

Slide 6Recall · A1–A3 ~1:30

Recall — What Made It Provable

Three hypotheses did all the work: A1 convex and $G$-Lipschitz per example; A2 $\lambda$-strong convexity of the objective, which is what makes $\theta^\star(D)$ a point; A3 Lipschitz Hessian, which controls the Newton residual.

Residual $O(MG^2/(2\lambda^3n^2))$, sensitivity $O(2G/(\lambda n))$. Note the $\lambda^3$ in the denominator — this is where non-convexity will do its damage.

Slide 7A4, A5 ~2:30

Two Hypotheses Nobody States

This slide is the hinge of the section and it is worth slowing down. A1–A3 are stated in every paper. A4 and A5 are never stated because they are baked into the notation: writing $z \in D$ presumes the request is an index; writing $S \subseteq \Theta$ presumes the released object is a weight vector.

  • A4 fails because a real request is "forget this person", a predicate over documents, not a row id.
  • A5 fails because the deployed artefact is a text distribution, and nobody audits weights.

"The assumptions that break hardest are the two nobody bothered to write down, because for logistic regression they are too obvious to mention."

Slide 8P1–P4 ~1:30

Four Properties of LLM Training

The roadmap for the rest of the section. P1 non-convexity with exact symmetries; P2 concept-level request; P3 one pass over an enormous corpus; P4 a generator is released. Each maps to exactly one broken assumption, and we do them in order.

Slide 9Figure · assumption map ~1:00

The Assumption Map

The picture the audience should photograph. Left column P1–P4, right column A2/A4/A1/A5, four red arrows. Note the fourth arrow says reframes, not breaks — P4 does not falsify A5, it makes A5 answer the wrong question. That distinction matters for Lemma 3.

Slide 10P1 · symmetry ~2:30

P1 — Non-convexity Removes the Target

The cleanest possible witness for non-convexity: permutation symmetry of hidden units. Permute the units of a hidden layer, permute the corresponding rows and columns of the adjacent weight matrices, and the function computed is identical — so the loss is identical, for every dataset.

$F(\theta;D) = F(P\!\cdot\!\theta;D)$ for all $P\in S_h$. Minimisers therefore come in orbits of size up to $h!$; "the minimiser" $\theta_{-z}$ is not a point but a set.

Consequence: $\lambda=0$ exactly (not approximately), so $MG^2/(2\lambda^3n^2)$ is not a loose bound but an infinite one.

"Couldn't you quotient out the symmetry?" — For permutations, in principle. But permutation symmetry is only the symmetry we can write down; ReLU rescaling gives a continuous family, and neither exhausts the non-convexity. Quotienting fixes the example, not the theorem.

Slide 11P1 · Newton direction ~2:30

P1 — The Newton Step Can Point Uphill

The second, sharper consequence. Diagonalising the Hessian makes the failure a one-line computation.

$H=\sum_i\mu_i v_iv_i^\top$, $g_i=\langle\nabla F,v_i\rangle$, $\Delta=-H^{-1}\nabla F=-\sum_i (g_i/\mu_i)v_i$.

$\langle\nabla F,\Delta\rangle = -\sum_i g_i^2/\mu_i$. Every $\mu_i<0$ flips the sign of its term.

So at a saddle — and deep nets are saddle-rich — the Newton step is an ascent direction in those coordinates. Under A2 all $\mu_i\ge\lambda>0$ and the sum is unambiguously negative; that is precisely the work A2 was doing.

"The Newton step isn't merely inaccurate here. In the negative-curvature directions it moves you the wrong way, on purpose."

Slide 12P2 · concept requests ~2:00

P2 — The Request Names a Concept

A request is a predicate $\varphi$ on documents; the index set $S_\varphi=\{z: \varphi(z)\}$ is induced, not given. Two things go wrong: you may not be able to enumerate $S_\varphi$, and even if you could, removing it is not what was asked.

The Harry Potter paper is the honest statement of the real goal — break the association between entities, not delete rows. Cite it as evidence that practitioners already know A4 is false.

Slide 13Proposition 1 ~2:30

Proposition 1 — Exact Deletion Misses the Concept

The first numbered result, and it is deliberately shocking: even a perfect unlearning algorithm — one whose output is exactly distributed as a full retrain without $S_\varphi$ — emits the target completion with probability $q$, whatever $q$ is.

Exactness: $\theta_u \stackrel{d}{=} \mathcal{A}(D\setminus S_\varphi)$. Emission probability is a fixed measurable functional of that law. Equal laws, equal values. $\blacksquare$

The proof is three lines and that is the point: nothing here is subtle. The subtlety is entirely in noticing that the ideal target was never the goal.

Slide 14Discussion ~2:00

Reading Proposition 1

$q$ is large exactly when the concept is inferable from the surviving corpus, which for anything worth forgetting it is. Harry Potter plot points appear in fan wikis and reviews; anatomy is in every textbook; virology is in every paper that cites the one you deleted.

"Concept removal isn't a harder version of deletion. It's a different objective — and no algorithm can beat a target that already fails."

"So concept unlearning is impossible?" — Impossible relative to the retrain reference. It may still be achievable relative to a different reference, e.g. a model that was never exposed to the inference chain. Nobody has defined that reference yet; that is Section 04's open problem.

Slide 15P3 · Lemma 2 ~2:30

P3 — One Pass, One Gradient

Setup carefully: two SGD runs, identical seed and identical data order, differing only in that one skips the update at index $j$. This is the natural "influence of one example" question when you cannot retrain.

Lemma 2: $\lVert\theta_T-\theta_T'\rVert \le \eta_j G\, c^{\,T-j}$, where $c$ is the expansiveness constant of the gradient map.

Attribute to Hardt–Recht–Singer: the expansiveness lemmas are theirs, the application to deletion is ours.

Slide 16Proof ~2:00

Proof of Lemma 2

Two moves. Before $j$ the runs coincide. At $j$ the gap opens by exactly one gradient step, bounded by $\eta_jG$. After $j$ both runs apply the same map to different points, so the gap multiplies by at most $c$ per step. Induct.

Emphasise "same map, different points" — that is what expansiveness is about, and students routinely misread it as a bound on the map's output rather than on how it separates two inputs.

Slide 17The dichotomy ~2:30

The Dichotomy Hidden in $c$

The punchline of P3, and it is a genuine fork with no third branch.

  • Convex, $\eta\le 2/\beta$: $c=1$, so influence $\le\eta_jG$ — smaller than the seed-to-seed noise floor, hence unmeasurable.
  • Non-convex: $c=1+\eta\beta$, so the bound grows like $e^{\eta\beta(T-j)}$ — vacuous within a few thousand steps.

"Either the influence is too small to detect, or the bound on it is too big to use. There is no regime where per-example influence is both real and controlled."

Slide 18P4 · Lemma 3 ~2:30

P4 — Deletion Is Judged on Text

Define $\Gamma_\theta$ once and use it for the rest of the lecture: the law of a generation under a fixed decoding rule. Everything downstream — every benchmark in Section 03 — is a functional of $\Gamma_\theta$.

Lemma 3: $\mathrm{TV}(\Gamma_{\theta_u},\Gamma_{\theta_r})\le \mathrm{TV}(P_u,P_r)$, by the data-processing inequality for the Markov kernel $\theta\mapsto\Gamma_\theta$. Strictness: permutation-equivalent $\theta$ and $P\!\cdot\!\theta$ have identical $\Gamma$ but are distinct in $\Theta$.

The inequality points the wrong way for us. A parameter-space certificate would give an output guarantee; output evidence gives no parameter guarantee.

Slide 19Figure ~1:00

The Direction That Fails

The figure for Lemma 3. Two distinct parameter points map to the same output law; the dashed red arrow back is labelled "no inverse". This picture returns on slide 76 in benchmark form, so it is worth landing now.

Slide 20Proposition 4 ~2:00

Proposition 4 — The Certificate Does Not Transfer

The section's theorem, and it is a bookkeeping statement: four clauses, one per property, each already proved. Read the clauses; do not re-derive them here.

Slide 21Proof ~2:00

Proof of Proposition 4

Each line points back to a slide: (i) slide 10, (ii) slide 11, (iii) slides 15–17, (iv) slide 18. The highlight is the load-bearing sentence — these are independent failures, so a paper that fixes one has not moved the other three.

"What about a convexified or linearised model?" — Then (i)–(iii) can be repaired, and (iv) still stands, because (iv) is about what is released rather than how it was trained.

Slide 22Salvage ~2:00

What Survives

Important tonal correction after four negative results. The vocabulary survives — the definition, the hypothesis-testing frame, the TV ceiling, and exact retraining when you can afford it. The method does not: no influence functions, no single Newton step, no calibrated noise, no deletion capacity.

"Everything from here on is a heuristic. That's a statement of fact, not a complaint — heuristics are what you use when the theorem is unavailable, and this one is provably unavailable."

Slide 23Section recap ~1:00

Section 01 — Where We Stand

Three bullets and one arrow: no certificate → only measurement. Then pose the question that opens Section 02: given that we can no longer prove anything, what objective is at least safe to minimise?

02 — Objectives: from collapse to bounded forgetting

Slide 24Section divider 02 ~0:15

Objectives — From Collapse to Bounded Forgetting

Subtitle is the promise: one template, one weight function, and everything follows. This section is not a survey; it is a single equation with a parameter, and every method is a choice of that parameter.

Slide 25Setup ~2:00

The Design Problem

Fix notation for the whole section: $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ the trained model, $\mathcal{D}_f$ forget, $\mathcal{D}_r$ retain, $\ell_\theta(x,y)=\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ the sequence log-likelihood (not per-token — Proposition 9 turns on this).

The loss splits into a push term and a hold term. Everything interesting is in the push term, and specifically in one question the highlight states: how should the forget loss behave once forgetting has already succeeded?

Slide 26GA ~1:30

Gradient Ascent

The obvious first idea and the baseline everything reacts to: run the pretraining loss with a flipped sign on the forget set. Equivalent to unlikelihood training. It works just long enough to be tempting.

Slide 27Proposition 5 ~2:00

Proposition 5 — GA Has No Optimum

The failure is not empirical, it is structural: the infimum is $-\infty$ and it is not attained. Three-line proof — drive one forget probability to zero, the rest of the average is bounded above by $0$, done.

"This isn't a claim that GA is unstable in practice. It's a claim that GA has no answer. You are asking an optimiser to minimise a function with no minimum."

Slide 28Discussion ~2:00

Why That Matters in Practice

Translate the proposition into behaviour. We wanted: move off $\mathcal{D}_f$, then hold. GA does: keep pushing at undiminished strength forever. And because no parameter direction is reserved for $\mathcal{D}_f$, the continued pushing lands on $\mathcal{D}_r$ — this is exactly the catastrophic-collapse curve on slide 36.

"Doesn't early stopping fix it?" — It masks it. Early stopping means the stopping time, not the objective, is doing the work — and there is no principled way to pick it, since the retrain reference that would validate the choice is unavailable (Section 01).

Slide 29Theorem 6 ~3:00

Theorem 6 — Divergence Rates

Quantifies "keeps pushing" against "brakes" in the smallest model where both are computable: one scalar parameter, $\pi_\theta=\sigma(\theta)$, gradient flow.

GA: $\dot\theta = -\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GA}}$ has constant-order drift, so $\theta(t)=-t(1+o(1))$.

NPO: the weight $2\sigma(\beta r_\theta)\approx 2Ce^{-\beta v}$ once $v=-\theta$ is large, so $\dot v = 2Ce^{-\beta v}$, giving $e^{\beta v}=2C\beta t + c_1$, i.e. $v=\tfrac1\beta\log t\,(1+o(1))$.

Linear against logarithmic — from the same start, with the same direction. Write the ODE on the board; it is the clearest thing in the section.

Slide 30Recall · DPO ~2:30

Recall — Direct Preference Optimization

Needed as scaffolding, so keep it brisk. The implicit reward is the log-ratio $r_\theta(y)=\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$; DPO raises the winner's ratio and lowers the loser's, inside one sigmoid.

Point at the two branches explicitly, because the next slide deletes one of them.

Slide 31NPO derivation ~2:30

NPO — Delete the Positive Branch

The derivation is the whole idea: in unlearning nobody supplies a preferred answer, only a rejected one. Drop $y_w$ and you are left with $-\log\sigma(-\beta r_\theta)$; multiply by $2/\beta$ for the normalisation that makes Proposition 8 come out clean.

$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPO}} = -\tfrac{2}{\beta}\mathbb{E}\log\sigma(-\beta r_\theta) = \tfrac{2}{\beta}\mathbb{E}\log(1+e^{\beta r_\theta})$, using $-\log\sigma(-u)=\log(1+e^u)$.

Flag the $2/\beta$ as deliberate and say the next two results explain it — otherwise it looks arbitrary and students stop trusting the algebra.

Slide 32Theorem 7 ~2:30

Theorem 7 — NPO Is Self-Limiting

The centrepiece of the deck. Three clauses answering the design question from slide 25: (i) the loss is bounded below by $0$ so an optimum exists; (ii) the gradient is GA's direction scaled by $w_\theta=2\sigma(\beta r_\theta)\in(0,2)$; (iii) that weight is bounded by the likelihood ratio to the $\beta$.

The sentence to land: the weight vanishes exactly when forgetting succeeds. The brake is triggered by progress, not by a schedule.

Slide 33Proof ~3:00

Proof of Theorem 7

(i) $\log(1+e^u)>0$ always, $\to 0$ as $u\to-\infty$; take $\pi_\theta\to0$ so $r_\theta\to-\infty$.

(ii) $\tfrac{d}{du}\log\sigma(u)=\sigma(-u)$ and $\nabla_\theta r_\theta=\nabla_\theta\ell_\theta$ (the reference has no $\theta$). Chain rule with the inner $-\beta$: $-\tfrac{2}{\beta}\sigma(\beta r_\theta)(-\beta)\nabla\ell_\theta = 2\sigma(\beta r_\theta)\nabla\ell_\theta$.

(iii) $\sigma(u)\le e^u$, so $2\sigma(\beta r_\theta)\le 2e^{\beta r_\theta}=2(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})^\beta$.

Do (ii) slowly on the board — the two sign flips cancel and students who miss that think the gradient points the wrong way.

Slide 34The key line ~2:00

The Whole Difference, in One Line

If they remember one slide from Section 02, this is it. Same direction, different weight: $w^{\mathrm{GA}}\equiv 1$ versus $w^{\mathrm{NPO}}=2\sigma(\beta r_\theta)$.

"GA and NPO take the same step. GA takes it at full strength forever; NPO releases the pedal as the model forgets. That's the entire method."

Slide 35Figure · weight curves ~1:00

The Brake, Drawn

The red horizontal line is GA at constant $w=1$ (drawn against the ceiling $2$); the two blue curves are NPO at small and large $\beta$. Large $\beta$ brakes earlier and harder. Preview: this picture is the Pareto frontier on slide 40.

Slide 36Figure · NPO Fig. 2 ~1:30

Catastrophic Collapse, Measured

The empirical counterpart of Proposition 5. GA drags forget quality and retain quality down together — a single collapsing curve. NPO separates them. Point at the moment the GA retain curve turns over; that is the point at which the objective is still happily decreasing.

Slide 37Proposition 8 ~2:00

Proposition 8 — NPO Contains GA

NPO is not a rival family, it is a one-parameter deformation containing GA at the boundary. Note the constant $c_\beta=2\log2/\beta$ is $\theta$-independent, so it does not affect the argmin — say so, because its size ($\to\infty$) is alarming otherwise.

The interesting term is $\tfrac{\beta}{4}\mathbb{E}[r_\theta^2]$: the leading correction is a quadratic penalty on drift from the reference. NPO $=$ GA $+$ a proximal term, to first order.

Slide 38Proof ~2:30

Proof of Proposition 8

$g(u)=\log(1+e^u)$: $g(0)=\log2$, $g'=\sigma$ so $g'(0)=\tfrac12$, $g''=\sigma(1-\sigma)$ so $g''(0)=\tfrac14$.

$\tfrac{2}{\beta}g(\beta r) = \tfrac{2\log2}{\beta} + r + \tfrac{\beta}{4}r^2 + O(\beta^2)$ — the $\tfrac2\beta$ cancels the $\beta$ in the linear term, which is what the normalisation was for.

Then $r_\theta=\ell_\theta-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and take expectations. Gradient side: $w_\theta\to 2\sigma(0)=1$.

Slide 39Reading $\beta$ ~1:30

What $\beta$ Buys

Theorem 6 and Proposition 8 read the same knob from two ends. Small $\beta$: weight near $1$, GA-like, fast and unstable. Large $\beta$: weight decays quickly, gentle and slow at rate $\tfrac1\beta\log t$. Insist on the framing — this is the forgetting-versus-utility dial, not a nuisance hyperparameter to grid-search.

Slide 40Figure · NPO Fig. 3 ~1:00

The Dial, Measured

Each $\beta$ is a point on a frontier. Connect back to slide 35: the brake curve's shape is the frontier position. Nobody has a principled way to choose the point, and Section 03 explains why the axis it is chosen on is itself suspect.

Slide 41SimNPO motivation ~1:30

Two Complaints About NPO

Cost: a second forward pass and a second model in memory at every step. Bias: the weight uses the sequence log-ratio, so it scales with response length. Say the highlight verbatim — the first complaint is engineering, the second is a theorem, and it is on the next slide.

Slide 42Proposition 9 ~2:30

Proposition 9 — NPO Is Length-Biased

Set up the comparison carefully: two examples forgotten equally well per token, so $r_\theta(y)=\lvert y\rvert\rho$ with the same $\rho<0$. Their weight ratio tends to $e^{\beta\rho(\lvert y_1\rvert-\lvert y_2\rvert)}$ — exponential in the length difference.

$w_\theta(y)=2\sigma(\beta\lvert y\rvert\rho)$ by Theorem 7(ii); $\sigma(u)\sim e^u$ as $u\to-\infty$; divide.

Slide 43Discussion ~2:30

Reading Proposition 9

The damning reading: the brake fires on length, not on progress — so two of Theorem 7's virtues fight each other. Long forget targets (biographies, book passages, exactly the realistic ones) stall first; short ones keep taking full pressure, so retain damage concentrates on them.

"Theorem 7 said the brake fires when you've forgotten. Proposition 9 says it also fires when the answer is merely long. Same sigmoid, two different triggers, and only one of them is what we wanted."

End on the fix: divide by $\lvert y\rvert$ before the sigmoid, not after. That is the next slide in one sentence.

Slide 44SimNPO ~2:00

SimNPO — Normalise and Drop the Reference

Two edits to NPO, each answering one complaint from slide 41: divide the log-likelihood by $\lvert y\rvert$ inside the sigmoid, and replace $r_\theta$ (which needs $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$) by $\ell_\theta$ with a fixed margin $\gamma$. Resulting weight is self-limiting, length-free and reference-free.

"Is $\gamma$ just $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ in disguise?" — It is a constant reference point rather than an input-dependent one. Cheaper, and it removes the length coupling; the cost is that it no longer anchors per-example.

Slide 45Survey ~2:00

Four Other Places to Intervene

Deliberately compressed. The loss-weight axis is now exhausted, so the remaining moves change what you optimise rather than how hard: the target distribution (entropy maximisation), the target string (refusal or persona fine-tuning), internal activations (RMU), or the decoding-time state (activation redirection). Two of them get their own slide next.

Slide 46ME ~2:00

Entropy Maximisation Has an Optimum

The instructive contrast with Proposition 5. Pulling toward the uniform distribution gives $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\Vert u)=\log\lvert V\rvert - H(\pi_\theta)$, which is bounded below by $0$ and attained at $\pi_\theta=u$.

"NPO puts the brake in the weight. Entropy maximisation puts it in the target. Same disease, two different cures — and that's the taxonomy for the rest of the section."

Slide 47ELM · figure ~1:30

ELM — Erase by Redefining the Target

The paper's own Figure 1, and it reads left to right without commentary. The same prompt is run three ways — conditioned as an expert in the concept, unconditioned, and conditioned as a novice. Those three next-token laws are combined into one erased target distribution, and the model is fine-tuned toward it by cross-entropy.

Point at the arrow into $\theta^\star$ and say what is unusual: the objective never pushes away from anything. There is a named distribution on the right-hand side, and the loss is a distance to it.

"Every method so far has been defined by what it runs from. This one is defined by what it runs toward — and that single change is what puts a floor under the loss."

Slide 48ELM · analysis ~2:30

ELM — What the Target Buys

Write the target law down, because the picture hides the tilt: $\pi^\star \propto \pi_{\mathrm{nov}}(\pi_{\mathrm{nov}}/\pi_{\mathrm{exp}})^{\lambda}$ — the novice law pushed further away from the expert law by an exponent $\lambda$. Fitting it by cross-entropy gives a loss bounded below by $H(\pi^\star)$ and attained at $\pi_\theta=\pi^\star$.

That is the third cure for Proposition 5, and it belongs in the taxonomy explicitly: NPO puts the brake in the weight, entropy maximisation and ELM put it in the target. ELM is the one whose target is not a fixed distribution but a re-derived one.

Then land the P2 point. The request ELM answers is "remove bioweapons knowledge", not "remove these documents". It is the only objective in the section specified at concept level, which is the shape Proposition 1 said a row-deletion request cannot reach.

"Where does $\lambda$ come from?" — It is a chosen strength, not a derived one. $\lambda=0$ gives plain novice conditioning; large $\lambda$ extrapolates past the novice and starts to damage fluency. Same dial-shaped honesty problem as $\beta$ in NPO.

Slide 49RMU ~2:00

RMU — Intervene on Activations

Representation misdirection: at a chosen layer, push forget-input activations toward a fixed random vector $c\xi$ and hold retain-input activations at their reference values. Both terms are squared distances to fixed targets, hence bounded below — again the brake is in the target.

Note for later: RMU is the method shipped with WMDP (slide 64), so the benchmark and its headline method come from the same paper. Worth flagging as a validity concern before Section 03 gets there.

Slide 50Proposition 10 ~2:30

Proposition 10 — One Family

The section's structural theorem: GA, NPO and SimNPO are one gradient template with three weights. The second half is the sharp part — among losses $\mathbb{E}[\phi(\ell_\theta)]$, the weight is constant iff $\phi'$ is constant iff $\phi$ is affine. GA is the unique constant-pressure member.

"So GA isn't a bad member of the family. It's the only member without a brake, and it's the only one you get if you insist on an affine loss."

Slide 51Figure · design space ~1:30

The Design Space

Two axes. Horizontal: forget pressure, constant (GA, red) to self-limiting (NPO, SimNPO, ME). 03 — Benchmarks: what is actually measured

Slide 54Section divider 03 ~0:15

Benchmarks — What Is Actually Measured

Four suites, one shared statistic, and a validity failure. Warn the audience that this section contains the two results most likely to annoy someone in the room, and that both are elementary.

Slide 55Taxonomy ~1:30

How Success Gets Declared

Three shapes: indistinguishability from a retrained model, accuracy on forget-topic questions, adversarial probing. Only the first has a hypothesis-testing interpretation — and the highlight is the setup for Theorem 11: it is also the most misread.

Slide 56TOFU · figure ~1:30

TOFU — The Benchmark Pipeline

TOFU's Figure 1, and the whole benchmark is in it: a pretrained model, fine-tuned on the fictitious-author corpus, then unlearned on a split of that corpus. Trace the three llamas in order and stop on the middle arrow.

The thing to say out loud is that every stage here is under the evaluator's control — the corpus is synthetic, so the retain model can actually be trained. That is what makes the Part I reference object available, and it is why the next three slides are able to criticise the metric rather than the design.

"This is the only benchmark in the field where the object Part I needed — a model that genuinely never saw the data — exists and is cheap. Hold that thought, because the metric built on top of it still goes wrong."

Slide 57TOFU ~2:00

TOFU — Fictitious Authors

The design is genuinely clever and should be praised before it is criticised. Synthetic authors mean the base model provably did not see them, so "never trained on this" is available as ground truth and the retain model is cheap to compute. That is the one place in the field where the Part I reference object actually exists.

Slide 58Forget quality ~2:00

TOFU — Forget Quality

Define the truth ratio, then the metric: a two-sample KS test between the unlearned model's ratios and the retain model's, reporting the $p$-value. Say the operational reading out loud — high $p$ means the test could not tell them apart — because the next slide attacks exactly that reading.

Slide 59Theorem 11 ~3:00

Theorem 11 — A $p$-Value Is Not a Score

The section's main result. Under perfect unlearning the two samples come from one law, so the null holds, so the $p$-value is uniform. Two consequences: a perfect unlearner clears a $0.9$ bar only one time in ten; and if you report the best of $k$ runs, $\mathbb{E}[p_{\max}]=k/(k+1)$.

"The paradox is that this is a failure mode of success. If your method is perfect, this metric says you failed nine times out of ten."

Slide 60Proof ~2:30

Proof of Theorem 11

(i) Probability integral transform: $T$ has continuous law $F$, $p=1-F(T)$, so $F(T)\sim\mathrm{U}[0,1]$ and $p\sim\mathrm{U}[0,1]$.

(ii) $\Pr[p_{\max}\le t]=t^k$; density $kt^{k-1}$; $\mathbb{E}[p_{\max}]=k/(k+1)$; $\Pr[p_{\max}\ge0.9]=1-0.9^k$.

Stress the scope note: only continuity of the null law is used, so nothing here is a criticism of the KS test specifically. Any $p$-value-as-score benchmark inherits it.

Slide 61Table ~1:30

Selection Inflates the Score

Theorem 11(ii) as numbers. At $k=10$ the expected reported score is $0.909$ and the chance of clearing $0.9$ is $0.651$ — with an algorithm that does nothing but produce independent perfect runs. Ask the room how many checkpoints a typical paper sweeps.

Slide 62Discussion ~2:00

Reading Theorem 11

The three bullets are the statistical reading: failing to reject is absence of evidence; the test has low power at $n=200$; and low power makes high $p$ easier, so a weak test flatters a weak unlearner. That last one is the perverse incentive.

"What should they report instead?" — An equivalence test (TOST-style) with a stated tolerance $\delta$: reject "the two differ by more than $\delta$". That inverts the null so evidence accumulates for the claim being made, and forces the tolerance into the open.

Slide 63WMDP · figure ~1:00

WMDP — What Is In the Set

WMDP's Figure 1: 3,668 questions in three hazard domains — biology 1,273, chemistry 408, cyber 1,987 — with the sub-categories listed around each wedge.

Use it for scale and for format. Every item is multiple choice, which is exactly the property Corollary 12 will exploit two slides later: the benchmark measures answering behaviour under one template, not the presence of the knowledge.

"Read the wedges, then read the format line. Three thousand questions is a lot of coverage of one prompt shape."

Slide 64WMDP ~2:00

WMDP — Hazardous Knowledge

~4,000 multiple-choice questions across bio, cyber and chem security; the metric is hazardous-set accuracy against MMLU accuracy, with down-and-flat as the goal. Note again that RMU shipped in the same paper.

Slide 65Corollary 12 ~2:00

Corollary 12 — Accuracy Is Prompt-Relative

A one-line proof for a claim people resist: the benchmark template is one element of the set the adversary's supremum ranges over, so $A(\theta;T_{\mathrm{bench}})\le\sup_{T'}A(\theta;T')$. A low benchmark score is necessary for capability removal and never sufficient.

"WMDP reports the left-hand side. Every safety claim made with it concerns the right-hand side. The gap is not measured anywhere."

RWKU — The Evaluation Framework

RWKU's Figure 1, worth a slow read because the layout is the argument. One real target — Stephen King — and then four probe sets built around him: forget probes (memorisation, manipulation, adversarial injection), a neighbour set on adjacent entities, an MIA set, and a general utility set.

Slide 70Table ~1:30

The Four Suites

Reference table. The closing line is the point: only TOFU and MUSE carry a retrained reference, so only they can even state the Part I definition. WMDP and RWKU are measuring something else and should not be read as unlearning certificates.

Slide 71Recall · Lecture 04 ~2:00

Recall — A Validity Failure

Series continuity: this is Proposition 7 from the membership-inference deck. If two evaluation groups are fixed by a $\theta$-independent rule, the best achievable advantage is $\mathrm{TV}(Q_1,Q_0)$ — attained by a test that never queries the model.

The transfer is the sentence at the bottom: the same argument fires whenever a score compares the forget corpus to the retain corpus, which is most of this section.

Slide 72Proposition 13 ~2:30

Proposition 13 — The Split Confound

The unlearning-specific instance. If the forget and retain corpora differ in difficulty, then $M(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\ne0$ before any unlearning happens, and $M(\theta_u)$ silently mixes forgetting with that offset.

The only offset-free estimand is the difference-in-differences $M(\theta_u)-M(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ — i.e. you must report the base-model row.

Slide 73Discussion ~1:30

Which Designs Escape It

TOFU escapes because both branches are scored on the same forget questions and only the model varies. Exposed: any headline of the form "forget accuracy minus retain accuracy" reported without a base-model row. The rule to remember is the highlight — hold the text fixed and vary the model, never the reverse.

Slide 74Proposition 14 ~2:00

Proposition 14 — Output Metrics Are Blind

A triviality with teeth. Any black-box metric factors as $\mathcal{M}=\Psi\circ\Gamma$, so models with equal output laws get equal scores, whatever their weights encode. Every metric in the table on slide 70 is of this form.

This is Lemma 3's failure direction (slide 18) recast as a statement about benchmarks. Say the connection aloud — it is the deck's spine.

Slide 75Objection handling ~2:30

Is Proposition 14 Vacuous?

The obvious objection: if suppression and deletion always produced different text, the proposition would be empty. Three empirical answers — hidden-layer probes recover what generation hides; a few gradient steps on unrelated text restore forget-set performance; quantisation alone can undo suppression.

"Same score, different weights, different behaviour the moment anyone fine-tunes. That gap is what Section 04 promotes into the definition."

Slide 76Figure ~1:00

What the Benchmark Sees

Slide 19's picture in benchmark clothes: a suppressed model (knowledge intact) and a deleted model (knowledge gone) collapse to one reported number. The map is not injective, and the benchmark only sees the image.

Slide 77Cooper et al. ~2:00

The Word Is Doing Too Much Work

The policy-side version of the same complaint. Deleting a document does not delete the capability it taught; a right to erasure is about a data subject, not a model behaviour; suppressing an output is a content filter renamed. Use this to justify the terminology slides at the end of Section 04.

Slide 78Section recap ~1:00

Section 03 — Where We Stand

Three bullets keyed to Theorem 11, its selection corollary, and Corollary 12 / Proposition 14. The closing highlight is deliberately fair: the measurements are informative, they just do not separate suppression from deletion. That is the handoff into Section 04.

04 — Relearning, suppression, and the open problem

Slide 79Section divider 04 ~0:15

Relearning, Suppression, and the Open Problem

The constructive section. Section 03 ended with a gap no metric can close; this one closes it by changing the definition rather than the measurement.

Slide 80Framing ~2:00

Two Hypotheses

A model that stops answering admits exactly two explanations: the representation is gone (deletion), or it is intact behind a learned gate (suppression). Proposition 14 says no output metric distinguishes them.

"If you can't measure the difference, stop trying to measure it and put it in the definition instead. That's the whole move of this section."

Slide 81Definition 15 ~3:00

Definition 15 — Closure Under Adaptation

The deck's constructive contribution. Fix a behaviour functional $B$ and an adaptation class $\mathcal{T}$ (at most $m$ steps on at most $n$ permitted examples). Suppression is $B(\theta_u)\le\varepsilon$; deletion is $\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}B(\tau(\theta_u))\le\varepsilon$.

Insist on the last line: the budget is part of the claim. "Deleted" with no $\mathcal{T}$ named is not a statement. This mirrors the cryptographic convention of naming the adversary class.

"How do you evaluate a supremum over all fine-tunes?" — You don't; you lower-bound it with specific attacks, which is why Corollary 17 matters. The definition is what a claim means, not a procedure — same relationship as between semantic security and a distinguishing game.

Slide 82Proposition 16 ~1:30

Proposition 16 — Strictly Stronger

Forward direction is one line (take $\tau=\mathrm{id}$, which requires $\mathrm{id}\in\mathcal{T}$ — say why that hypothesis is there). Strictness needs a construction, and it is on the next slide.

Slide 83Construction ~2:30

Proof of Proposition 16 — The Gate

The separating example, and it is deliberately minimal: release $f_{\theta_0}(x)-c\,g(x)$ where $g$ is a fixed feature firing on forget prompts and $c\ge0$ is a single trainable scalar.

Large $c$ ⇒ $B(\theta_u)\le\varepsilon$: suppression holds at any level you like. But $\theta_0$ is untouched, and one step $c\mapsto c-\eta\,\partial_c\mathcal{L}$ with large $\eta$ returns $c$ to $0$, so $B\gg\varepsilon$.

The highlight is the moral: suppression can be one coordinate deep, and no output metric reveals its depth. Nobody builds this gate on purpose — the point is that gradient descent can find one, and nothing in the evaluation would notice.

Slide 84Corollary 17 ~2:00

Corollary 17 — Attacks Are One-Sided

Trivial proof, important consequence: a supremum over a subset is at most the supremum over the set. So a successful relearning attack refutes deletion; an unsuccessful one establishes nothing at all.

"This is the cryptanalysis asymmetry. Attacks certify weakness, never strength — so 'we ran the attacks and they failed' is not a security claim, and it isn't a deletion claim either."

Slide 85Figure ~1:00

Closure, Drawn

Green: deletion, every adaptation in the budget stays inside the region. Red: suppression, one arrow escapes. The picture is the definition; use it whenever the algebra of the supremum gets abstract.

Slide 86Benign relearning ~2:30

Benign Relearning

The empirical refutation. Unlearn a book, then fine-tune on model-generated summaries of it — and the memorisation returns. Walk the pipeline figure left to right: base model, unlearn, relearn-set construction via API access, relearned model.

Slide 87Discussion ~2:00

Why That Is Damning

Three reasons, and the first is the one that lands: the relearning corpus is public, legal, and contains none of the original tokens. Nothing about this is an attack in the adversarial sense — it is ordinary downstream fine-tuning. The budget is a handful of steps, well inside any realistic $\mathcal{T}$, so by Corollary 17 this refutes deletion for the methods tested.

Close by naming the mechanism: the knowledge was dormant, not absent — exactly the gate of Proposition 16, found by gradient descent rather than by construction.

Slide 88Lab result · setup ~2:30

What Property Triggers Recovery?

Our lab's result, and the design is the interesting part: split the relearning corpus along two independent axes. Same topic with new syntax; same syntax with new entities. Ask the room to predict before revealing — almost everyone says topic.

The answer: same-topic-new-syntax leaves the content forgotten; same-syntax-new-entities gives full recovery with no topical overlap at all. The re-entry path is the sentence shape.

Slide 89Lab result · figure ~2:00

Recovery Tracks Syntactic Similarity

Three panels, GA / NPO / SCRUB, each with three bars: target set, topically relevant set, syntactically similar set. Across all three methods the syntactic bar is high and the topical bar is low, on representation similarity, gradient similarity and relearn success rate alike. The effect is method-independent, which is what makes it a property of unlearning rather than of one objective.

Slide 90Mechanism ~2:00

A Mechanism, and a Fix

Proposed mechanism: unlearning perturbs the surface forms it was shown. A familiar syntactic frame that was never in the forget queries reaches past the perturbation to a representation nobody touched.

The mitigation is actionable and cheap: diversify the syntax of forget queries during unlearning, not only their content. This is the one slide in Section 04 that tells a practitioner to do something differently tomorrow.

Slide 91DUSK ~1:30

DUSK — Do Not Unlearn Shared Knowledge

Second lab paper. The figure shows documents split into content unique to the forget set and content shared with retain sources. Set up the formalism on the next slide rather than explaining it here.

Slide 92DUSK formalism ~2:00

The Partition DUSK Enforces

$\mathcal{D}_f = U\,\dot\cup\,S$: $U$ unique to the forget set, $S$ also present in retain sources. The target is two-sided — $B_U(\theta_u)\le\varepsilon$ and $B_S(\theta_u)\approx B_S(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$.

"Forget the biography without forgetting the city he lived in. Standard benchmarks score only the first half, so a method that destroys the second half still scores well."

Connect to Proposition 13: this is another way a single headline number hides a two-sided requirement.

Slide 93R-TOFU ~1:30

R-TOFU — Two Surfaces to Leak From

Third lab paper, and the newest setting: reasoning models emit a chain of thought and a final answer, so there are two output surfaces. Let the figure do the setup; the findings are next.

Slide 94R-TOFU findings ~2:30

What the Chain of Thought Reveals

Three findings: answer-only unlearning leaves residue (the model refuses the answer, then reasons its way there); chain-of-thought unlearning is the stronger variant; and forcing short or empty reasoning at decoding time exposes knowledge the default decoder hides — a decoding-level analogue of Corollary 12.

The highlight is the theoretical hook: a second output surface is a second $\Gamma_\theta$, and Proposition 14 applies to each separately. So a suite that scores only the answer is blind on both counts.

Slide 95Competition ~2:00

Are We Making Progress?

The NeurIPS 2023 competition as an aggregate data point: 1000+ submissions, fixed compute. Best entries beat naive fine-tuning by a few points on the membership gap; scores saturate near the retrained reference on the competition's own forget set; transfer to different forget sets is poor.

Tie it to Theorem 11's selection argument — a score tuned on one forget set is biased for every other one. Same mechanism, competition scale.

Slide 96Terminology ~2:30

Five Things, One Word

The position paper's proposal. Reserve unlearning for the Part I definition, and give the other five honest names: output likelihood suppression (GA, NPO, SimNPO); internal-representation obfuscation (RMU, redirection); knowledge editing; behavioural refusal; inference-time intervention.

Read the table against Section 02 — every objective we derived lands in row one or row two. Not one of them is in the reserved row.

Slide 97Argument ~2:00

Why the Naming Is a Safety Measure

The argument, not the assertion: one word lets a weak method borrow a strong word's guarantees. "Unlearned" is read as retraining-equivalent, and compliance gets claimed on that reading. Section 01 proved the guarantee is unavailable, so the vocabulary should say so.

"This is the cheapest correction available, and the only one that scales — it costs nothing and it doesn't need anyone's benchmark to improve."

Slide 98Open problem ~2:30

The Open Problem

The intellectual close. Part I had a unit of deletion — a row. An LLM corpus has none, and the candidates (a token, a fact, a capability, a syntactic frame) are not refinements of each other. The fourth is our own contribution to the list, from slide 88.

Land the last bullet slowly: until the unit is named, unlearning is not underpowered, it is ill-posed. Data valuation, influence functions and membership inference are three views of the same missing definition.

Slide 99Takeaways ~1:30

Takeaways

Four bullets, each keyed to a numbered result: Proposition 4; Theorem 7 and Proposition 10; Theorem 11; Definition 15 and Corollary 17. Then the closing contrast — Part I could define it and prove it, Part II can barely define it and cannot verify it.

Slide 100Q&A ~remaining

Questions

Three seeded questions on the slide. If the room is quiet, the productive one is the first — should regulators accept output-level audits at all? Proposition 14 says such audits cannot distinguish suppression from deletion, so the honest answer is that they can only support suppression claims, and the regulation is written in the language of deletion.

"Is exact unlearning the only honest path?" — It is the only one with a guarantee, and it costs a retrain. The interesting middle ground is sharding (SISA-style) at pretraining time, which buys exactness per shard at the cost of model quality — worth naming if someone asks.
