13. A Tiny Nudge Flips a Confident Answer

A tiny nudge · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈12.5 min

Message: Adding a change of about one 8-bit brightness step per pixel, chosen using the model's gradients, turned GoogLeNet's 57.7% "panda" into a 99.3% "gibbon" on an image that looks unchanged to us.

Reading the slide. Read the threat-model line above the figure first: the attacker edits pixels slightly, knows the gradients, and wants any wrong label. Then Goodfellow et al. Fig. 1, left to right: the panda (57.7%), plus 0.007 times a sign pattern (itself read as nematode, 8.2%), equals an image read as gibbon (99.3%). The ε line converts .007 into about one 8-bit intensity step after GoogLeNet's input scaling. It is one example on one model, not a rate.

Script

The most famous picture in this field. On the left, an image GoogLeNet calls "panda" with 57.7% confidence. In the middle, a pattern that looks like coloured static. We add a tiny multiple of it, 0.007, to the panda. On the right is the result. To us it is the same panda. The network now says "gibbon", with 99.3% confidence.

How small is 0.007? The authors say it equals the size of the smallest bit of an 8-bit image encoding after GoogLeNet's conversion to real numbers: about one step of brightness per pixel.

Notice the line above the figure. Every example today comes with a threat model: what the attacker controls, what they know, and what they want. Here: they change pixels by about one step each, they know the model's gradients, and they want any wrong label.

The figure

Fig. 1 of Goodfellow et al.: x (panda, 57.7%) + .007 × sign(∇ₓJ(θ, x, y)) (the noise, itself classified "nematode", 8.2%) = adversarial image ("gibbon", 99.3%).

Setup, model, date

GoogLeNet on ImageNet; ε = .007 in the ℓ∞ sense; white-box attack (FGSM, slide S57). Published 2015.

What this establishes

An adversarial example exists for this image and model, at a change humans do not notice.

What this does not establish

Nothing about how often this happens on natural inputs, or about other models, budgets or physical images. One illustrative example.

14. Anatomy of an Adversarial Example

A tiny nudge · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈13.5 min

Message: An adversarial example x′ = x + δ adds a change too small to alter the true label for a human, but chosen by search so the model's answer changes; random noise of the same size usually does nothing.

Reading the slide. The definition gives the formula. The diagram reads as an equation: clean input x, plus a change δ of size at most ε, equals x′, which looks the same. Under x′ the outcome: a human says panda, the model says gibbon. The bottom note says "small" needs a ruler, fixed later by a budget ε and a norm (S42–S45).

Script

Let us name the parts. An adversarial example is x′ = x + δ. The change δ is too small to alter the true label, at least for a human, but it is chosen so that the model's answer changes.

The key word is "chosen". Random noise of the same size usually does nothing. The attacker searches for the specific δ that hurts the model most.

And "small" needs a ruler. In Section 04 (slides S42–S45) we will fix it with a budget ε and a norm, the way we measure size.

The figure

Schematic drawn for this lecture: input x + change δ (size ≤ ε) = x′; a human still says panda, the model says gibbon.

Assumptions

"Too small to alter the true label" is the working assumption behind small-norm threat models. It is a judgement about humans, not a mathematical fact, and fails for large ε.

18. Who Chooses the Input Changes the Question

A tiny nudge · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈17 min

Message: Accuracy is an average-case score where nature draws the test inputs; robust accuracy is worst case, where an adversary picks inputs within allowed changes, and a model can score high on the first and near zero on the second.

Reading the slide. Two boxes. Left: average case, nature draws inputs from the test distribution, measured by accuracy. Right: worst case, an adversary picks the input within allowed changes, measured by robust accuracy. The bottom line says high accuracy answers only the first question.

Script

Here is the conceptual shift. Normal test accuracy is an average case: nature draws inputs from the test distribution, and we count how many are right.

Robustness is a worst case: an adversary picks the input, within allowed changes, to make the model fail. That number is called robust accuracy.

A model can be 95% accurate and have near-zero robust accuracy. High accuracy answers the first question, not the second.

25. A Linear Score Adds Up Every Pixel

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈23 min

Message: A linear model scores a class by a weighted sum over all n pixel values, so changing each pixel by δᵢ moves the score by the sum of wᵢδᵢ: n small terms added into one total.

Reading the slide. The formula s(x) = wᵀx = Σ wᵢxᵢ defines the score. w are the learned weights and n is the number of pixel values. The second line gives the change in score when pixel i moves by δᵢ. The intuition: every pixel contributes, so many small terms can add up.

Script

Now with a formula. A simple model scores a class by a weighted sum: s(x) = w·x, the sum over n pixel values of wᵢ xᵢ. Here w are the weights the model learned and n is the number of pixel values.

Change pixel i by δᵢ, and the score moves by the sum of wᵢ δᵢ: n small terms, one sum.

Primary sources

  • Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy, "Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples" (ICLR 2015) — the panda (Fig. 1), FGSM, the "too linear" argument (§3) and Figs. 2 and 4.

26. The Best Push: Follow the Sign of Each Weight

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈24 min

Message: If each pixel may move at most ε, the best push moves every pixel ε in the direction its weight points, so every term adds and the score moves by ε times the sum of absolute weights.

Reading the slide. Read the rule, then the formula δ = ε·sign(w), which gives wᵀδ = ε‖w‖₁. The worked line shows the sign rule: w₁ = +2, raise pixel 1, gain 2ε; w₂ = −3, lower pixel 2, gain 3ε. The bottom line names why it works: every term is positive, nothing cancels.

Script

The rule: each pixel may move at most ε. What is the best move? Push each pixel ε the way its weight points: up where the weight is positive, down where it is negative. That is δ = ε·sign(w). Tiny example: if w₁ = +2, raise pixel 1 by ε and the score gains 2ε; if w₂ = −3, lower pixel 2 by ε and the score gains 3ε. Both moves increase the chosen score.

Then every term wᵢ δᵢ is positive; nothing cancels. The score moves by ε times the sum of |wᵢ|, written ε‖w‖₁.

Assumptions

Exact for a linear score; for deep networks this is a local approximation.

Primary sources

  • Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy, "Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples" (ICLR 2015) — the panda (Fig. 1), FGSM, the "too linear" argument (§3) and Figs. 2 and 4.

28. This Push Has a Name: FGSM

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈26 min

Message: FGSM, the Fast Gradient Sign Method, applies the same sign push to the loss: move every pixel by ε along the sign of the loss gradient with respect to the input.

Reading the slide. The formula: x′ = x + ε·sign(∇ₓL(f(x), y)). The gradient says which way each pixel raises the loss; sign keeps only the direction; ε sets the size. 56. An Attack Is a Search Inside the Ball

Worst case · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈50 min

Message: An attack maximises the loss over all changes inside the ball, using the gradient with respect to the input; it mirrors training, which lowers the loss by changing the weights, while an attack raises it by changing the input.

Reading the slide. The formula: maximise L(f(x + δ), y) over ‖δ‖ ≤ ε. The three tags name its parts: the loss L is high when the model is wrong, the gradient ∇ₓL gives the direction in which the loss rises, and the constraint keeps δ in the ball. The bottom line contrasts training with attacking; the weights stay fixed during an attack.

Script

Formally, the attacker solves: maximize the loss L of the model's output on x + δ, against the true label y, over all δ with size at most ε.

Three ingredients. The loss, which is high when the model is wrong. The gradient of the loss with respect to the input, which says in which direction the loss rises. And the constraint: stay inside the ball.

This mirrors training. Training lowers the loss by changing the weights; an attack raises it by changing the input, with the weights fixed.

Going further

Other formulations exist. 59. PGD: Gradient Descent, Run Uphill

Worst case · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈54 min

Message: PGD runs gradient steps on the input, uphill: take a small sign step from the current point, clip back into the ε-ball and valid pixel range, repeat K times; FGSM is the one-step case, and Madry and colleagues made PGD the default.

Reading the slide. Two cards. Training: change the weights to lower the loss, w ← w − η∇_wL. PGD: change the input to raise the loss, x′ ← clip(x′ + α·sign(∇_{x′}L)). The loop below reads: small step uphill, clip to the ball and [0, 1], repeat K times. Note the gradient is taken at the current x′, not the original x. The bottom line places FGSM as the special case.

Script

The fix uses an idea you already know. Training runs gradient descent on the weights: a small step that lowers the loss, repeated. PGD, Projected Gradient Descent, runs the same loop on the input, uphill: a small step that raises the loss.

Here is the ℓ∞ version. One step: move the current input x′ a little, α, along the sign of the gradient taken at x′ itself, not at the original x. Then clip: pull the result back into the ε-ball around x and into the valid pixel range [0, 1]. Repeat K times; PGD-7 or PGD-20 means 7 or 20 steps. In practice PGD also starts from a random point in the ball, and may restart several times and keep the worst point found.

FGSM is the one-step case: start at the original input, no random start, one step of size ε. Madry and colleagues made PGD the default attack for evaluating and training robust models.

69. Adapted Attacks Rebuild the Missing Gradient

Script

How do you attack a defense that hides its gradient? Rebuild the gradient. If a step is broken or flat, use a smooth stand-in for it when computing the gradient: BPDA, Backward Pass Differentiable Approximation. The result. Athalye and colleagues examined the nine white-box defenses accepted at ICLR 2018. Seven hid gradients. With adapted attacks, six were broken completely and one partially. The tested Madry et al. adversarially trained model retained 47% accuracy under the adapted attack.

Hiding the gradient delays the attacker; it does not stop one. A conference-reviewed defense is not a verified defense.

Message: If f's vote under noise is lopsided, a small shift of x cannot swing it, so Cohen and colleagues prove a certified ℓ2 radius for the smoothed classifier g; it is estimated by sampling with failure probability at most α, may abstain, and certifies stability, not correctness.

Why does voting give a certificate? Intuition: if the vote at x is lopsided, say 90% panda, then moving x a little only shifts the noise cloud a little; the vote cannot swing to another class until you move far enough.

Message: Physical attacks must survive printing, colour and resolution changes, and a camera at different distances, angles and light, so they optimise the average loss over sampled transformations (EOT), which raises average success, not success on each one.
