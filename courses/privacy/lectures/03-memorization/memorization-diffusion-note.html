Memorization in Diffusion Models — Companion Notes

Speaker's reading companion. Off-slide derivations, regularity conditions, and bookkeeping for the memorization deck. Slide-side statements are kept clean; every proof step the slides compress is written out here in full.

0. Sign conventions and the precision spectrum

The deck states every curvature lemma in terms of precision eigenvalues. This is a bookkeeping choice, and it is worth being explicit about it once, because the published statements in the literature mix conventions and a careless reader will conclude that a manifestly non-negative quantity equals a negative number.

Fix a local Gaussian model $x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$ with $\Sigma \succ 0$. Then

$$\log p(x) = -\tfrac12 (x-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu) + \text{const}, \qquad s(x) = \nabla \log p(x) = -\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu),$$
$$H \;=\; \nabla^2 \log p(x) \;=\; -\Sigma^{-1} \qquad (\text{independent of } x).$$

So $H \prec 0$: every eigenvalue of the log-density Hessian is strictly negative. Writing $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_D^2$ for the eigenvalues of $\Sigma$, define

$$\Lambda_i \;:=\; -\lambda_i(H) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2} \;>\; 0.$$

Why this matters. Lemma 4.2 as printed in the paper reads $\sum_i (\lambda_i - \lambda_{i,c})^2 / \lambda_{i,c}$. If the $\lambda$ there are read as eigenvalues of the log-density Hessian, the denominator is negative and the whole sum is negative — impossible for an expected squared norm. Read as precisions $\Lambda_i = 1/\sigma_i^2$ the statement is correct and non-negative. The deck therefore states everything in $\Lambda$, and flags the sign explicitly on the "Local Gaussian Toolkit" slide.

Sharpness intuition in this convention: a sharp direction is one with small $\sigma_i$, hence large $\Lambda_i$, hence a large-magnitude negative Hessian eigenvalue. "Heavy negative-eigenvalue tail" in the empirical histograms means "a few very large precisions".

1. Levina–Bickel: the maximum-likelihood LID estimator

The deck states the estimator and asserts it is an MLE. Here is the derivation, following Levina and Bickel (NeurIPS 2004).

1.1 The local Poisson model

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. samples from a density $f$ on a $k$-dimensional manifold embedded in $\mathbb{R}^D$. Fix a point $x$ and consider the counting process

$$N(r) \;=\; \#\{\, i \,:\, \|X_i - x\| \le r \,\}, \qquad 0 \le r \le R.$$

Assume $f$ is approximately constant, $f(x)$, in the small ball $B(x,R)$. The volume of a $k$-dimensional ball of radius $r$ is $V_k r^k$ with $V_k = \pi^{k/2} / \Gamma(k/2 + 1)$. Then the expected count is $\mathbb{E}[N(r)] \approx n f(x) V_k r^k$, and $N(r)$ is modelled as an inhomogeneous Poisson process in the radial variable with rate

$$\lambda(r) \;=\; \frac{d}{dr}\Big( n f(x) V_k r^k \Big) \;=\; n f(x) V_k \, k \, r^{\,k-1}.$$

This is the sentence the slide compresses to "neighbours in a small ball form a Poisson process with rate $\propto r^{k-1}$". Write $\theta := \log\big(n f(x) V_k\big)$, so $\log \lambda(r) = \theta + \log k + (k-1)\log r$.

1.2 The log-likelihood

For an inhomogeneous Poisson process observed on $[0,R]$ with event times $T_1 \lt T_2 \lt \cdots \lt T_{N(R)}$ (here $T_i$ = distance to the $i$-th nearest neighbour of $x$), the log-likelihood is

$$L(k, \theta) \;=\; \int_0^R \log \lambda(r) \, dN(r) \;-\; \int_0^R \lambda(r)\, dr.$$

Writing the integrated rate as $\int_0^R \lambda(r)\,dr = e^{\theta} R^{k}$ (absorbing the constants $n f(x) V_k$ into $\theta$),

$$L(k,\theta) \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{N(R)} \Big[\theta + \log k + (k-1)\log T_j\Big] \;-\; e^{\theta} R^{k}.$$

1.3 Solving the score equations

Differentiate in $\theta$:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} \;=\; N(R) - e^{\theta} R^{k} \;=\; 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{\hat\theta} R^{\hat k} = N(R).$$

Differentiate in $k$:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial k} \;=\; \frac{N(R)}{k} + \sum_{j=1}^{N(R)} \log T_j \;-\; e^{\theta} R^{k} \log R \;=\; 0.$$

Substitute $e^{\hat\theta} R^{\hat k} = N(R)$ into the second equation:

$$\frac{N(R)}{\hat k} \;=\; N(R)\log R - \sum_{j=1}^{N(R)} \log T_j \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{N(R)} \log \frac{R}{T_j}.$$

Hence

$$\hat k_R(x) \;=\; \left[ \frac{1}{N(R)} \sum_{j=1}^{N(R)} \log \frac{R}{T_j(x)} \right]^{-1}.$$

1.4 From radius $R$ to $K$ neighbours

In practice one fixes the number of neighbours $K$ rather than the radius. Set $R = T_K(x)$, the distance to the $K$-th nearest neighbour. Then $N(R) = K$, but the $K$-th term of the sum contributes $\log(T_K/T_K) = 0$, so only $K-1$ terms are informative; the standard bias correction replaces the divisor $K$ by $K-1$:

$$\widehat{\mathrm{LID}}_K(x) \;=\; \left[ \frac{1}{K-1} \sum_{i=1}^{K-1} \log \frac{T_K(x)}{T_i(x)} \right]^{-1}.$$

This is exactly the formula on the slide. Note the estimator is scale-free: it depends only on the ratios $T_K/T_i$, so it is invariant under a global rescaling of the ambient metric.

Reading the two failure modes. If all $K$ neighbours sit at nearly the same distance (points on a shell), every $\log(T_K/T_i) \approx 0$, the bracket $\to 0$, and $\widehat{\mathrm{LID}} \to \infty$. If the neighbours are tightly clustered near $x$ while $T_K$ is far, the logs are large, the bracket is large, and $\widehat{\mathrm{LID}} \to 0$. Both are visible in the deck's schematic.

1.5 Connection to the small-ball definition

The definition on the slide is

$$\mathrm{LID}(x) \;=\; \lim_{r \to 0^+} \frac{\log \Pr[\|X - x\| \le r]}{\log r}.$$

Under the local-uniformity assumption $\Pr[\|X-x\| \le r] \approx f(x) V_k r^k$, so the numerator is $k \log r + O(1)$ and the ratio $\to k$. The MLE above is the finite-sample counterpart: the Hill-type estimator of the tail exponent of the nearest-neighbour distance distribution.

2. Gaussian smoothing of a thin support — full proof

Theorem (deck). Let $q$ be a density supported on a $k$-dimensional affine subspace $V \subset \mathbb{R}^D$, and let $p_\sigma = q * \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_D)$. Decompose $x = (x_V, x_\perp)$ in an orthonormal basis adapted to $V$. Then

$$\nabla^2 \log p_\sigma(x) \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} \nabla^2 \log q_\sigma(x_V) & 0 \\ 0 & -\sigma^{-2} I_{D-k} \end{pmatrix}.$$

2.1 Step 1 — splitting the convolution

Write $y \in V$ for the integration variable, so $q(y)\,dy$ is a measure on $V$ only. Then

$$p_\sigma(x) = \int_V q(y) \, \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{D/2}} \exp\!\left( -\frac{\|x - y\|^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) dy.$$

Since $y \in V$, the vector $x - y$ decomposes orthogonally as $(x_V - y) \oplus x_\perp$, and Pythagoras gives $\|x-y\|^2 = \|x_V - y\|^2 + \|x_\perp\|^2$. The second term does not involve $y$ and factors out of the integral:

$$p_\sigma(x) \;=\; \underbrace{\left[ \int_V q(y) \frac{e^{-\|x_V-y\|^2/2\sigma^2}}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{k/2}}\,dy \right]}_{=\; q_\sigma(x_V)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{e^{-\|x_\perp\|^2/2\sigma^2}}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{(D-k)/2}}}_{\text{isotropic in } x_\perp}.$$

Key trick. The step above is where isotropy is used, and it is the only place. A Gaussian with covariance $\sigma^2 I$ is the unique kernel whose exponent is a squared Euclidean norm, hence the unique kernel that splits across every orthogonal decomposition. An anisotropic kernel splits only along its own eigendirections; if those are not aligned with $V$, the cross-terms survive and the Hessian is not block-diagonal.

2.2 Step 2 — logarithms and two derivatives

Taking logs turns the product into a sum of a function of $x_V$ alone and a function of $x_\perp$ alone:

$$\log p_\sigma(x) \;=\; \log q_\sigma(x_V) \;-\; \frac{\|x_\perp\|^2}{2\sigma^2} \;+\; \text{const}.$$

Because the variables are separated, all mixed second partials $\partial^2/\partial x_V \partial x_\perp$ vanish, so the Hessian is block diagonal. The $x_V$ block is $\nabla^2 \log q_\sigma(x_V)$ by definition. The $x_\perp$ block is the Hessian of a pure isotropic quadratic:

$$\nabla_{x_\perp}^2 \left( -\frac{\|x_\perp\|^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) \;=\; -\frac{1}{\sigma^2} I_{D-k}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

2.3 The two corollaries

Corollary 1 (spectral read-off of LID). The normal block contributes exactly $D-k$ eigenvalues equal to $-\sigma^{-2}$. Generically the tangential block $\nabla^2 \log q_\sigma(x_V)$ has no eigenvalue at exactly $-\sigma^{-2}$, so

$$\mathrm{LID} \;=\; k \;=\; D \;-\; \#\{\, i \,:\, \lambda_i(H) \approx -\sigma^{-2} \,\}.$$

The dimension estimate and the curvature estimate are therefore two readings of the same spectrum. This is the structural reason why Ross's LID-based detector and the SAIL sharpness-based detector agree on which prompts are memorized.

Corollary 2 (a fully memorized point). If the model has collapsed onto a single training image, $p_0 = \delta_{x_0}$, then $k = 0$, $p_\sigma = \mathcal{N}(x_0, \sigma^2 I_D)$, and

$$H = -\sigma^{-2} I_D, \qquad \operatorname{tr} H = -\frac{D}{\sigma^2},$$

the extreme value of the trace. Every finite-$k$ distribution has a strictly less negative trace, since the tangential block of a genuine density is flatter than $-\sigma^{-2}$ in at least the directions where $q_\sigma$ has spread. Memorization thus pushes the trace toward its floor, which is precisely what the empirical eigenvalue histograms show.

3. Simultaneous diagonalization of the two covariances

Lemmas 4.2 and 4.3 both assume the conditional and unconditional local Gaussians share a mode $\mu$ and have commuting covariances $\Sigma \Sigma_c = \Sigma_c \Sigma$. That assumption is doing real work and deserves a note.

Two symmetric matrices commute if and only if they are simultaneously diagonalizable: there is an orthonormal basis $\{u_i\}$ with $\Sigma u_i = \sigma_i^2 u_i$ and $\Sigma_c u_i = \sigma_{i,c}^2 u_i$. Then

$$H^\Delta := H_c - H = \Sigma^{-1} - \Sigma_c^{-1}, \qquad H^\Delta u_i = \left( \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2} - \frac{1}{\sigma_{i,c}^2} \right) u_i = (\Lambda_i - \Lambda_{i,c}) u_i.$$

Without commutation, $H^\Delta$ is still symmetric (a difference of symmetric matrices) so it still has a real spectrum, but its eigenvalues are no longer differences of the individual precisions, and the clean per-coordinate sums in the lemmas become trace expressions that do not factor. The lemmas remain directionally true — the metric still grows when the conditional collapses — but the closed forms are lost.

Interpretation of the assumption: conditioning on the prompt sharpens the local Gaussian along its existing principal directions rather than rotating them. This is reasonable when the prompt selects a mode of an already-present structure, and it is the regime in which the memorization signal is being measured.

The key identity

$$s^\Delta(x) \;=\; s_c(x) - s(x) \;=\; -\Sigma_c^{-1}(x-\mu) + \Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu) \;=\; H^\Delta (x-\mu).$$

Everything downstream is a consequence of this one line: the score gap is the curvature gap applied to the displacement. It is what lets a first-order quantity (a score difference) report second-order information (curvature), and it is why higher moments of the same object climb the derivative ladder.

4. Lemma A and the moment ladder

4.1 Lemma A

Statement. For $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ and any fixed matrix $A$, $\ \mathbb{E}\|Az\|^2 = \operatorname{tr}(A \Sigma A^\top)$.

Proof. $\|Az\|^2 = z^\top A^\top A z = \operatorname{tr}(A^\top A\, z z^\top)$ by cyclicity of the trace. Trace and expectation are both linear, so

$$\mathbb{E}\|Az\|^2 = \operatorname{tr}\!\big(A^\top A \, \mathbb{E}[zz^\top]\big) = \operatorname{tr}(A^\top A \Sigma) = \operatorname{tr}(A \Sigma A^\top). \qquad \blacksquare$$

Every one of the three SAIL lemmas is this identity applied to a different $A$.

4.2 The general moment ladder

Proposition. With $x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma_c)$ and $s^\Delta(x) = H^\Delta(x-\mu)$ as above, for every integer $m \ge 0$,

$$\mathbb{E}\left\| \big(H^\Delta\big)^m s^\Delta(x) \right\|^2 \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{D} \frac{\big(\Lambda_i - \Lambda_{i,c}\big)^{2m+2}}{\Lambda_{i,c}}.$$

Proof. Set $A = (H^\Delta)^{m+1}$ and $z = x - \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_c)$, so that $(H^\Delta)^m s^\Delta(x) = A z$. By Lemma A,

$$\mathbb{E}\|Az\|^2 = \operatorname{tr}\!\big(A \Sigma_c A^\top\big) = \operatorname{tr}\!\Big( \big(H^\Delta\big)^{m+1} \Sigma_c \big(H^\Delta\big)^{m+1} \Big),$$

using symmetry of $H^\Delta$. In the shared eigenbasis $\{u_i\}$ all three factors are diagonal, with entries $(\Lambda_i - \Lambda_{i,c})^{m+1}$, $\sigma_{i,c}^2 = 1/\Lambda_{i,c}$, and $(\Lambda_i - \Lambda_{i,c})^{m+1}$ respectively. The trace is the sum of the products of the diagonal entries. $\blacksquare$

Reading the ladder. $m=0$ gives $\sum_i (\Lambda_i - \Lambda_{i,c})^2 / \Lambda_{i,c}$, which is Lemma 4.2 — Wen's statistic. $m=1$ gives $\sum_i (\Lambda_i-\Lambda_{i,c})^4/\Lambda_{i,c}$, the proposed metric $\|H^\Delta s^\Delta\|^2$. Each rung raises the exponent by two, so each rung is more sensitive to the largest precision gap and less sensitive to the bulk. The trade-off is variance: higher powers of an estimated Hessian are noisier, which is why the ladder is not climbed indefinitely.

4.3 Why the gap is weak at the first step

At large $t$ both $p_t$ and $p_t(\cdot\mid c)$ are heavily smoothed toward $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_t^2 I)$, so $\Lambda_i \approx \Lambda_{i,c} \approx \sigma_t^{-2}$ for all $i$ and every rung of the ladder is near zero. The separation opens as $t$ decreases and the conditional collapses in a few directions. The empirical observation that the magnitude gap is already visible at the first sampling step is therefore stronger than the local-Gaussian model predicts, and is worth flagging in the talk as an experimental fact rather than a consequence of the theory.

5. Regularity for the Fisher / integration-by-parts identity

The deck proves $\ \mathbb{E}\|\nabla \log p\|^2 = -\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr} \nabla^2 \log p]\ $ in two lines. The steps are:

$$\int p\,(\partial_i \log p)^2 \;=\; \int (\partial_i p)(\partial_i \log p) \;=\; -\int p\, \partial_i^2 \log p,$$

the first equality from $\partial_i p = p\,\partial_i \log p$ and the second from integration by parts in the $i$-th coordinate, then summing over $i$.

Conditions under which each step is legitimate:

  • Positivity and smoothness. $p > 0$ everywhere and $p \in C^2$, so $\log p$ and its two derivatives exist pointwise. Gaussian-smoothed densities $p_\sigma$ satisfy this for any $\sigma > 0$, which is why the identity is safe to use at every noise level $t > 0$ even when $p_0$ is a point mass.
  • Vanishing boundary term. Integration by parts drops $\big[ p\,\partial_i \log p \big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = \big[\partial_i p\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}$. This vanishes when $\partial_i p \to 0$ at infinity, which holds for any density with sub-exponential tails — in particular for $p_\sigma$, whose tails are Gaussian.
  • Integrability. Both sides must be finite: $\mathbb{E}\|\nabla \log p\|^2 \lt \infty$ (finite Fisher information) and $\mathbb{E}|\operatorname{tr}\nabla^2 \log p| \lt \infty$. Again automatic for $p_\sigma$ with $\sigma > 0$; not automatic in the $\sigma \to 0$ limit, where both sides diverge like $D/\sigma^2$ (Corollary 2 above).

Consequence worth stating in the talk: the identity is not a Gaussian fact. Lemma 4.1's Gaussian computation is a special case of a general relation between expected squared score and expected trace curvature. This is what licenses reading a score-norm detector as a curvature detector on real, non-Gaussian $p_t$.

6. Cross-attention algebra behind the padding effect

The deck states the padding proposition; here is the computation in full.

6.1 Setup

CLIP's text encoder produces $77$ token embeddings $e_1, \dots, e_{77}$. Let $P$ index the real prompt tokens, let $\mathrm{eot}$ be the end-of-text position, and let $\mathcal{G}$ be the set of padding positions. Because the encoder is causal, position $\mathrm{eot}$ attends over all prompt tokens while padding positions attend over everything up to themselves — and in the standard implementation the padding embeddings are produced by the same trailing context, so

$$e_k \;=\; e_{\text{eot}} \qquad \text{for all } k \in \mathcal{G}.$$

6.2 Identical embeddings give identical keys and values

In the U-Net's cross-attention, keys and values are linear maps of the text embeddings: $k_j = W_K e_j$, $v_j = W_V e_j$. Equal embeddings therefore give equal keys and equal values:

$$k_j = k_{\text{eot}}, \quad v_j = v_{\text{eot}} \qquad \text{for all } j \in \mathcal{G} \cup \{\mathrm{eot}\}.$$

Equal keys give equal pre-softmax logits $\langle q, k_j \rangle / \sqrt{d}$, hence equal post-softmax weights. Writing $a_j$ for the attention weight the query would place on position $j$, all $|\mathcal{G}| + 1$ of these positions receive the same weight $a_{\text{eot}}$.

6.3 The aggregated weight

Since all of these positions also carry the same value vector $v_{\text{eot}}$, their contributions to the attention output add:

$$\sum_{j \in \mathcal{G} \cup \{\mathrm{eot}\}} a_j v_j \;=\; \Big( (1 + |\mathcal{G}|)\, a_{\text{eot}} \Big) v_{\text{eot}} \;=:\; A_{\text{eot}}\, v_{\text{eot}}.$$

So the effective attention mass on the single EoT direction is

$$A_{\text{eot}} \;=\; \big(1 + |\mathcal{G}|\big)\, a_{\text{eot}}.$$

Mechanism. With a short prompt, $|\mathcal{G}|$ is large and $A_{\text{eot}}$ dominates the conditioning signal: the U-Net is effectively conditioned on one vector rather than on a distributed representation of the caption. One vector selects one point in image space — a collapsed conditional, exactly the $\sigma_{i,c} \to 0$ regime of Section 3. This is why short prompts and duplicated captions are the two strongest empirical predictors of memorization.

6.4 The two mitigations in this notation

  • Mask the padding. Excluding $\mathcal{G}$ from the attention support sends $A_{\text{eot}} \mapsto a_{\text{eot}}$: the multiplier disappears entirely and the conditioning is redistributed over the prompt tokens. Removes the amplification but also removes whatever useful role the aggregate played during training.
  • Randomize the padding embeddings. Replacing the padding embeddings with $m$ distinct vectors breaks the equal-key argument for the other $|\mathcal{G}| - m$ positions, giving $A_{\text{eot}} \mapsto (1+m)\,a_{\text{eot}}$ with $m \ll |\mathcal{G}|$. Softer: it damps the multiplier without changing the attention support, so it can be applied at inference time on a pretrained model.

7. The finite-difference approximation of $H^\Delta s^\Delta$

The proposed metric needs $H^\Delta s^\Delta$, but the Hessian of the log-density is $D \times D$ with $D$ in the tens of thousands, so it is never formed. The deck's Taylor slide replaces it with a directional derivative; here is the justification.

By definition $H^\Delta(x) = \nabla_x s^\Delta(x)$: the curvature gap is the Jacobian of the score gap. For any direction $u$, the Jacobian-vector product is a directional derivative:

$$H^\Delta(x)\, u \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{s^\Delta(x + h u) - s^\Delta(x)}{h}.$$

Taking $u = s^\Delta(x)$ itself gives the quantity we want. A first-order finite difference at a small step $h$ therefore costs two evaluations of the score gap — that is, four network evaluations (conditional and unconditional at each of two points) — and no Hessian is ever materialized.

Error control: Taylor with remainder gives

$$\frac{s^\Delta(x+hu) - s^\Delta(x)}{h} \;=\; H^\Delta(x)\,u \;+\; \frac{h}{2}\,\nabla^2 s^\Delta(x)[u,u] \;+\; O(h^2),$$

so the bias is $O(h)$ while the variance of the difference quotient scales like $O(1/h)$ under score-estimation noise of fixed magnitude. The usual bias–variance compromise applies; a central difference $\big(s^\Delta(x+hu) - s^\Delta(x-hu)\big)/2h$ improves the bias to $O(h^2)$ at the cost of two more network evaluations.

8. Three definitions, one phenomenon — what not to conflate

The deck opens by separating three mathematical objects that are all called "memorization". Speaker notes on why the separation matters:

  • Extraction / eidetic ($\ell, \delta, k$). A statement about an attack: there exists a short prompt from which an algorithm recovers the training example to within tolerance $\delta$, and the example is $k$-rare in the training set. This is the definition that transfers verbatim from LLMs to diffusion models, because it never mentions the model's internal geometry — only a prompt, a generator, and a distance.
  • Similarity ($\mathrm{rep}_\tau$). A statement about an output: the generated image is within threshold $\tau$ of some training image in a fixed embedding. It depends on the embedding (SSCD, CLIP) and on $\tau$, and is silent about whether a short prompt could have found it. A model can score high on $\mathrm{rep}_\tau$ for reasons that are not memorization (genuinely common visual structure) and can memorize without triggering it (if the embedding is insensitive to the copied content).
  • Verbatim categories (MV / RV / TV). A statement about the relation between the output and the training set: one image, several images, or a shared template. This is the only one of the three that distinguishes copying a work from copying a layout, which is exactly the distinction copyright law turns on.

Practical consequence for reading the literature: a mitigation that lowers $\mathrm{rep}_\tau$ has not necessarily broken extractability, and a detector benchmarked on a public extraction prompt set has not necessarily been evaluated on template copying. Papers rarely say which object they mean; the slide's four-column table is the map to keep in mind.
