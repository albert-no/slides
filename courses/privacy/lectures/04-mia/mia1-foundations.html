Why Membership Inference Matters

Motivation, stakes, central question

The Central Question

Membership inference.

Given a released artifact and a target record, decide whether that record was trained on.

record $z$+ artifact $\theta$→ $b \in \{0, 1\}$
  • Weakest meaningful privacy violation.
  • Yet often catastrophic: membership itself is the secret.

Notation Fixed for This Lecture

SymbolMeaning
$D$Dataset, $n$ records drawn i.i.d. from $\mathcal{D}$
$M,\; \theta = M(D)$Randomized mechanism and its released artifact
$z$Target record under test
$\mathcal{A}$Adversary: maps $(\theta, z)$ to a guess in $\{0,1\}$
$\alpha, \beta, \pi$False positive rate, false negative rate, power $\pi = 1 - \beta$

Why This Is the Right Threat Model

Operational

Membership is a yes/no event. Measurable, falsifiable, auditable.

Minimal

One bit. If one bit leaks, richer leakage is plausible.

Universal

Any mechanism touching data admits the question.

Success here is a lower bound on leakage; failure is not a proof of safety.

When Membership Is the Secret

The Picture Behind Every Attack

Two distributions, one threshold. Everything else is detail.

Why This Paper Existed

  • GWAS published aggregate allele frequencies per cohort, for replication.
  • NIH-funded studies posted these tables publicly (dbGaP).
  • Conventional wisdom: averages over hundreds of people hide individuals.
  • Homer et al. asked whether that is actually true.

The first membership inference attack: from published frequencies, decide $z \in D$ or $z \notin D$.

Homer et al. 2008

The Probabilistic Model

Model (idealized single-locus).

Fix SNP $j$ with population frequency $p_j$. Each person's allele indicator is

$Y_{k,j} \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_j), \qquad k = 1, \dots, n, \quad \text{independent across } k, j.$

The mechanism publishes the cohort frequency

$M_j \;=\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} Y_{k,j}.$

Population frequency $p_j$ treated as known exactly from a large reference panel.

Homer et al. 2008

The Distance Statistic

For target $i$ at SNP $j$, compare distance to cohort against distance to population:

$D_j \;=\; \bigl|Y_{i,j} - M_j\bigr| \;-\; \bigl|Y_{i,j} - p_j\bigr|.$

Hypotheses.

$H_0$: target absent, $Y_{i,j}$ independent of $M_j$.   $H_1$: target is one of the $n$ contributors.

Negative $D_j$ means "closer to the cohort than to the population".

Homer et al. 2008

Key Trick: Remove the Absolute Value

Key trick.

For $Y \in \{0,1\}$ and any $a \in [0,1]$:   $|Y - a| = a + Y(1 - 2a)$.

$\begin{aligned} D_j &= \bigl(M_j + Y_{i,j}(1 - 2M_j)\bigr) - \bigl(p_j + Y_{i,j}(1 - 2p_j)\bigr) \\ &= (M_j - p_j) - 2Y_{i,j}(M_j - p_j) \\ &= (M_j - p_j)\,(1 - 2Y_{i,j}). \end{aligned}$

A product of two centered quantities. Mean and variance now follow in one line each.

Homer et al. 2008

Lemma 1 — Non-Member Has No Signal

Lemma 1.

Under $H_0$ (target not in the cohort), $\;\mathbb{E}[D_j] = 0$.

  • Under $H_0$ the target's allele $Y_{i,j}$ is independent of the cohort mean $M_j$.
  • $\mathbb{E}[M_j] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_k \mathbb{E}[Y_{k,j}] = p_j$, so $M_j - p_j$ is centered.
  • Independence factorizes the expectation of the product.
$\mathbb{E}[D_j] \stackrel{(3)}{=} \mathbb{E}[M_j - p_j]\cdot \mathbb{E}[1 - 2Y_{i,j}] \stackrel{(2)}{=} 0 \cdot (1 - 2p_j) = 0. \;\square$
Homer et al. 2008

Lemma 2 — Member Has a Negative Bias

Lemma 2.

Under $H_1$ (target is one of the $n$ contributors),

$\mathbb{E}[D_j] \;=\; -\,\frac{2\,p_j(1 - p_j)}{n}.$

The target's own allele sits inside $M_j$ with weight $1/n$. That self-contribution is the leak.

Homer et al. 2008

Proof of Lemma 2 — Steps 1 and 2

  • Split the cohort mean. Let $\bar{Y}_{-i,j}$ be the mean over the other $n-1$ people:
$M_j - p_j \;=\; \tfrac{1}{n}\bigl(Y_{i,j} - p_j\bigr) \;+\; \tfrac{n-1}{n}\bigl(\bar{Y}_{-i,j} - p_j\bigr).$
  • Kill the second term. $\bar{Y}_{-i,j}$ is independent of $Y_{i,j}$ with mean $p_j$, so its contribution vanishes.
Homer et al. 2008

Proof of Lemma 2 — Steps 3 and 4

  • Evaluate the self term. Use $Y^2 = Y$ for $Y \in \{0,1\}$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y] = p_j$:
$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\bigl[(Y_{i,j} - p_j)(1 - 2Y_{i,j})\bigr] &= \mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_{i,j} - 2Y_{i,j}^2 - p_j + 2p_j Y_{i,j}\bigr] \\ &= p_j - 2p_j - p_j + 2p_j^2 \;=\; -2p_j(1 - p_j). \end{aligned}$
  • Reassemble with the $1/n$ weight from Step 1.
$\mathbb{E}[D_j] \;=\; \tfrac{1}{n}\bigl(-2p_j(1-p_j)\bigr) + 0 \;=\; -\,\frac{2p_j(1-p_j)}{n}. \;\square$
Homer et al. 2008

The Sign Flip Is the Whole Attack

$0$ non-member: mean $0$ member: mean $-2p(1-p)/n$ per-SNP statistic $D_j$
Homer et al. 2008

Proposition 1 — Per-SNP Variance

Proposition 1.

Write $v_j = p_j(1-p_j)$. Under $H_0$, $\;\mathrm{Var}(D_j) = v_j / n$. Under $H_1$ the variance agrees to order $1/n$.

  • $(1 - 2Y)^2 = 1$ for $Y \in \{0,1\}$, so $D_j^2 = (M_j - p_j)^2$ exactly.
  • Hence $\mathbb{E}[D_j^2] = \mathrm{Var}(M_j) = v_j/n$ under $H_0$, where $\mathbb{E}[D_j] = 0$.
  • Under $H_1$ the squared mean is $4v_j^2/n^2 = O(1/n^2)$, negligible beside $v_j/n$.
Homer et al. 2008

Signal-to-Noise at One SNP

$\mathrm{SNR}_j \;=\; \frac{\bigl|\mathbb{E}_{H_1}[D_j] - \mathbb{E}_{H_0}[D_j]\bigr|}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(D_j)}} \;=\; \frac{2v_j/n}{\sqrt{v_j/n}} \;=\; 2\sqrt{\frac{v_j}{n}}.$
  • Cohort of $n = 1000$, $v_j = 0.2$: $\;\mathrm{SNR}_j \approx 0.028$.
  • Utterly undetectable at a single locus.

Signal decays like $1/\sqrt{n}$, but the number of loci is free. Sum them.

Homer et al. 2008

Aggregation: Many Weak Signals

separation grows like $\sqrt{m}$
Homer et al. 2008

The Aggregated Test Statistic

Sum over $m$ independent SNPs and standardize by the null deviation:

$S = \sum_{j=1}^{m} D_j, \qquad T = \frac{S}{\sqrt{m\bar{v}/n}}, \qquad \bar{v} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m v_j.$

Recall (Lindeberg CLT).

A standardized sum of independent, uniformly small, square-integrable terms converges to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$.

Each $|D_j| \le 1$ and $m \to \infty$: the Lindeberg condition holds.

Homer et al. 2008

Theorem 1 — Power of the Homer Attack

Theorem 1 (idealized model).

Reject $H_0$ when $T \le -z_{1-\alpha}$. As $m \to \infty$ this test has size $\alpha$ and power

$\pi \;=\; \Phi\!\left(\mu - z_{1-\alpha}\right), \qquad \mu \;=\; 2\sqrt{\frac{m\,\bar{v}}{n}}.$

$\Phi$ standard normal CDF; $z_{1-\alpha} = \Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)$.

Homer et al., PLoS Genetics 2008 (attack). Power analysis in the style of Sankararaman, Obozinski, Jordan and Halperin, Nature Genetics 2009.

Proof of Theorem 1 — Outline

Four steps
  1. Null law: $T \to \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, so the cutoff $-z_{1-\alpha}$ has size $\alpha$.
  2. Alternative mean: sum the per-SNP bias of Lemma 2.
  3. Alternative variance: unchanged to leading order by Proposition 1.
  4. Read off the power as a normal tail probability.

Only Step 2 carries the privacy content. Steps 1, 3, 4 are bookkeeping.

Proof of Theorem 1 — Steps 1 and 2

  • Null. Lemma 1 and Proposition 1 give $S$ mean $0$, variance $m\bar{v}/n$. The CLT gives $T \to \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, hence size $\alpha$.
  • Alternative mean. Lemma 2 applies at every locus, so
$\mathbb{E}_{H_1}[S] = -\sum_{j=1}^m \frac{2v_j}{n} = -\frac{2m\bar{v}}{n}.$

Proof of Theorem 1 — Steps 3 and 4

  • Alternative variance. Proposition 1 gives $\mathrm{Var}_{H_1}(S) = m\bar{v}/n + O(m/n^2)$, so $T \to \mathcal{N}(-\mu, 1)$ with
$\mu = \frac{2m\bar{v}/n}{\sqrt{m\bar{v}/n}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{m\bar{v}}{n}}.$
  • Power. Shift the normal variable and use $\Phi(-x) = 1 - \Phi(x)$.
$\pi = \Pr_{H_1}\!\left[T \le -z_{1-\alpha}\right] = \Phi\!\left(-z_{1-\alpha} + \mu\right). \;\square$

Proof of Theorem 1 — Recap

$\begin{aligned} \pi &\stackrel{(1)}{=} \Pr_{H_1}\bigl[T \le -z_{1-\alpha}\bigr] \\ &\stackrel{(2)}{=} \Pr\bigl[\mathcal{N}(-\mu, 1) \le -z_{1-\alpha}\bigr] \\ &\stackrel{(3)}{=} \Phi\bigl(\mu - z_{1-\alpha}\bigr) \\ &\stackrel{(4)}{=} \Phi\!\left(2\sqrt{m\bar{v}/n} \;-\; z_{1-\alpha}\right). \;\square \end{aligned}$

(1) test definition  ·  (2) Steps 2 and 3  ·  (3) normal symmetry  ·  (4) Step 3.

Reading the Theorem

$\mu = 2\sqrt{\frac{m\bar{v}}{n}}$

More loci

$\mu$ grows like $\sqrt{m}$. The adversary buys power with array density.

Bigger cohort

$\mu$ shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$. Dilution is the only defence here.

Rarer alleles

$\bar{v} = \overline{p(1-p)}$ peaks at $p = 1/2$. Common SNPs leak most.

Power depends on $m/n$ only. Doubling the cohort is undone by doubling the array.

Corollary 1 — Loci Needed

Corollary 1.

To reach size $\alpha$ and power $1-\beta$ it suffices that

$m \;\ge\; \frac{n\,\bigl(z_{1-\alpha} + z_{1-\beta}\bigr)^2}{4\,\bar{v}}.$
  • Rearrange $\Phi(\mu - z_{1-\alpha}) \ge 1 - \beta$, i.e. $\mu \ge z_{1-\alpha} + z_{1-\beta}$.
  • Substitute $\mu = 2\sqrt{m\bar{v}/n}$ and solve for $m$. $\;\square$

What the Model Idealizes

  • Linkage disequilibrium. Nearby SNPs correlate; effective $m$ is smaller than the array size.
  • Finite reference panel. Estimating $p_j$ adds noise, inflating the loci requirement.
  • Population structure. Ancestry mismatch between panel and cohort biases $D_j$.
  • Diploidy. Two copies per locus; the Bernoulli becomes Binomial(2, p).

Each weakens the attack quantitatively. None removes the sign flip.

Why membership inference is the right notion

The Core Principle

Aggregate statistics leak individual membership.

In genomics

Allele frequencies identify participants.

In machine learning

Parameters and predictions identify training records.

The mechanism changed. The self-contribution term did not.

Recap: Reconstruction Attacks

Reconstruction.

The adversary outputs $\hat{D}$ agreeing with $D$ on a $1 - o(1)$ fraction of records.

$\mathcal{A}\bigl(M(D)\bigr) \;\to\; \hat{D} \approx D.$
  • Very strong goal: recovers the records themselves.
  • Dinur and Nissim: reconstruction from $O(n)$ noisy linear queries.

Is Defeating Reconstruction Enough?

Counterexample.

Let $M(D) = D_I$ where $I$ is uniform on $\{1, \dots, n\}$: publish one record, chosen at random.

Defeats reconstruction

One record reveals nothing about the other $n-1$.

Destroys privacy

Whoever is published is exposed with certainty.

Defeating reconstruction is not privacy. A weaker attack still succeeds.

The Sampling Mechanism, Quantified

Neighboring datasets $D_1 = D \cup \{x\}$ and $D_0 = D \setminus \{x\}$, and the event $S = \{x\}$:

$\Pr\bigl[M(D_1) \in S\bigr] = \frac{1}{n+1}, \qquad \Pr\bigl[M(D_0) \in S\bigr] = 0.$
  • No finite $e^{\varepsilon}$ multiplier can bridge a positive number and zero.
  • The mechanism satisfies no $(\varepsilon, 0)$-DP guarantee at all.

Distinguishability catches what reconstruction misses.

Cryptographic Precedent: IND-CPA

The Right Notion

Reconstruction

Recover the data. Failure of the attack does not imply privacy.

Membership inference

Distinguish two neighboring worlds. Failure does imply a DP-style guarantee.

Membership inference is the operational dual of differential privacy.

The MI Game and the Optimal Attack

Advantage, total variation, Neyman-Pearson

The Membership Inference Game

Challenger versus adversary
  1. Challenger fixes a dataset $D$ and a target record $x \notin D$.
  2. Challenger draws a secret bit $b$ uniform on $\{0,1\}$.
  3. Sets $D_1 = D \cup \{x\}$ if $b = 1$, else $D_0 = D$.
  4. Runs the mechanism and hands $\theta = M(D_b)$ to the adversary.
  5. Adversary outputs $\hat{b} = \mathcal{A}(\theta, x)$ and wins if $\hat{b} = b$.

Everything except $b$ is public: $D$, $x$, the mechanism, its hyperparameters.

The Game as a Diagram

$D_1 = D \cup \{x\}$ $D_0 = D$ $b = 1$ $b = 0$ $\theta$ $\theta$ outputs $\hat{b}$

The Two Worlds

Write $P_1$ and $P_0$ for the laws of the released artifact in the two worlds:

$P_1 = \mathrm{law}\bigl(M(D_1)\bigr), \qquad P_0 = \mathrm{law}\bigl(M(D_0)\bigr).$

Recall ($(\varepsilon,\delta)$-differential privacy).

For every measurable $S$ and every neighboring pair, in both directions,

$\Pr[M(D_1) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[M(D_0)\in S] + \delta.$

Differential privacy constrains the pair $(P_1, P_0)$. Membership inference probes it.

Definition: MI Advantage

Definition (advantage).

$\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}) \;=\; \Bigl|\Pr\bigl[\mathcal{A} = 1 \mid b = 1\bigr] \;-\; \Pr\bigl[\mathcal{A} = 1 \mid b = 0\bigr]\Bigr|.$
  • $\mathrm{Adv} = 0$: the guess is independent of the secret bit.
  • $\mathrm{Adv} = 1$: perfect membership recovery.

Calibrated so that always guessing $1$ scores zero, unlike raw accuracy.

Proposition 2 — Advantage Is a Rate Gap

Proposition 2.

$\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}) = \bigl|\mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR}\bigr| = 2\,\Pr[\hat{b} = b] - 1.$
  • By definition $\mathrm{TPR} = \Pr[\mathcal{A}=1 \mid b=1]$ and $\mathrm{FPR} = \Pr[\mathcal{A}=1 \mid b=0]$, giving the first equality.
  • The bit is uniform, so $\Pr[\hat{b}=b] = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{TPR} + \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - \mathrm{FPR})$.
  • Rearranging gives $2\Pr[\hat{b}=b] - 1 = \mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR}$. $\;\square$

Assuming the adversary is at least as good as chance, so the gap is non-negative.

Recall: Total Variation Distance

Recall.

For distributions $P_1, P_0$ with densities $p_1, p_0$,

$\mathrm{TV}(P_1, P_0) \;=\; \sup_{S} \bigl|P_1(S) - P_0(S)\bigr| \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\int \bigl|p_1 - p_0\bigr|.$

The supremum runs over measurable events. It is attained at $S^\star = \{p_1 \gt p_0\}$.

Proposition 3 — Best Advantage Is Total Variation

Proposition 3.

$\sup_{\mathcal{A}} \; \mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}) \;=\; \mathrm{TV}(P_1, P_0),$

the supremum over all measurable, possibly randomized adversaries.

Membership privacy is exactly a statistical distance. Nothing about learning enters yet.

Proof of Proposition 3 — Upper Bound

  • A randomized adversary is a function $\phi(\theta) = \Pr[\mathcal{A}(\theta) = 1] \in [0,1]$.
  • Both conditional probabilities are integrals of the same $\phi$ against different laws:
$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}) &= \left|\int \phi\,(p_1 - p_0)\right| = \left|\int \phi\,(p_1 - p_0)^{+} - \int \phi\,(p_0 - p_1)^{+}\right| \\ &\le \max\left\{\int (p_1 - p_0)^{+},\; \int (p_0 - p_1)^{+}\right\}. \end{aligned}$
  • Both densities integrate to one, so both integrals equal $\tfrac{1}{2}\int|p_1 - p_0| = \mathrm{TV}(P_1,P_0)$.

Proof of Proposition 3 — Achievability

  • Take the deterministic adversary $\mathcal{A}^\star(\theta) = \mathbb{1}\bigl[p_1(\theta) \gt p_0(\theta)\bigr]$, i.e. $\phi = \mathbb{1}_{S^\star}$.
  • Then the integral above is attained exactly:
$\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}^\star) = \int_{S^\star} (p_1 - p_0) = \int (p_1 - p_0)^{+} = \mathrm{TV}(P_1, P_0). \;\square$

The optimal adversary thresholds the likelihood ratio at one. Foreshadowing Neyman-Pearson.

Why Loss Is the Natural Statistic

  • Training minimizes loss on members and nothing else.
  • So the member-versus-non-member gap is largest, by construction, in the loss.
  • Under a Bayesian posterior over parameters, membership depends on $\theta$ only through the target's loss.

The loss is a sufficient statistic in the idealized model. Everything downstream thresholds it.

Sablayrolles, Douze, Schmid, Ollivier and Jegou, "White-box vs Black-box: Bayes Optimal Strategies for Membership Inference", ICML 2019

Neyman-Pearson Setup

Observe $T$, with density $p_0$ under $H_0$ and $p_1$ under $H_1$. A test $\phi : \mathcal{T} \to [0,1]$ rejects with probability $\phi(t)$.

$\alpha(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_0\bigl[\phi(T)\bigr] \quad \text{(size)}, \qquad \pi(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_1\bigl[\phi(T)\bigr] \quad \text{(power)}.$

Goal.

Among all $\phi$ with $\alpha(\phi) \le \alpha$, find one maximizing $\pi(\phi)$.

Theorem 2 — Neyman-Pearson Lemma

Theorem 2.

Fix $\alpha \in (0,1)$. There exist $\tau \ge 0$ and $\gamma \in [0,1]$ such that

$\phi^\star(t) = \begin{cases} 1 & p_1(t) \gt \tau\, p_0(t), \\ \gamma & p_1(t) = \tau\, p_0(t), \\ 0 & p_1(t) \lt \tau\, p_0(t), \end{cases}$

has $\alpha(\phi^\star) = \alpha$, and $\pi(\phi^\star) \ge \pi(\phi)$ for every $\phi$ with $\alpha(\phi) \le \alpha$.

Proof of Theorem 2 — Outline

Three moves
  1. Calibrate. Choose $(\tau, \gamma)$ so the test has size exactly $\alpha$.
  2. Sign trick. Show one product is pointwise non-negative.
  3. Integrate. The integral rearranges into power minus $\tau$ times size.

The whole lemma is one inequality that holds pointwise before any integration.

Proof of Theorem 2 — Step 1, Calibration

  • Let $\Lambda(t) = p_1(t)/p_0(t)$ and define the null survival function $G(\tau) = \Pr_0\bigl[\Lambda(T) \gt \tau\bigr]$.
  • $G$ is non-increasing and right-continuous with $G(\infty) = 0$. Set $\tau = \inf\{u \ge 0 : G(u) \le \alpha\}$.
  • Then $G(\tau) \le \alpha \le G(\tau^-)$, so the jump at $\tau$ covers the shortfall. Choose
$\gamma = \frac{\alpha - G(\tau)}{\Pr_0[\Lambda(T) = \tau]} \quad \text{when the denominator is positive, else } \gamma = 0.$

By construction $\alpha(\phi^\star) = G(\tau) + \gamma \Pr_0[\Lambda = \tau] = \alpha$.

Proof of Theorem 2 — Step 2, the Sign Trick

Key trick.

For every competitor $\phi$ and every $t$,

$\bigl(\phi^\star(t) - \phi(t)\bigr)\bigl(p_1(t) - \tau\, p_0(t)\bigr) \;\ge\; 0.$
  • If $p_1(t) \gt \tau p_0(t)$: then $\phi^\star(t) = 1 \ge \phi(t)$, so both factors are non-negative.
  • If $p_1(t) \lt \tau p_0(t)$: then $\phi^\star(t) = 0 \le \phi(t)$, so both factors are non-positive.
  • If $p_1(t) = \tau p_0(t)$: the second factor vanishes.

Proof of Theorem 2 — Step 3, Integrate

Integrate the pointwise inequality over $\mathcal{T}$ and split the two terms:

$\begin{aligned} 0 &\le \int \bigl(\phi^\star - \phi\bigr)\bigl(p_1 - \tau p_0\bigr) \\ &= \underbrace{\int (\phi^\star - \phi)\,p_1}_{\pi(\phi^\star) - \pi(\phi)} \;-\; \tau \underbrace{\int (\phi^\star - \phi)\,p_0}_{\alpha(\phi^\star) - \alpha(\phi)}. \end{aligned}$

Rearranged: $\;\pi(\phi^\star) - \pi(\phi) \;\ge\; \tau\bigl(\alpha(\phi^\star) - \alpha(\phi)\bigr)$.

Proof of Theorem 2 — Step 4, Use the Size Constraint

  • The competitor obeys $\alpha(\phi) \le \alpha = \alpha(\phi^\star)$, so $\alpha(\phi^\star) - \alpha(\phi) \ge 0$.
  • The multiplier $\tau$ is non-negative, so Step 3's right-hand side is too.
$\pi(\phi^\star) - \pi(\phi) \;\ge\; \tau\bigl(\alpha(\phi^\star) - \alpha(\phi)\bigr) \;\ge\; 0. \;\square$

Randomization at the boundary exists only to hit size $\alpha$ exactly on discrete data.

Proof of Theorem 2 — Recap

$\begin{aligned} \pi(\phi^\star) - \pi(\phi) &\stackrel{(1)}{=} \int (\phi^\star - \phi)\,p_1 \\ &\stackrel{(2)}{=} \int (\phi^\star - \phi)(p_1 - \tau p_0) \;+\; \tau\bigl(\alpha(\phi^\star) - \alpha(\phi)\bigr) \\ &\stackrel{(3)}{\ge} 0 \;+\; \tau\bigl(\alpha - \alpha(\phi)\bigr) \;\stackrel{(4)}{\ge}\; 0. \end{aligned}$

(1) definition of power  ·  (2) add and subtract  ·  (3) sign trick  ·  (4) size constraint.

The Rejection Region

reject $H_0$ accept $H_0$ $p_0$ $p_1$ $\Lambda(t) = \tau$ observed statistic $t$

Corollary 2 — Every Optimal Attack Is a Ratio Test

Corollary 2.

An attack optimal at some operating point must agree almost everywhere with a threshold rule on

$\Lambda(\theta) \;=\; \frac{p_1(\theta)}{p_0(\theta)} \;=\; \frac{\text{density of the artifact when } x \text{ was trained on}}{\text{density when it was not}}.$

Immediate from Theorem 2, applied at the attack's own size.

What Corollary 2 Buys Us

Design

Attack research reduces to estimating $\Lambda$ well.

Evaluation

A single accuracy number cannot capture a whole curve.

Defence

Bounding $\Lambda$ uniformly is exactly what differential privacy does.

Differential Privacy as an Upper Bound

The ROC wedge and what it does not say

Recall: Approximate Differential Privacy

Recall ($(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP).

$M$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-differentially private if for all neighboring $D, D'$ and all measurable $S$,

$\Pr\bigl[M(D) \in S\bigr] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr\bigl[M(D') \in S\bigr] \;+\; \delta.$
  • Applies to both orderings of the pair, and to every event.
  • $\varepsilon$ caps a multiplicative gap; $\delta$ allows a small additive failure.

Recasting DP as a Testing Constraint

Fix an adversary and let $S$ be its acceptance region for $\hat{b} = 1$:

$\alpha = \mathrm{FPR} = P_0(S), \qquad \beta = \mathrm{FNR} = 1 - P_1(S).$

Key trick.

Apply the DP inequality twice: once to $S$, once to $S^{c}$. Two events, two constraints.

Theorem 3 — DP Constrains the Testing Region

Theorem 3 (Wasserman and Zhou; Kairouz, Oh and Viswanath).

If $M$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP, then every adversary distinguishing $P_1$ from $P_0$ satisfies

$\alpha + e^{\varepsilon}\beta \;\ge\; 1 - \delta \qquad \text{and} \qquad \beta + e^{\varepsilon}\alpha \;\ge\; 1 - \delta.$

Wasserman and Zhou, JASA 2010; Kairouz, Oh and Viswanath, ICML 2015.

Proof of Theorem 3 — First Constraint

  • Apply DP in the direction $P_1 \le e^{\varepsilon}P_0 + \delta$ to the acceptance event $S$:
$P_1(S) \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon} P_0(S) + \delta.$
  • Substitute $P_1(S) = 1 - \beta$ and $P_0(S) = \alpha$, then rearrange:
$1 - \beta \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\alpha + \delta \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \beta + e^{\varepsilon}\alpha \;\ge\; 1 - \delta.$

One event gives one edge. The mirrored edge needs the complement.

Proof of Theorem 3 — Second Constraint

  • Apply DP in the direction $P_0 \le e^{\varepsilon}P_1 + \delta$ to the complement $S^{c}$:
$P_0(S^{c}) \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon} P_1(S^{c}) + \delta.$
  • Substitute $P_0(S^{c}) = 1 - \alpha$ and $P_1(S^{c}) = \beta$, then rearrange:
$1 - \alpha \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\beta + \delta \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \alpha + e^{\varepsilon}\beta \;\ge\; 1 - \delta. \;\square$

Two events, two edges. Together they carve out the achievable wedge.

Corollary 3 — The ROC Cap

Corollary 3.

Every point of every attack ROC curve against an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP mechanism obeys

$\mathrm{TPR} \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{FPR} \;+\; \delta.$
  • Write $\mathrm{TPR} = 1 - \beta$ and $\mathrm{FPR} = \alpha$ in the constraint $1 - \beta \le e^{\varepsilon}\alpha + \delta$.
  • By symmetry the mirrored cap $1 - \mathrm{FPR} \le e^{\varepsilon}(1 - \mathrm{TPR}) + \delta$ also holds. $\;\square$

The DP Wedge

DP ceiling achievable attacks chance $\delta$

Red edge: $\mathrm{TPR} = e^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{FPR} + \delta$. No attack reaches above it.

Corollary 4 — Advantage Bound

Corollary 4.

Against an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP mechanism, every adversary has

$\mathrm{Adv}(\mathcal{A}) \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon} - 1 + \delta.$
  • Let $q = \Pr[\mathcal{A}=1 \mid b=1]$ and $p = \Pr[\mathcal{A}=1 \mid b=0]$, so $q \le e^{\varepsilon}p + \delta$.
  • Then $q - p \le (e^{\varepsilon}-1)p + \delta \le e^{\varepsilon}-1+\delta$, using $p \le 1$.
  • The reverse direction bounds $p - q$ identically, so the absolute value is bounded. $\;\square$

What the Bound Does Not Say — Scope

  • Worst-case over datasets. It quantifies over every neighboring pair, however adversarial.
  • Worst-case over records. A typical record is usually far safer than the ceiling.

A guarantee about the hardest case, not about your case.

What the Bound Does Not Say — Direction

  • It bounds attacks, not measurements. A weak empirical attack proves nothing about $\varepsilon$.
  • It says nothing about tightness. Whether any attack meets the ceiling depends on the mechanism.

The gap between ceiling and measured attack performance is the subject of the later lectures.

The Bound Runs Both Ways

Upper bound

A proof of $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP caps every present and future attack.

Lower bound

An attack achieving $(\mathrm{FPR}, \mathrm{TPR})$ certifies $\varepsilon \ge \log\frac{\mathrm{TPR}-\delta}{\mathrm{FPR}}$.

Contrapositive of Corollary 3: a successful attack is a privacy audit.

Auditing in One Line

Run the strongest available attack, record its best operating point, and invert the cap:

$\hat{\varepsilon}_{\text{lower}} \;=\; \log\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{TPR} - \delta}{\mathrm{FPR}}\right).$
  • Catches implementation bugs: a claimed $\varepsilon = 1$ refuted by an audit reading $\hat{\varepsilon} = 6$.
  • Only ever a lower bound. Weak attacks give weak certificates.
  • Sharpest in the low-FPR corner, where the ratio is most sensitive.

Evaluating Membership Inference

ROC, AUC, TPR at low FPR

An Attack Is a Curve, Not a Number

A score-based attack computes $T(\theta, z)$ and thresholds it. Sweeping the threshold traces

$\mathrm{FPR}(\tau) = \Pr_0\bigl[T \gt \tau\bigr], \qquad \mathrm{TPR}(\tau) = \Pr_1\bigl[T \gt \tau\bigr].$

Definition (ROC).

The set $\bigl\{(\mathrm{FPR}(\tau), \mathrm{TPR}(\tau)) : \tau \in \mathbb{R}\bigr\}$, running from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$.

Corollary 2 said the optimal family is generated by thresholding $\Lambda$. This is that family.

The ROC Plane

one threshold $\tau$ attack chance perfect

AUC in Two Forms

Integral form. Area under the curve just drawn:

$\mathrm{AUC} \;=\; \int_0^1 \mathrm{TPR}\bigl(\mathrm{FPR}\bigr)\, d\,\mathrm{FPR}.$

Probabilistic form. Draw an independent member score $T^{+}$ and non-member score $T^{-}$:

$\mathrm{AUC} \;=\; \Pr\bigl[T^{+} \gt T^{-}\bigr].$

These look unrelated. They are the same number.

Proposition 4 — The Two Forms Agree

Proposition 4.

If $T^{-} \sim P_0$ has a continuous distribution and $T^{+} \sim P_1$ is independent of it, then

$\int_0^1 \mathrm{TPR}\bigl(\mathrm{FPR}\bigr)\,d\,\mathrm{FPR} \;=\; \Pr\bigl[T^{+} \gt T^{-}\bigr].$

Key trick: reparametrize the curve by the threshold, and read the result as an expectation.

Proof of Proposition 4 — Steps 1 and 2

  • Reparametrize by the threshold: $u = 1 - F_0(\tau)$ gives $du = -f_0(\tau)\,d\tau$, and $\tau : +\infty \to -\infty$ sweeps $u : 0 \to 1$.
$\int_0^1 \mathrm{TPR}\,du \;=\; \int_{+\infty}^{-\infty} \bigl(1 - F_1(\tau)\bigr)\bigl(-f_0(\tau)\bigr)\,d\tau \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \bigl(1 - F_1(\tau)\bigr) f_0(\tau)\,d\tau.$
  • Recognize the survival function. $1 - F_1(\tau) = \Pr\bigl[T^{+} \gt \tau\bigr]$.

Proof of Proposition 4 — Step 3

  • Tower property. Average that survival function against the law of $T^{-}$:
$\begin{aligned} \int \Pr\bigl[T^{+} \gt \tau\bigr] f_0(\tau)\,d\tau &= \mathbb{E}_{T^{-}}\Bigl[\Pr\bigl[T^{+} \gt T^{-} \,\bigm|\, T^{-}\bigr]\Bigr] \\ &= \Pr\bigl[T^{+} \gt T^{-}\bigr]. \;\square \end{aligned}$

With atoms in the score distribution the identity becomes $\mathrm{AUC} = \Pr[T^{+} \gt T^{-}] + \tfrac{1}{2}\Pr[T^{+} = T^{-}]$.

Proposition 5 — AUC Is an Average-Case Quantity

Proposition 5.

AUC is the chance a uniformly random member outranks a uniformly random non-member. Every threshold is weighted equally.

But the MIA threat model is worst-case per record: identify some individuals with near-certainty.

An average-case statistic scoring a worst-case threat model. The mismatch is structural, not cosmetic.

Why AUC Misses MIA Risk

  • Most of the area sits at high FPR, where no auditor operates.
  • A curve hugging the diagonal can outscore one spiking near the origin.
  • Population averages hide the vulnerable subpopulation entirely.

Two attacks at 60% balanced accuracy: one flags 10% of members at 0.1% FPR, the other none.

Only the first is a real privacy failure. Only the first would change a deployment decision.

The Right Metric

Report TPR at fixed low FPR.

Power at $\mathrm{FPR} \in \{10^{-3}, 10^{-4}, 10^{-5}\}$, plotted on log-log axes.

  • Matches the threat model: high-confidence identification of individuals.
  • Matches the DP bound: Corollary 3 is tightest exactly in this corner.
  • Matches Corollary 2: reports the curve, not one summary of it.
