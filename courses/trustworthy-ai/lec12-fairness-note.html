1. Fairness

Where bias enters · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈0.5 min

Today is about fairness: where bias gets into a machine-learning system, how we measure it, and what it costs to fix it.

One idea runs through the whole lecture. Every fairness claim is a claim about a rate, and every rate has a denominator. When people disagree about whether a system is fair, they are often computing different rates.

Key takeaway: Every fairness claim is a rate with a denominator.

3. Section 01 — Where Bias Enters

Where bias enters · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈1.5 min

We start before any metric: how does a model become biased in the first place? First, why the decision matters: a small lending picture, then a real case.

Key takeaway: Bias usually arrives with the data, not the algorithm.

S74. Who Gets the Opportunity?

Where bias enters · ≈0.75 min · elapsed ≈?

Before any study, why should we care? Take an illustrative lending decision. An applicant asks for a loan; a model produces a score; the lender compares the score with a cutoff. The score has become an allocation: some people are selected and get a loan, others are denied.

Notice the two ways this can go wrong. Approve someone who then does not repay, and the lender loses money. Deny someone who would have repaid, and the applicant loses a chance at credit and at building a repayment record. These are different errors, borne by different people. A fairness question always starts with which harm we are measuring and for whom.

Key takeaway: A score becomes a decision about who gets an opportunity, and its two errors hurt different people.

The figure

Diagram: applicant → score from a model → decision (score vs cutoff), branching to "selected: loan approved" (Yonsei blue) and "not selected: denied". Each branch names its error: did not repay (lender's loss) or would have repaid (applicant's lost chance).

Assumptions

Illustrative example of the lending setting used throughout this lecture; it is not a measured case and claims no prevalence of either error.

4. Predicting Cost Missed Medical Need

Where bias enters · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈3 min

Now a real case. A widely used US health algorithm decided which patients got extra care. It predicted future health-care cost, as a stand-in for health need, and patients at the top of the score were enrolled in a care-management program. But less money is spent on Black patients' care.

Read the figure. The x-axis is the algorithm's risk-score percentile; the y-axis is the mean number of active chronic illnesses. Purple dashed is Black patients, orange solid is White patients. At every score, the purple curve sits above: at the same risk score, Black patients were considerably sicker. The dashed vertical lines are the program's thresholds: the 55th percentile (referred for screening) and the 97th (defaulted into the program).

The authors also simulated a remedy. The share of Black patients auto-identified for extra care would rise from 17.7% to 46.5%. That is a simulation of who would be selected, not a deployed intervention or a measured health benefit. The model wasn't miscalibrated for cost; cost was the wrong target. We'll come back to why: the label is one of three places bias enters.

Key takeaway: A proxy label can carry inequity straight into the model.

The figure

(2019), Fig. 1A, unmodified crop of the authors' eScholarship copy (panel B and the caption omitted): mean number of active chronic conditions versus percentile of algorithm risk score, by race. × marks are risk percentiles by race; circles are risk deciles with 95% confidence intervals clustered by patient. The enlarged key beside the image restates the axes and the legend.

The earlier version of this slide showed two bars instead: 17.7% (cost proxy, actual algorithm) and 46.5% (simulated remedy), the Black share of patients auto-identified for extra care. Those numbers are retained above.

Setup, model, date

Obermeyer, Powers, Vogeli and Mullainathan, Science 2019, studying a commercial algorithm used by a large US health system.

What this establishes

Using cost as the label understated Black patients' need at a given score.

What this does not establish

The 46.5% is a simulation of the selected share, not a deployed outcome, a population prevalence, or evidence that the extra care improved health.

5. Past Records Can Shape Decisions

Where bias enters · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈4 min

Why did that happen? A model learns patterns from its training data, and that data records past decisions, past outcomes and past records: for a lender, who was approved. The labels come from those records, the model learns the pattern, and new decisions can repeat it.

So a learned pattern is not automatically the right target. If the past carried a disparity, it can travel along this pathway. Whether a particular model reproduces, shrinks or amplifies a disparity has to be measured; it is not automatic. That is also why an accurate model can still be unfair: accuracy is measured against the same past.

Key takeaway: A model can reproduce or amplify disparities in its data; whether it does is measured, not assumed.

The figure

Diagram: past records (who was approved) → training data (labels from records) → model (learns the pattern) → new decisions (can repeat it), under a dashed bracket marking a possible pathway for a past disparity.

Assumptions

An intuition, not a claim about any specific system.

6. Three Places Bias Enters

Where bias enters · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈5.5 min

Here is the whole process: world → data → labels → model → decisions. Three entry points are marked on it. One, sampling: biased data, where some groups are under-sampled or missing. Two, target choice: biased labels, where the target itself records past prejudice. Three, feedback: decisions change who gets observed, so the return arrow is tomorrow's data, not a new kind of evidence.

The next slides take each in turn with an example.

Key takeaway: Data, labels, and feedback loops.

The figure

Process map world → data → labels → model → decisions. Tag 1 (sampling) on data, tag 2 (target choice) on labels; a dashed return arrow from decisions back into data carries tag 3, "feedback: decisions shape future data".

The three entry points (from the earlier cards)

  • 1 · Biased data: some groups under-sampled or missing.

  • 3 · Feedback loops: predictions reshape future data.

7. Benchmark Composition Can Hide Gaps

Where bias enters · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈7 min

Who is in the data matters. Gender Shades measured two widely used face benchmarks: IJB-A was 79.6% lighter-skinned and Adience 86.2%. The authors built a balanced benchmark, the Pilot Parliaments Benchmark, at 53.6%.

Notice what this does and doesn't show. Composition alone doesn't tell you what causes an error gap. But an unbalanced test set can hide one: if almost every test face is lighter-skinned, a high overall accuracy says little about darker-skinned faces. A balanced evaluation can expose groups the skewed one overlooks.

Key takeaway: An unbalanced benchmark can hide a gap.

The figure

Setup, model, date

What this establishes

The two older benchmarks were heavily skewed toward lighter skin.

What this does not establish

That the skew caused the error gaps reported later (slide 47).

8. A Proxy Can Answer the Wrong Question

Where bias enters · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈8 min

Often we don't measure what we care about. A lender wants to know whether someone will repay: that is the actual outcome. But for people who were never approved, repayment is never observed. So suppose the recorded label is Y = "was approved before". It is available for everyone, but it answers a different question.

Take one past applicant who was denied but would have repaid. The actual outcome is positive; the recorded label is Y = 0. The label calls a would-be repayer a negative, and yesterday's decisions become today's ground truth. So choosing the target variable is itself a fairness decision.

Key takeaway: The choice of label is a fairness decision.

The figure

Two panels separated by ≠: what we want (actual outcome: will this person repay?, unseen for anyone denied) vs what we record (the label Y = approved before, seen for everyone). Below, one illustrative denied applicant: actual outcome would have repaid (dashed green, unobserved); recorded Y = 0 (hollow circle = recorded negative).

Assumptions

Illustrative example, not a specific system. A past-approval label does not by itself establish prejudice in any real dataset; whether it carries one depends on how past approvals were made. Target design is a choice to examine, not a default.

9. Missing Outcomes Can Reinforce Exclusion

Where bias enters · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈9 min

A loop. Suppose a model denies loans to group A more often than group B. Denied applicants never get a loan, so they never build a repayment record. Now add one assumption: the next model reads that missing record as risk. Then group A gets lower scores, and is denied again. The loop closes.

The assumption is doing the work. Missing evidence is not evidence of risk. The point is that a model's decisions can create the data used to judge it.

Key takeaway: Decisions can create the data that later justifies them.

The figure

Closed clockwise cycle: group A denied more often → no loan, no record (repayment never observed) → missing read as risky (dashed box, labelled as an assumption) → lower scores for A in the next model → back to denial.

Assumptions

Illustrative lending loop; no specific system is claimed. The loop requires the learner or lender to interpret missing evidence unfavorably; without that assumption, missing outcomes alone do not make the loop reinforce.

10. Dropping a Column Can Leave Information

Where bias enters · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈10 min

The obvious fix is to delete the sensitive column. It need not remove disparities. In this illustrative table, the group column is deleted, but the ZIP area column still separates A from B, because here we assume ZIP area correlates with group. In real data, ZIP code, name and school can each be correlated with race, so the remaining features can still carry group information, and a model can use it. Whether a given feature does so has to be checked in the data at hand.

Worse, once the column is gone, you can no longer measure the gap. Fairness needs more than blindness.

Key takeaway: Deleting the attribute need not remove disparities, and it stops you measuring them.

The figure

Illustrative feature table (group, years employed, ZIP area; four applicants). The group column is struck out and marked deleted; the ZIP area column is outlined, with an arrow to "ZIP area still separates A from B" and the stated assumption "ZIP correlates with group".

Going further

Several methods in Section 04 need the group label at training time (reweighing, reductions) or at decision time (group thresholds). Dropping it makes them impossible.

12. One Decision, Two Recorded Outcomes

Measuring fairness · ≈1.25 min · elapsed ≈12 min

Three symbols, one running example. A lender decides who is selected: the loan is approved, $\hat Y = 1$. $A$ is the group, here A or B. $Y$ is the recorded outcome: the person repaid or did not repay.

Read the picture. Filled circles repaid, hollow circles did not. A blue box means selected. Ten people per group. Every fairness criterion in this section compares something about the selected and the repaid across the two groups.

One honest caveat: this population is synthetic and fully labeled. In real lending, a denied applicant never gets a repayment record, so $Y$ is missing exactly where the decision said no.

Key takeaway: $A$ group, $Y$ recorded outcome, $\hat Y$ the decision.

S13a. Selection Counts Everyone

Measuring fairness · ≈0.75 min · elapsed ≈?

Every rate today is a fraction, and the question is always: out of whom? The first is the simplest. The selection rate counts the selected out of everyone in the group. In group A, 4 of 10 are selected.

Key takeaway: Selection rate: the denominator is the whole group.

The figure

Group A of the lending example, the whole group shaded as the denominator; the four boxed people are the numerator. Group B is dimmed.

Formulas

From the per-group confusion matrix (TP, FP, FN, TN): selection rate $= (TP + FP)/n$; true-positive rate $\mathrm{TPR} = TP/(TP + FN)$; false-positive rate $\mathrm{FPR} = FP/(FP + TN)$; positive predictive value $\mathrm{PPV} = TP/(TP + FP)$. Group A: 4/10, 3/6, 1/4, 3/4. Group B: 4/10, 1/2, 3/8, 1/4. Formal versions, with conditioning events, are in lec12tech.html, slide 4.

S13b. True Positives Count Repayers

Measuring fairness · ≈0.75 min · elapsed ≈?

Now shrink the denominator. The true-positive rate, TPR, looks only at people who repaid, and asks how many of them were selected. Group A has six repayers; three are selected, so TPR is 3/6. The non-repayers do not enter this count at all.

Key takeaway: TPR: selected out of those who repaid.

The figure

Group A, repaid row shaded; three of its six people are boxed.

S13c. False Positives Count Non-Repayers

Measuring fairness · ≈0.75 min · elapsed ≈?

The false-positive rate, FPR, uses the other row: of those who did not repay, how many were selected? In group A, 1 of 4.

Be careful with the word "error". Here selection helps the applicant, so a false positive is a loan given to someone who did not repay. In a setting where the positive label harms the person, such as a high-risk flag, the false positive is the harm. What an error costs depends on the application.

Key takeaway: FPR: selected out of those who did not repay.

The figure

Group A, did-not-repay row shaded; one of its four people is boxed.

S13d. Precision Counts the Selected

Measuring fairness · ≈0.75 min · elapsed ≈?

The last rate turns the question around. Positive predictive value, PPV, starts from the selected and asks how many of them repaid. In group A, 3 of the 4 selected repaid.

Same people, four different denominators, four different questions. Keep this in mind: the COMPAS dispute later is exactly a dispute about denominators.

Key takeaway: Same people, different denominator, different question.

The figure

Group A, selected column shaded; three of the four selected are filled (repaid).

14. Equal Selection Can Hide Unequal Outcomes

Measuring fairness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈16.5 min

The first criterion: demographic parity. The rule: the same selection rate in every group. The formula is in the technical supplement.

It looks only at who is selected, never at $Y$. In the picture, both groups select 4 of 10, so parity holds. But in group A, 3 of the 4 selected repaid; in group B, only 1 of 4. Whether that is a feature or a flaw depends on whether you trust $Y$.

If the labels are biased, as in Section 01, ignoring them can be the point. If they are trustworthy, parity can force selecting less-qualified people in one group.

Key takeaway: Parity equalizes selection rates and ignores the outcome.

The figure

Both groups of the lending example, each shaded whole; selection 4/10 in each, selected who repaid 3/4 (A) vs 1/4 (B).

15. Equal Opportunity Leaves One Error Unchecked

Measuring fairness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈18.5 min

Equal opportunity conditions on the recorded outcome, but only on one side. The rule: the same TPR in every group. Qualified people, here those who repaid, have the same chance of being selected wherever they come from.

In our lending groups it holds: half the repayers are selected in A (3 of 6) and in B (1 of 2). But look at the hollow row. Group A selects 1 of 4 non-repayers; group B selects 3 of 8. Equal TPR says nothing about FPR.

It is the criterion to use when what matters is that qualified people in every group get the same chance.

Key takeaway: Equal opportunity = equal TPR; FPR stays free.

The figure

Both groups, repaid row shaded. TPR 3/6 = 1/2 in both; FPR 1/4 (A) vs 3/8 (B).

16. Equalized Odds Checks Both Classes

Measuring fairness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈20.5 min

Equalized odds closes the gap: the same TPR and the same FPR in every group. Given $Y = 1$, equal true-positive rates; given $Y = 0$, equal false-positive rates. Each recorded class bears the same error rate in both groups.

Our lending groups fail it, so this is a different illustrative population: 10 repaid and 10 did not repay per group. Among repayers, 8 of 10 are selected in both groups; among non-repayers, 2 of 10 in both. Each row is its own denominator. The rule matches rates, not raw counts of mistakes.

Key takeaway: Equalized odds = equal TPR and FPR; equal opportunity = equal TPR.

The figure

Four rows of ten (A and B, repaid and did not repay), each shaded as its own denominator: TPR 8/10 and FPR 2/10 in both groups. Equivalently, both groups' confusion-matrix rates are TPR 0.8, FNR 0.2, FPR 0.2, TNR 0.8.

S17a. Equalized Odds Needs a Shared Attainable Point

Measuring fairness · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈?

A picture from Hardt, Price and Srebro. Each axis is one of our rates: across, FPR; up, TPR. Each group has its own ROC curve, the (FPR, TPR) pairs reachable by thresholding its score. With randomization, a group can also reach points between those, which gives each group a shaded attainable region.

Equalized odds needs one point that both groups can reach, so it must lie in the overlap of the two regions. The star marks one such point. Reaching it may require randomized thresholds.

Key takeaway: Equalized odds lives in the overlap of the groups' attainable regions.

18. A Calibrated Score Has a Meaning

Measuring fairness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈25 min

Calibration is about scores, not yes-or-no decisions. The rule: in every group, among everyone given score $s$, a share $s$ have $Y = 1$.

So among those scored 0.7, 7 in 10 repaid, in both groups. The number means the same thing for everyone. Note that agreeing between groups is not enough: 0.7 has to mean 7 in 10. Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan call this condition (A).

Key takeaway: Calibrated: a score means the same thing in every group.

Two groups, each with ten people scored 0.7, seven of whom repaid.

S19b. Calibration Does Not Fix Cutoff Precision

Measuring fairness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈?

Now make a decision: select everyone scored above 0.5. Both groups select 30 of 40. Predictive parity asks for equal PPV, the share of the selected who repaid. In group A, 24 of 30, or 0.8. In group B, 21 of 30, or 0.7.

Why? PPV averages the scores above the cutoff, and A's selected are mostly 0.9s while B's are mostly 0.6s. Two groups can both be calibrated and still differ in PPV. These sound alike and are not: calibration is about every score, predictive parity about one cutoff.

With only two score values, calibration does give predictive parity when the cutoff selects the same top score in both groups and both groups have someone at that score. Three values are enough to break it.

Key takeaway: Calibration is about every score; predictive parity about one cutoff.

The figure

The S19a buckets with a cutoff line at 0.5; the 0.6 and 0.9 buckets are boxed as selected and shaded as the PPV denominator.

S20a. Which Population Does the Rule Compare?

Measuring fairness · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈?

A recap in one picture, using group A of the lending example. Demographic parity compares selection rates among everyone. Equal opportunity compares TPR, among those who repaid. Equalized odds compares TPR and FPR, so it uses two denominators: those who repaid, and separately those who did not.

Key takeaway: Each rule fixes its own denominator.

The figure

Three copies of group A, each shading the denominator of one rule. It replaces the earlier five-row table (criterion, what it compares, among whom): demographic parity, selection rate among everyone; equal opportunity, TPR among $Y = 1$; equalized odds, TPR and FPR; predictive parity, PPV among the selected; calibration, outcomes at each score. The last two are on S20b.

Going further

Two notions not in the picture. Both ask for something a dataset alone does not provide.

  • Individual fairness (Dwork et al. 2012, Def. 2.1): similar individuals should be treated similarly. Given a task-specific metric $d$ on individuals and a distance $D$ between output distributions, a randomized classifier $M$ is $(D, d)$-Lipschitz if $D(Mx, My) \le d(x, y)$ for all $x, y$. The hard part is $d$: who decides which people are similar?
  • Counterfactual fairness (Kusner et al. 2017, Def. 5): in a causal model, the prediction's distribution for a person should not change if their attribute $A$ had been different, holding the person's background variables fixed. It needs a causal model of how $A$ affects the features.

Formal statements: lec12tech.html, slides 6–7.

Primary sources

  • Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold & Zemel, "Fairness Through Awareness", ITCS 2012 — statistical parity; Lipschitz (individual) fairness, Definition 2.1.
  • Hardt, Price & Srebro, "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning", NeurIPS 2016 — equalized odds and equal opportunity (Defs. 2.1–2.2).
  • Kusner, Loftus, Russell & Silva, "Counterfactual Fairness", NIPS 2017 — Definition 5 (counterfactual fairness) in a causal model $(U, V, F)$.

S20b. Score Meaning Is Not Selection Precision

Measuring fairness · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈?

The last two criteria side by side, on group A of the calibrated example. Calibration looks at people with the same score: of the ten scored 0.6, six repaid. Predictive parity looks at the selected, everyone above the cutoff: 24 of 30 repaid. One is a property of the score; the other of a decision made with it.

One caution for COMPAS. Chouldechova's own calibration check, her Definition 1, is weaker: it only asks that groups agree score by score, which suits COMPAS deciles that are ranks, not probabilities. The "PPV averages the scores" argument needs the stronger, probability version used here.

Key takeaway: Calibration: same score. Predictive parity: same decision.

28. Rearrest Is Not Offending

A caveat that sits under every COMPAS number. $Y$ is recorded rearrest, not offending. An offence counts only if it is followed by an arrest; without an arrest it never becomes $Y = 1$.

30. Three Equalities Can Conflict

COMPAS and impossibility · ≈1.25 min · elapsed ≈40 min

Within a group, three numbers fix the false-positive rate: the base rate, the PPV and the FNR, the false-negative rate. It's just bookkeeping on the confusion matrix; the formula is in the technical supplement.

Now fix PPV equal across groups, strictly between 0 and 1, and FNR below 1. If base rates differ, FPR and FNR cannot both be equal. The only escapes are equal base rates or perfect PPV.

Calibration is not a premise of this result; it uses only the binary prediction.

Key takeaway: Equal PPV plus different base rates forces unequal error rates.

The figure

Diagram: base rate, PPV and FNR together determine FPR. The identity itself, $\mathrm{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\mathrm{PPV}}{\mathrm{PPV}}\cdot(1-\mathrm{FNR})$ for $0 \lt p \lt 1$, is in the supplement.

33. The Score Mix Explains This Example

COMPAS and impossibility · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈44 min

Why does it happen here? With scores fixed at 0.2 and 0.8, calibration ties each group's mean score to its base rate: A 0.5, B 0.25. So a lower base rate means fewer high scores: half of A, but only 1/12 of B. A shared cutoff then flags fewer people in B, so B gets a lower FPR and a higher FNR.

That is a property of this two-score construction, not of every calibrated model. Other calibrated scores split the errors differently; the general statement is Chouldechova's identity.

Key takeaway: In this example, calibration ties the score mix to the base rate.

37. Reweighing

Mitigation · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈49 min

Reweighing, from Kamiran and Calders. Give each training example a weight so that group and label look independent. The rule: a (group, label) cell's weight is the share you'd expect if group and label were independent, divided by the share you actually observe.

A cell below its independence-expected share gets a weight above 1; a cell above it gets a weight below 1. No example is deleted and no label is changed. Weighting needs full support: if a (group, label) combination that independence requires has no examples, no weight can create them, and the weighted data stay dependent. Example: expected 30%, observed 20%, weight 1.5. That comes from group s being half the data and 60% of labels positive: $0.5 \times 0.6 = 0.3$ expected, against 0.2 observed.

Key takeaway: Reweighing makes group and label look independent, without deleting anything.

The figure

The verbal rule, weight = expected share ÷ observed share, with a numeric example. The formula $w(a, y) = \Pr[A = a]\Pr[Y = y] / \Pr[A = a, Y = y]$, defined when $\Pr[A = a, Y = y] \gt 0$, is in the supplement.

Assumptions

The group is known at training time. The labels are taken as correct. Every required (group, label) cell has examples.

Going further

40. The Reductions Approach

Mitigation · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈55.5 min

Agarwal and colleagues turn a fairness constraint into a sequence of ordinary training runs. The fairness gap gets a price, like a penalty. Each round reweights the examples by that price and fits a plain cost-weighted classifier. Then it measures the gap, adjusts the price, and repeats.

The output is a random mix of the classifiers fit along the way. That randomization is part of the guarantee, which is worth telling a deployer: two identical people can get different decisions.

Key takeaway: Reductions: fairness constraints via repeated ordinary fits.

All three take $Y$ as given. If the label is biased, as in Section 01, none of them fixes it.

Key takeaway: Every method takes the label as given.

49. An Unanswerable Question Needs “Unknown”

S49b. Added Evidence Changes the Correct Answer

50. A Bias Score Says Which Way Answers Lean

Beyond classifiers · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈74 min

Before reading the results, the scale. Zero means no lean; positive means answers lean toward the stereotype; negative means against it; the ends are ±100.

The two context types are scored differently. In disambiguated contexts the score looks at the answers other than "unknown" and asks what share follow the stereotype. In ambiguous contexts that same lean is multiplied by the error rate, so a model that correctly answers "unknown" every time scores zero, and a model that is usually right gets a small score even if its errors lean. Neither is a percentage of harmful answers, and it is a benchmark score, not a deployment harm rate.

Key takeaway: The sign gives the direction; the two panels are scaled differently.

Setup, model, date

Disambiguated: $s_{\mathrm{DIS}} = 2\,(n_{\text{biased}} / n_{\text{non-unknown}}) - 1$. Ambiguous: $s_{\mathrm{AMB}} = (1 - \text{accuracy})\, s_{\mathrm{DIS}}$. Both reported ×100, range −100 to 100.

52. An Audit Requirement Is Not a Fairness Verdict

Beyond classifiers · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈80 min

One dated example of how law turns fairness into an audit. New York City's rules for automated employment decision tools took effect on July 5, 2023. An employer may not use such a tool if more than a year has passed since its last bias audit by an independent auditor. The audit computes prescribed metrics: the selection rate for each sex and race/ethnicity category, including intersectional categories, and the impact ratio, which is a category's selection rate divided by the rate of the most selected category. The rules' own example: 170 of 390 Black applicants selected (44%) against 412 of 797 White applicants (52%, the most selected group) gives an impact ratio of about 0.84; the ratio uses the unrounded rates, since 44 ÷ 52 alone would round to 0.85. A summary of the results, with the data source and the audit date, must be published.

What the audit does not do is say which ratio, or which fairness goal, is good enough. The rate and the denominator are fixed; the verdict is not.

The rest of this article is the earlier four-jurisdiction comparison, kept as a dated snapshot. It was checked on 10 Oct 2026 and has not been re-verified as current law in this revision. New York City's Local Law 144 requires a bias audit of automated hiring tools and publication of impact ratios. But Local Law 144 itself does not require any specific action based on the results; other anti-discrimination law still applies. On enforcement: the State Comptroller's December 2025 audit found the city had surveyed 32 companies and identified one compliance issue, while the auditors found at least 17 potential instances of non-compliance. That is an observation about enforcement, not a measure of fairness.

The EU AI Act requires high-risk systems to examine and mitigate data bias, in Article 10. An amending regulation, 2026/1744, moves application for Annex III high-risk systems to 2 December 2027; systems under Annex I follow on 2 August 2028.

Colorado's SB26-189 requires notice and review after adverse decisions from 2027, but creates no new private right of action. Korea's AI Basic Act sets risk-management and oversight duties for high-impact AI, in Article 34. Its impact assessment is phrased as an effort, "endeavor", and there is no bias audit. (Historical and unverified: the operative Korean articles were not re-checked in this revision.)

The common thread: prescribed audit metrics do not settle every fairness question. They fix what to report, not which criterion matters for your decision.

Key takeaway: An audit requirement fixes what to measure and publish, not which fairness goal is enough.

54. Ask What the Label Misses

Synthesis · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈84 min

Back to the care-allocation case we opened with, and walk the questions through it. Which decision? Patients whose risk score is at or above the 97th percentile are auto-enrolled in an extra-care program. What does the label record? Future health cost; the score predicts cost.

What need is missed? The actual need is illness, and cost is not illness: less was spent on Black patients' care, so at the same score Black patients were sicker. Who bears the error? Sicker Black patients: at the same score, they needed more care than the score shows.

The point: a score can predict its recorded label well and still miss the actual need.

Key takeaway: Ask what the recorded label leaves out of the actual need.

S54b. Ask What the Fix Can Change

Synthesis · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈?

Which stage can you change? Start with the target. If the label is cost, then any rate you compute on it, error rates or selection rates, inherits the gap between cost and need. Equalizing a rate on the wrong label does not fix that.

Then the remaining choices are decisions: which criterion, evaluated on which population, against what utility. None of them is settled by the math, and the people affected should have a say. In the study's simulation, correcting the disparity would raise the Black share auto-identified for extra care from 17.7% to 46.5%. Those are shares of selected patients, observed versus simulated, not a deployed intervention and not a measure of treatment effect.

Key takeaway: Fix the target first; then choose the criterion, population and utility openly.

The full checklist (from the earlier version of this slide)

Six questions. One: which decision, and which error hurts most? Two: what does $Y$ actually record? Three: which rate, out of whom? Four: do base rates differ, and if so, which criteria conflict? Five: which stage can you change? Six: evaluated on what data, against what utility?

Run COMPAS through it. The decision is pretrial detention, and a false positive jails someone. $Y$ records rearrest, not offending. ProPublica and Northpointe used different denominators. 55. Name the Decision, Denominator, and Tradeoff

Synthesis · ≈3.25 min · elapsed ≈87 min

Three choices sit under every fairness claim. The decision: which error hurts, whom, and what the label records. The denominator: which rate, out of whom. The tradeoff: with unequal base rates, some criteria cannot all hold, so you choose which to keep.

The five takeaways behind that map: bias enters through data, labels and feedback loops. Each criterion compares a rate with its own denominator. In COMPAS the analyses evaluated different criteria. With unequal base rates, some criteria cannot all hold. Each mitigation targets one criterion, under assumptions.

And choosing the criterion is a value judgment. The math tells you what conflicts; it does not tell you what to choose.

Key takeaway: Choosing a fairness criterion is a value judgment.
