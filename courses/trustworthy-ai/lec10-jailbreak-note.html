5. Why Getting Around Refusal Matters

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈3.5 min

Message: Getting around refusal matters for three reasons: some answers give real uplift toward serious harm, one shared trick works for every user of the model for months, and models that run code or send messages can turn a bypass into a real action.

Reading the slide. The three boxes are read left to right: Uplift, Scale, Agency, each with a one-line reason. The bold line below combines them: a bypass can become real-world help or a real action. The last line states today's two questions, robustness and measurement.

Script

Why should we care whether refusal can be bypassed? Three reasons.

Uplift: some answers give real help toward serious harm, such as weapons, malware or fraud at scale. Scale: a trick shared online works for every user of the model, and can keep working for months. Agency: models now act for the user — they run code, browse and send messages — so a bypass can become an action, not only a text.

So today's question: how robust is refusal, and how do we measure that honestly?

The figure

Three cards drawn for this lecture.

Assumptions

Uplift assumes the jailbroken answer adds something the attacker could not easily find elsewhere; slide S63 shows a jailbroken reply with no useful content, so uplift has to be measured, not assumed.

Going further

Agency connects to prompt injection, where the hijacking text comes from a document or web page the agent reads rather than from the user.

6. Jailbreak Prompts Are Shared and Last for Months

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈4.5 min

Message: In one study of jailbreak prompts collected online from December 2022 to December 2023, prompts were shared in 131 communities and refined over months; the top five reached a 0.95 success rate on GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, on the authors' own question set.

Reading the slide. The four large numbers are read as a row: 1,405 prompts found, 131 communities, 0.95 success for the top five, and 240+ days online for the oldest of those five. The 0.95 is measured on Shen et al.'s forbidden-question set, so it is a rate for that set and those 2023 models, not a general success rate. The bottom lines give the consequence: one posted discovery works for anyone who copies it, and some accounts kept refining prompts for 100+ days.

Script

Is this only a lab curiosity? Shen and colleagues collected prompts from Reddit, Discord, prompt-sharing websites and open datasets, December 2022 to December 2023.

Out of 15,140 prompts, 1,405 were jailbreak prompts, shared across 131 communities. The five most effective reached a 0.95 attack success rate on GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, and the oldest of those five had stayed online for more than 240 days.

These are communities, not lone attackers: 28 accounts kept refining their prompts for over 100 days. Once posted, one discovery works for anyone who copies it.

8. A Jailbreak Bypasses Safeguards Through Inputs

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈7 min

Message: A jailbreak is an input that gets a safety-trained model to produce what its safeguards are meant to block. Today covers inputs from the user; prompt injection and data poisoning are not covered, and safety removal by fine-tuning appears only as a contrast.

Reading the slide. The definition sits at the top. The table then lists three neighbouring threats, who controls what in each (third-party text the app reads, training data, the weights) and how today treats them. Read the last column as the scope of this lecture: not covered, not covered, contrast only.

Script

Our working definition: a jailbreak is an input that gets a safety-trained model to produce what its safeguards are meant to block.

Two words do the work. "Input": the attacker acts through what the model reads, not through its weights. "Safeguards": we are attacking a behavior someone deliberately trained or attached.

The table separates neighbours. Prompt injection, where text from a third party the app reads hijacks the model, is a different threat and is covered in the prompt-injection lecture (Lecture 13). Data poisoning, which corrupts training data, was Lecture 9. Removing safety by fine-tuning the weights appears today only as a labelled contrast, because it is not a prompt attack.

Assumptions

The deployer, not the user, decides what is blocked. The definition says nothing about whether the blocked content is actually dangerous; that judgment is part of evaluation (Section 05).

Going further

Usage varies in the literature: some papers call any successful harmful-output attack a "jailbreak", including fine-tuning. We keep the narrower, input-only meaning so that results compare within one threat model.

9. Safety Training Teaches the Model When to Refuse

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈8 min

Message: Safety training is the part of alignment that teaches a pretrained model to decline harmful requests while staying helpful, and refusal is its visible behavior. Because refusal is learned from examples, nothing guarantees it generalizes to inputs unlike those examples.

Reading the slide. Read the diagram left to right: a pretrained model that only predicts text goes through alignment, which aims at two goals, helpful and harmless. The harmless half is what this lecture calls safety training. The two bold lines below say that refusal is its visible behavior and that generalization beyond the training examples is not guaranteed.

Script

We have talked about alignment; now name the part this lecture is about. A pretrained model only predicts text. Alignment trains it toward what people want; the usual goals are helpful, honest and harmless, and the slide shows the two that matter today.

Helpful: do what the user asks. Harmless: decline requests that would cause harm. Training for the harmless part is what we call safety training, and refusal is its visible behavior. When a jailbreak works, this is the behavior it gets around.

How is it taught? In RLHF, human raters compare answers; in Constitutional AI, an AI model judges answers against a written list of principles. Either way the model learns from examples, and nothing guarantees it generalizes beyond them. The next slide (S10) shows one concrete recipe.

The figure

InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., OpenAI, 2022), which adopts the helpful, honest and harmless goals of Askell et al., and Constitutional AI (Bai et al., Anthropic, December 2022).

10. Example: How Llama 2 Chat Was Taught to Refuse

What safety training adds · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈9.5 min

Message: Llama 2 Chat, as described by Meta in 2023, learned to refuse in three steps, safety fine-tuning, safety RLHF and context distillation, each from examples covering risk categories such as crime, hate and self-harm. Inputs unlike those examples are where jailbreaks look.

Reading the slide. The three boxes are the three training steps, read left to right: imitate written safe answers, optimize against a safety reward model, then distil a safety preprompt into the model. The line below lists the risk categories the prompts covered. The bold last line draws the conclusion for attackers: all three steps learn from examples.

Script

One concrete recipe, from Meta's Llama 2 Chat paper. Safety was added in three steps, after the model had learned to follow instructions.

First, safety fine-tuning: annotators wrote adversarial prompts with safe answers, and the model imitated them. Second, safety RLHF: a separate safety reward model scored the model's replies to adversarial prompts, and training pushed toward higher scores. Third, context distillation: they put a safety preprompt such as "You are a safe and responsible assistant" in front of the prompt, then fine-tuned the model on the safer answers without it.

The prompts covered risk categories such as illicit and criminal activities, hateful and harmful activities including self-harm, and unqualified advice. All three steps learn from examples. Inputs unlike those examples are where jailbreaks look.

The figure

Three steps drawn for this lecture from Section 4.2 of Touvron et al.

Setup, model, date

Touvron et al. (Meta), arXiv July 2023, Section 4.2: safety supervised fine-tuning, safety RLHF, and safety context distillation (Section 4.2.4).

What this does not establish

Other vendors' pipelines differ and are mostly unpublished. The recipe tells what the model saw in training, not how far refusal generalizes beyond it.

Primary sources

  • Touvron et al., "Llama 2: Open Foundation and Fine-Tuned Chat Models" (arXiv 2023) — safety fine-tuning, safety RLHF and safety context distillation (§4.2).

11. Two Ways to Fail: Refusing Too Little or Too Much

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈10.5 min

Message: Safety can fail in two directions: under-refusal, answering a harmful request, which is what a jailbreak targets, and over-refusal, declining a benign one, which is the cost of caution. A defense should report both, because shrinking one error can grow the other.

Reading the slide. Read the 2×2 grid by rows and columns: rows are benign or harmful requests, columns are whether the model answers or refuses. The two correct cells are helpful answering and safe refusal. The red cell, harmful request answered, is under-refusal; the grey cell, benign request refused, is over-refusal. The line below warns that a defense that only shrinks the red cell may grow the grey one.

Script

A two-by-two. Rows: is the request benign or harmful? Columns: does the model answer or refuse? Answering benign requests is helpful. Refusing harmful ones is a safe refusal.

The two off-diagonal cells are both failures. Answering a harmful request is under-refusal: that is the jailbreak's target. Refusing a benign request is over-refusal: the cost of caution.

Keep both cells in view all lecture. A defense that only shrinks the red cell may quietly grow the grey one.

The figure

A confusion-matrix sketch drawn for this lecture. Red marks under-refusal (jailbreak success); grey marks over-refusal.

Assumptions

That "benign" and "harmful" can be labelled. In practice the boundary is contested and policy-dependent; XSTest (slide S65) builds prompts near that boundary on purpose.

12. What the Attacker Can Touch Sets the Threat

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈11.5 min

Message: What an attacker can touch sets the threat model: chat-only prompts, control of the reply or gradients of an open model, or the weights themselves. Higher rungs need more access, so attack results should be compared only within the same rung.

Reading the slide. Read the ladder from the bottom up. Chat only: type prompts and read replies, used by hand-written attacks, PAIR, many-shot and Best-of-N. Response control or gradients: prefill the reply or search an open model, as in prefilling and GCG. Weights: fine-tune or edit the model, which is not a prompt attack and appears today only as a contrast.

Script

Attacks differ in access, and access changes what is possible. The lowest rung is chat only: type prompts, read replies. Hand-written jailbreaks, PAIR, many-shot and Best-of-N all live here.

One rung up, the attacker controls part of the response or has gradients from an open model: prefilling the start of the reply, or GCG suffix search.

The top rung is weights: fine-tuning or editing the model. That is not a prompt attack, and we will label it as a contrast when it appears.

The rule: compare attacks on the same rung. An attack with gradients beating an attack without them tells you little.

The figure

An access ladder drawn for this lecture, with today's attacks placed on their rungs.

Going further

Prefilling sits on the middle rung because some commercial APIs let the user write the start of the assistant turn; with open weights it is always possible.

13. Every Attack Success Rate Needs Four Labels

What safety training adds · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈13 min

Message: An attack success rate (ASR), the share of requests counted as successful, needs four labels to mean anything: the denominator of requests, the budget of tries per request, the exact model version and the judge. Two ASRs that differ on any label are not directly comparable.

Reading the slide. The four boxes are the four labels, each with the question it answers: what requests and how many, one try or best of N, which dated model snapshot, and who calls a reply harmful with what error rates. The line below defines ASR. The last line is the rule the rest of the lecture applies to every number.

Script

ASR, attack success rate, is the share of requests counted as successful. Every ASR needs four labels before it means anything.

The denominator: which requests, and how many? The budget: one try per request, or the best of N tries? The model: name and dated version. The judge: who decided the reply was harmful, and how often is that judge wrong?

Two ASRs that differ on any of these labels are not directly comparable. We will check all four on every number today.

Going further

The budget label is the one most often omitted. "At least one success in N attempts" grows quickly with N even when the per-attempt rate is tiny; slide S46 makes this concrete, and lec10tech gives the formula.

15. Two Hypotheses for Why Safety Training Fails

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈14.5 min

Message: Wei et al. proposed two hypotheses for why safety training fails: competing objectives, where the prompt makes refusing conflict with other training goals, and mismatched generalization, where capabilities reach inputs safety training never covered. Both come from 2023 tests on GPT-4 and Claude v1.3: explanations, not proofs.

Reading the slide. The two boxes are the two hypotheses, each with a one-line mechanism: the prompt forces a choice, or the prompt leaves safe ground. The next line says how they were used: to design new jailbreaks and test them. The last line gives the qualifier: proposed from tests on two 2023 models, so explanations rather than proofs.

Script

Wei and colleagues proposed two failure modes. Competing objectives: safety competes with other training goals, and the prompt forces a choice between them. Mismatched generalization: the model's capabilities reach inputs that safety training never saw, such as Base64, so the prompt leaves safe ground.

Then they used both hypotheses as design tools: each suggested new jailbreaks, which they built and tested.

Note the status of these ideas. They were proposed from tests on GPT-4 and Claude v1.3 in 2023. They organise the evidence well, but they are explanations, not proofs.

Setup, model, date

Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, NeurIPS 2023, Section 3.

They do not show a mechanism inside the model; they are behavioral hypotheses that predict which attacks should work. Slides S20–S27 look for mechanisms in open models.

16. Competing Objectives: Make Refusing Costly

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈15.5 min

Message: Competing-objective attacks make refusing costly: the prompt is built so that declining would break the model's text-prediction and instruction-following goals, and on the tested 2023 models safety sometimes gave way. A demanded opening line is the standard example.

Reading the slide. Read the three training goals left to right: predict text from pretraining, follow instructions from instruction tuning, stay safe from safety training. The attack arrow targets the first two, so that refusing would break them; the result box says safety gives way. The bold line below restates the forced choice.

Script

Wei et al. describe three training goals. Pretraining: continue text the likely way. Instruction tuning: do what the user asks. Safety training: decline harmful requests. Usually they agree.

A competing-objectives attack builds a prompt where refusing would break the first two. For example, demand a set opening line. Once that line is written, the likely continuation is the answer, and refusing would also disobey the instruction. Safety gives way.

The next slide (S17) tests this explanation: change the wording that makes refusing costly, and see whether the attack still works.

The figure

A three-goal schematic drawn for this lecture: predict text (pretraining), follow instructions (instruction tuning), stay safe (safety training), with the attack setting the first two against the third.

Setup, model, date

Wei et al., Section 3.1: state-of-the-art LLMs are "trained for language modeling […], instruction following […], and safety […]. This training can be exploited by crafting prompts that force a choice between either a restricted behavior or a response that is heavily penalized by the pretraining and instruction following objectives."

17. Change the Key Wording and the Attack Stops Working

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈16.5 min

Message: Wei et al.'s ablations on GPT-4, 32 curated prompts, show the conflict is the active ingredient: a forced eager opening line gave 0.22 harmful against 0.06 when replaced with "Hello!", and no-refusal rules gave 0.25 against 0.00 when inverted; a plain request gave 0.03.

Reading the slide. Each row compares one attack as written with an ablated version. Prefix injection: 0.22 as written, 0.06 when the demanded line becomes a neutral "Hello!". Refusal suppression: 0.25 as written, 0.00 when the rules are inverted. The numbers are shares of GPT-4 replies labelled harmful on 32 curated prompts, against 0.03 for the plain request. The families are described; working prompts are not shown.

Script

Is the competing-objectives explanation right? Wei et al. tested it with ablations on GPT-4: keep the attack, but change the part that is supposed to make refusing costly.

Prefix injection demands that the reply open with a carefree, eager line. As written, 22% of replies were harmful. Replace the demanded line with a plain "Hello!" and it fell to 6%. Refusal suppression gives rules that forbid apologies and words like "cannot": 25%. Invert those rules and it fell to 0%. A plain request: 3%.

So the attack works through the part that makes refusing costly. Shared role-play prompts like "DAN" use the same idea, pairing a persona with a fixed opening. We name the families; working prompts are not shown, here or anywhere in this course.

19. Combined Attacks Beat Single Ones

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈18.5 min

Message: In Wei et al.'s 2023 tests, combining both failure modes was far stronger than either alone: on 32 curated prompts, a combined attack reached 0.94 on GPT-4 and 0.81 on Claude v1.3, and an adaptive attacker trying any of 28 attacks reached 1.00 on both.

Reading the slide. Read the table top to bottom: plain request, one competing-objectives attack, one mismatch attack, a combination, and an adaptive any-of-28 row. Columns give the hand-labelled harmful share on GPT-4 and Claude v1.3, 2023 versions. Note the last row's budget: any of 28 attacks counts a success if one works, a much bigger budget than the single-attack rows.

Script

Now the numbers. Each cell is the share of outputs hand-labelled as harmful compliance (the paper's BAD BOT rate) on 32 curated prompts.

A plain request almost never works: 3% on GPT-4, 0% on Claude v1.3. Refusal suppression alone: 25% and 16%. Base64 alone: 34% and 38%. Combining several tricks, exploiting both failure modes: 94% and 81%.

The last row is "adaptive": count success if any of 28 attacks works. That reaches 100% on both. Notice the budget label changed: that row is any of 28, not one try.

26. One Direction in Activations Switches Refusal

Message: Arditi et al. found, in open chat models with white-box access, a single activation direction r, the mean harmful minus mean harmless activation: removing it stopped refusals of harmful requests, and adding it made the model refuse harmless ones. Reading the slide. The strip gives the access: the researcher edits activations, white-box. Script

A second inside look, by Arditi and colleagues. Read the access strip first: the researcher edits activations inside the model. That is white-box, and it tests an explanation; it is not an attack on a chat app.

43. Test-Time Scaling: Spend More Compute per Question

Message: Test-time scaling buys accuracy with more compute per question, not bigger weights: sample many answers and keep the best one by a verifier such as unit tests, or revise one answer step by step. The weights stay the same.

Script

46. Many Tries Turn a Small Rate Into a Large One

Message: If each try independently succeeds with probability $p$, the chance of at least one success in $N$ tries is $1-(1-p)^N$. With $p = 1\%$ that is 9.6% at 10 tries, 63% at 100 and nearly 100% at 1,000: illustrative arithmetic, not a study.

Reading the slide. Read the bars left to right, N = 1, 10, 100 and 1,000 tries, with heights 1%, 9.6%, 63% and about 100%. The formula below the chart produces them from $p = 1\%$. The last line gives the assumption: independent tries with the same $p$, which real attacks need not satisfy.

Script

An illustration, not from a study. Suppose each try succeeds with probability 1%, independently. The chance of at least one success in N tries is one minus 0.99 to the N.

N = 1: 1%. N = 10: 9.6%. N = 100: 63%. N = 1,000: essentially 100%.

This is why "any of N" and "per try" must never be mixed. Real tries are not independent and do not share one p, so treat this as intuition, not a model.

Script

80. Self-Assessment: One Extra Question per Reply

Script

Here is the mechanism. After Claude replies, the system adds one more user turn with an instruction, for example "Please reflect on the safety of this conversation". Claude answers with a short JSON assessment: flagged or not, a category, whether it refused, and a priority from 1 to 10.

It is cheap because the conversation is already processed, in the KV cache: only about 10 extra output tokens, under 2% extra compute. The two added turns are hidden from the user and then dropped.

81. Training Copies a Costly Teacher's Verdicts

Monitoring with self-assessment · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈82.5 min

Message: Self-assessment is trained to copy a costly teacher, a prompted classifier with every policy document that reasons before answering, by SFT and then RL with six checkable rewards, on synthetic harmful and hard-benign chats. The student matches it without policy text or reasoning.

Script

How does Claude learn to assess? It copies a teacher: a prompted classifier that reads every policy document and reasons before it answers. Accurate, but expensive.

Message: A self-monitor has limits: the teacher labels both training and test data, so they share blind spots; held-out tests covered only two already-known harm types; and a model judging itself may go easy on itself. Monitoring is one layer among several, finding what blocking missed.

Reading the slide. Read the three boxes as three questions with their answers: who judges, what about unknown harms, and judging itself. The bold line below places monitoring among the other defense layers.

Script

Three limits. Who judges? The teacher labels both the training data and the tests, so the student inherits the teacher's blind spots, and the tests cannot reveal them.
