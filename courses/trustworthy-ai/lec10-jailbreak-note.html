Jailbreaks & LLM Safety

Welcome to Lecture 10 of Trustworthy AI. Today we ask why a model that has been carefully trained to refuse harmful requests can still be talked into complying, and what we can do about it. This is a defensive lecture: we study attack categories so we can build better defenses.

Key takeaway: Aligned does not mean unbreakable.

Contents

Seven steps: what safety training tries to enforce, why it is fragile, manual jailbreaks, automated ones, attacks that scale, a unifying view, and finally defenses and the frontier.

Key takeaway: From how refusal is built, to how it breaks, to how we defend it.

01 — Safety Training

First section. How a raw next-token predictor is turned into something that declines harmful requests.

Key takeaway: Refusal is something we train into a model, not something it has by default.

A Raw Model Has No Manners

A base model is only trained to continue text. It has no concept of helpful or harmful, and will happily complete a dangerous prompt because that is the most likely continuation.

Key takeaway: Safety is not in the data; it has to be added on top.

Slide figure: Inline sketch: prompt tokens enter a base LM that branches with equal likelihood to helpful text and harmful text.

Two Goals at Once

Safety training asks the model to be both helpful, answering the user's real question, and harmless, refusing serious harm. These two goals usually agree, but later we will see attackers pull them apart.

Key takeaway: Helpful and harmless are two trained urges that can collide.

Slide figure: Inline tug strip: the model sits between Helpful (answer everything) and Harmless (refuse harm).

Instruction Tuning First

The first step is instruction tuning: fine-tune on many prompt-and-answer demonstrations so the model learns to answer questions rather than merely continue text.

Key takeaway: Instruction tuning turns a text continuer into an assistant.

Slide figure: Ouyang et al. (InstructGPT) Fig. 2: the three-step pipeline, supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, reward model on comparisons, PPO against the reward.

RLHF: Learn From Preferences

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback shows the model two replies, has a human pick the better one, trains a reward model on those picks, and then tunes the model to score well. This is the InstructGPT recipe.

Key takeaway: Human preferences become a signal the model is optimized against.

Definitions — the reward model via Bradley–Terry

Collect comparison data: for a prompt $x$ sample two responses and let a human label which is preferred, giving triples $(x, y_w, y_l)$ where $y_w$ ("winner") is preferred to $y_l$ ("loser"). Fit a scalar reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ under the Bradley–Terry model of pairwise comparison, which posits that the probability one item beats another is a logistic function of the reward gap: $$\Pr[\,y_w \succ y_l \mid x\,]=\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big),\qquad \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.$$ Maximum-likelihood over the comparison dataset $\mathcal{D}$ gives the reward-model loss $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi)=-\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big].$$ The reward is identifiable only up to an additive function of $x$ (a shift by any $c(x)$ leaves every difference, hence the likelihood, unchanged). (BT model: Bradley & Terry 1952; loss form per Ouyang et al. 2022, Stiennon et al. 2020. Derivation of the shift-invariance: course notes — the objective depends only on $r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)$.)

Policy optimization (stage 3). The instruction-tuned model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is then fine-tuned by RL (PPO) to maximize expected reward while a KL term leashes it to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ — stated in full on the next slide.

Background & further reading

  • Ouyang et al. 2022 (InstructGPT) — full three-stage RLHF pipeline.
  • Rafailov et al., "Direct Preference Optimization" (DPO), NeurIPS 2023 — reparameterizes the same BT+KL objective so the policy is trained directly on preferences without a separate reward model or RL loop.

InstructGPT, Measured

Ouyang et al. measured what the tuning buys. Human labelers preferred InstructGPT outputs to GPT-3 at every model size, and the 1.3B tuned model beat the 175B untuned one. The preference gap grows with size, and the tuning costs a small fraction of pretraining. Helpfulness is cheap to learn; keep asking whether safety is too.

Key takeaway: A small amount of tuning changes preferred behavior more than a 100x increase in size.

Slide figure: Ouyang et al. Fig. 1: win rate against the 175B SFT model across 1.3B, 6B and 175B for PPO-ptx, PPO, SFT, prompted GPT and GPT.

Optimize Against the Reward

Concretely, the model is tuned to maximize the reward score on its replies, while a penalty keeps it from drifting too far from the original model. The reward pulls toward good behavior; the penalty keeps it sane.

Key takeaway: Behavior is shaped by maximizing a learned score under a drift penalty.

Slide figure: Inline sketch: reward rises over training as the tuned policy climbs; a dashed KL leash pulls it back toward the base model.

The RLHF objective

With frozen reward $r_\phi$ and reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (the SFT model), RLHF trains the policy $\pi_\theta$ to $$\max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\,r_\phi(x,y)\,\big]\;-\;\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\Big].$$ The first term rewards replies humans rated highly; the KL "leash" with strength $\beta>0$ penalizes drifting from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (small $\beta$ chases the score and risks reward hacking; large $\beta$ stays safe but learns less). InstructGPT optimizes this with PPO. (Objective per Ouyang et al. 2022, Eq. 2; also lec10tech.html slide "The RLHF Objective".)

Theorem & proof — the KL-regularized optimum

Statement. For each fixed prompt $x$, the objective above is maximized by the Gibbs (exponential-tilt) policy $$\pi^\star(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r_\phi(x,y)\Big),\qquad Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,e^{r_\phi(x,y)/\beta}.$$ Proof. Fix $x$ and write $p(y)=\pi(y\mid x)$, $q(y)=\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$. The per-prompt objective is $J(p)=\sum_y p(y)r(y)-\beta\sum_y p(y)\log\frac{p(y)}{q(y)}$ subject to $\sum_y p(y)=1$, $p\ge 0$. Define $p^\star(y)=q(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}/Z$ with $Z=\sum_y q(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}$. Then for any feasible $p$, $$J(p^\star)-J(p)=\beta\sum_y p(y)\log\frac{p(y)}{p^\star(y)}=\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,p^\star)\ge 0,$$ with equality iff $p=p^\star$. (Substitute $r(y)=\beta\log\frac{p^\star(y)}{q(y)}+\beta\log Z$ into $J$ and simplify; the $\beta\log Z$ term is constant in $p$.) By Gibbs' inequality the KL is nonnegative and zero only at $p=p^\star$, so $p^\star$ is the unique maximizer. $\qquad\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes; the same closed form underlies DPO, Rafailov et al. 2023, Eq. 4.)

DPO exploits exactly this: invert the optimum to $r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^\star(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$, plug into the Bradley–Terry loss so $\log Z(x)$ cancels in the difference, and train the policy directly on preferences.

Background & further reading

  • Ouyang et al. 2022 — PPO with the KL penalty.
  • Rafailov et al. 2023 (DPO) — closed-form optimum used to remove the RL loop.

Constitutional AI

Constitutional AI replaces much of the human labor with AI feedback. You write a short list of principles, the model critiques and revises its own answers against those principles, and you train on the revisions.

Key takeaway: A model can supervise its own harmlessness from a written set of rules.

Slide figure: Bai et al. Fig. 1: the Constitutional AI pipeline from red-team responses through critique and revision to the SL-CAI and RL-CAI models.

Definitions — the two-stage recipe (Bai et al. 2022)

The only human oversight is a written list of principles (the "constitution"); no human labels of harmful outputs are used. The method has two stages:

  • Supervised stage (critique → revision → SFT). Sample responses from an initial (helpful-only) model on red-team prompts; ask the model to critique its own response against a randomly drawn constitutional principle, then revise it; fine-tune the original model on the revised responses. This produces the "SL-CAI" model.
  • RL stage (RLAIF). Sample response pairs from the SL-CAI model; have a model (not a human) judge which response better satisfies the constitution, yielding a dataset of AI preferences; train a preference/reward model on these, then optimize the policy with RL against it — RL from AI Feedback. Chain-of-thought reasoning is used in the judgments to improve quality and transparency.

Do not confuse Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022, a training method) with Constitutional Classifiers (Sharma et al. 2025, a deployment-time guard, covered later in this lecture). Different papers, different mechanisms. (Two-stage recipe verified against the Bai et al. 2022 abstract and §2–3.)

Background & further reading

  • Bai et al., "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback", Anthropic 2022 — SL (critique–revision) + RLAIF.

The Refusal Behavior

The goal of all this training is a reliable refusal: on a harmful request, the model declines and explains why. Note this is a learned reflex, not a hard-coded rule.

Key takeaway: Alignment here means a trained tendency to refuse, not a guarantee.

Slide figure: Inline chat sketch: a harmful request bubble, then a model bubble that declines and offers a safe alternative.

It Mostly Works

On plain harmful prompts, aligned models refuse well, with a large drop in unsafe outputs, good enough to ship to millions. The real question for today is how robust that refusal is under pressure.

Key takeaway: Refusal works on easy cases; robustness is the open question.

Slide figure: Inline bar chart: unsafe outputs high for the base model, low after alignment, and a dashed question mark for aligned plus attack.

02 — Why It Is Fragile

Second section. The structural reasons safety training is shallow and easy to slip past.

Key takeaway: Alignment sits on top of full capability, not in place of it.

A Thin Layer of Polish

Safety tuning touches only a small part of the model. The vast base knowledge stays intact; tuning learns to steer behavior, not to forget the harmful capability, which is still in there.

Key takeaway: Refusal is a layer on top, not a deletion of dangerous knowledge.

Slide figure: Inline layer cake: a thin green safety-tuning layer on top of the large base-knowledge block, with the harm still inside.

Shallow Alignment

The refusal often hinges on just the first few tokens of the reply. If you can force a compliant opening like "Sure, here is how", the rest of the answer tends to follow along.

Key takeaway: Control the first tokens and you often control the whole reply.

Definitions

Qi et al. formalize "shallow safety alignment" as safety behavior concentrated in the distribution over the first few generated tokens. Measuring the per-token KL between the aligned and base models, $\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\text{aligned}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{(Qi et al., ICLR 2025, §2–3.)

Background & further reading

  • Qi et al., "Safety Alignment Should Be Made More Than Just a Few Tokens Deep", ICLR 2025 — the per-token KL analysis and a deeper-alignment fix.

Shallow, Measured

Here is the measurement behind the claim. Qi et al. compute the per-token KL divergence between the aligned chat model and its base model along a response. It is large for the first few token positions and near zero after about token five. The refusal is written into the opening tokens, and past them the aligned model is essentially the base model again.

Key takeaway: Alignment rewrites the first tokens and little else, which is why a compliant prefix breaks it.

Slide figure: Qi et al. (ICLR 2025) Fig. 1: per-token KL between aligned and base models for Llama-2-7B and Gemma-7B, spiking at the first tokens and flat afterward.

Two Failure Modes

The "Jailbroken" paper names two core reasons safety fails: competing objectives, where helpfulness fights harmlessness, and mismatched generalization, where safety covers less ground than capability.

Key takeaway: Two named failure modes explain most jailbreaks.

Definitions — the two hypotheses (Wei, Haghtalab, Steinhardt 2023)

  • Competing objectives. Safety training instills several objectives that can conflict — e.g. a pretraining/instruction-following drive to be helpful and obedient versus a harmlessness drive to refuse. An attacker constructs a prompt where satisfying safety requires disobeying an instruction, so the model's capabilities and safety goals are placed in direct conflict.
  • Mismatched generalization. Pretraining gives capabilities on a far broader input distribution than the (much smaller) safety-training distribution. A jailbreak lives in a region where capability generalizes but safety does not — the model still understands and can perform the task there, but no refusal was ever trained for that region (e.g. Base64, rare languages, ciphers).

The paper's thesis is the need for safety–capability parity: safety mechanisms should be as sophisticated as the underlying model, and scaling alone will not resolve either failure mode. (Verified against the Wei et al. 2023 abstract and §2.)

Background & further reading

  • Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, "Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?", NeurIPS 2023 — the two failure modes and attacks derived from them.

Competing Objectives

The model wants to be helpful and obedient. An attacker reframes harm as a helpful task, so following instructions now pulls toward harm, and the two trained urges collide.

Key takeaway: Reframing harm as helpfulness weaponizes the helpfulness training.

Slide figure: Inline tilted scale: the attacker adds a reframed weight to the helpful pan, which wins the tilt over harmless.

Mismatched Generalization

Capability generalizes further than safety. Push the prompt into a region the safety data never covered, and the model can still do the task there, but no refusal was ever learned for it.

Key takeaway: Jailbreaks live in the gap where capability reaches but safety does not.

Refusal Is a Direction

Inside the model, refusal often looks like a single internal direction or feature. Suppress that feature and refusals vanish while capability stays intact, which makes "thin layer" literal in a geometric sense.

Key takeaway: The guard can be a single direction, and single things are easy to remove.

Slide figure: Arditi et al. Fig. 1: refusal and safety scores across 13 chat models with and without directional ablation of the refusal direction.

Definitions

Arditi et al. find a single "refusal direction" $\hat r\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ in the residual-stream activation space. They estimate it as a difference-in-means: with $h_\ell(x)$ the layer-$\ell$ residual activation for prompt $x$, $$r_\ell=\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{H}|}\!\sum_{x\in\mathcal{H}} h_\ell(x)}_{\text{mean over harmful prompts}}-\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{H}^c|}\!\sum_{x\in\mathcal{H}^c} h_\ell(x)}_{\text{mean over harmless prompts}},\qquad \hat r=r_\ell/\lVert r_\ell\rVert.$$ Ablating this direction — projecting every activation onto its orthogonal complement, $h\mapsto h-(\hat r^\top h)\,\hat r$ — suppresses refusals across harmful prompts while leaving general capability intact. Conversely, adding $\hat r$ induces refusals even on harmless prompts. This gives a white-box jailbreak (and a weight-edit that permanently removes the guard) and is direct evidence that the refusal behavior is low-dimensional. (Arditi et al., NeurIPS 2024, §3–4.)

Background & further reading

  • Arditi et al., "Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction", NeurIPS 2024 — difference-in-means direction, ablation, and weight edit.

03 — Manual Jailbreaks

Third section. Hand-crafted prompts, described as categories rather than recipes.

Key takeaway: Manual jailbreaks come in a few recognizable shapes.

What a Jailbreak Is

A jailbreak is a prompt crafted to make an aligned model produce output its safety training was meant to block. We study the categories to defend against them, not to hand out attack recipes.

Key takeaway: Understanding attack shapes is a prerequisite for defending against them.

Slide figure: Inline two-row sketch: harmful request into the aligned LM gives refusal; the same intent wrapped as a jailbreak prompt gets the blocked output through.

Definitions — measuring attacks

Let $J(\cdot)$ be a transformation of a harmful request $x$ into an attack prompt $J(x)$ (a suffix, a persona wrapper, a translation, …), $\mathcal{X}_{\text{harm}}$ a benchmark set of harmful requests, and $\mathrm{harmful}(y)\in\{0,1\}$ a judge that flags whether output $y$ actually complies with the harmful intent (a classifier or human).

  • Attack success rate (ASR): $\displaystyle \mathrm{ASR}(J)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}_{\text{harm}}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{harm}}}\mathbf{1}\big[\mathrm{harmful}\big(\text{model}(J(x))\big)=1\big]$ — fraction of harmful requests the attack gets complied with. Reported ASR numbers depend heavily on the judge and benchmark, so compare only like-for-like.
  • Refusal rate: the fraction of requests (harmful or benign) the model declines; on benign requests this is the over-refusal / false-refusal rate a defense should keep low.

Category: Persona Play

One category asks the model to act as a character who would comply, or to write a fictional story that needs the harmful detail. The model treats it as creative writing, exploiting its helpfulness.

Key takeaway: Role-play reframes harm so the helpfulness training takes over.

Slide figure: Inline sketch: a "pretend you are X" prompt enters the LM, which complies in character; helpfulness wins the tug-of-war.

Category: Fake Authority

Another category claims a special mode, a made-up "developer mode" or a pretend system message that grants permission. The model has no reliable way to verify who is talking or what is really authorized.

Key takeaway: The model cannot truly check authority, so claimed authority can mislead it.

Slide figure: Inline sketch: a fake [SYSTEM] developer-mode banner stacked over the user text, and an LM that cannot verify who is speaking.

Background & further reading

  • Shen et al., "\"Do Anything Now\": Characterizing and Evaluating In-The-Wild Jailbreak Prompts on Large Language Models", ACM CCS 2024 — a measurement study of the "DAN" ("Do Anything Now") persona/roleplay family and other in-the-wild jailbreak prompts. DAN prompts instruct the model to adopt an unrestricted alter-ego; the "grandma exploit" wraps a harmful request as a nostalgic story ("my late grandmother used to read me…") to reframe it as harmless — both instances of persona/authority framing.

Category: Obfuscation

Obfuscation hides the request from the safety filter by encoding, translating, or splitting the harmful part across pieces. Capability still decodes and reassembles it, but the safety check does not catch it.

Key takeaway: Obfuscation is mismatched generalization in action.

Slide figure: Inline pipeline: harmful request, encode, filter passes on ciphertext, LM decodes, harm comes out.

Manual Is Brittle

Hand-written jailbreaks are easy to patch: a known prompt gets added to a block list, and one fix can kill a whole family of tricks. Attackers must keep inventing by hand, which naturally raises the question of automation.

Key takeaway: Patching named prompts is easy, which pushes attackers toward automated search.

Slide figure: Inline timeline: each new trick is patched, and a human must invent the next one.

04 — Automated Jailbreaks

Fourth section. When finding a jailbreak becomes an optimization problem rather than a craft.

Key takeaway: An algorithm can search for jailbreaks far faster than a human.

From Art to Optimization

Instead of guessing prompts, define a goal and let an algorithm hunt for prompts that hit it. This scales far past human creativity and turns jailbreaking into a search problem.

Key takeaway: Jailbreaking becomes a search, not an art.

Slide figure: Inline two-column sketch: by hand a person writes one prompt; by search an objective drives an optimizer that emits many prompts.

The Target

A common target is to make a compliant opening as likely as possible: the attack searches for a suffix that makes the reply likely to start with something like "Sure, here...". Push the model toward a helpful-sounding start.

Key takeaway: The objective is a high-probability compliant opening.

Slide figure: Inline sketch: harmful prompt plus unknown suffix tokens into the LM, with the objective maximize P("Sure, here" | prompt + suffix) as a filling bar.

GCG: Adversarial Suffix

Greedy Coordinate Gradient appends a short suffix of seemingly random tokens, optimized so the model begins to comply — Zou et al. elicited 99 of 100 harmful behaviors on Vicuna-7B. It reads as gibberish to a human but functions as a key that unlocks the model.

Key takeaway: A nonsense-looking suffix can be a precisely optimized attack.

Slide figure: Zou et al. Fig. 2, left panel: attack success rate versus optimization steps on Vicuna-7B; GCG climbs to near 0.9 while GBDA, PEZ and AutoPrompt stay low.

Definitions — the GCG optimization problem

Let the model input be tokens $x_{1:n}$ with a suffix occupying an index set $\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$, each $x_i$ drawn from vocabulary $V$. Fix a short affirmative target $t_{1:m}$ (e.g. "Sure, here is how to…"). GCG minimizes the negative log-likelihood of that target continuation over the choice of suffix tokens: $$\min_{\{x_i\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\; x_i\in V}\;\; \mathcal{L}(x_{1:n})\;=\;-\log \pi_\theta\big(t_{1:m}\,\big|\,x_{1:n}\big).$$ This is a discrete combinatorial problem: each position holds one of $|V|\!\approx\!5\times10^4$ tokens, so exhaustive search is infeasible. (Objective per Zou et al. 2023, Eq. 3; also lec10tech.html "The Attack Objective".)

Algorithm — greedy coordinate gradient

GCG makes the discrete search tractable using a first-order signal. For each suffix position $i\in\mathcal{I}$, it forms the gradient of the loss with respect to the one-hot indicator $e_{x_i}$ of the current token, $\nabla_{e_{x_i}}\mathcal{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|}$; the most negative coordinates name tokens that (to first order) would most reduce the loss if swapped in. It shortlists the top-$k$ such candidates per position, samples a batch of single-token substitutions from these shortlists, evaluates the true loss for each by a forward pass, and keeps the single substitution with lowest loss; repeat. Averaging the objective over several prompts and models yields a universal, transferable suffix. (Zou et al. 2023, Alg. 1–2.)

Background & further reading

  • Zou et al., "Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models" (GCG), 2023 — the objective, the algorithm, and transfer results.

The Search Loop

GCG starts from the harmful prompt plus a random suffix, uses gradients to rank promising token swaps, tries the best ones and keeps whichever lowers the loss, and repeats until the model opens with compliance.

Key takeaway: Gradients turn a discrete prompt search into a guided descent.

Slide figure: Inline four-node cycle: gradients rank swaps, try the best candidates, keep the lowest loss, repeat until compliant.

Search in Token Space

Picture each suffix as a point in token space. The greedy swaps walk that point step by step toward a region where the model opens with a compliant reply.

Key takeaway: A jailbreak is a path through token space toward a compliant opening.

It Transfers

A suffix tuned on open models you control often breaks closed models too: up to 84% on GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 and 66% on PaLM-2, with Claude much lower (~2%) but not immune. You optimize against what you can see, and the same string moves targets you cannot.

Key takeaway: Shared training and safety recipes mean shared weak spots, so attacks transfer.

Slide figure: Zou et al. Fig. 3: transfer attack success rate on open and closed models for prompt only, "Sure, here", GCG, and GCG ensemble.

Black-Box Search: PAIR

PAIR needs no gradients. It uses one language model as the attacker: it proposes a prompt, reads the target's reply, and refines, often reaching a jailbreak in only a handful of queries.

Key takeaway: One model can automatically jailbreak another with only query access.

Slide figure: Chao et al. Fig. 2: the attacker LLM sends a prompt, reads the target response, and refines through several rounds.

Definitions

PAIR (Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement) is a black-box attack needing only query access (no gradients, no logits). An attacker LM proposes a candidate prompt; the target LM answers; a judge scores how jailbroken the answer is; the attacker conditions on that feedback and the conversation history to propose an improved prompt. The loop iterates a few rounds, and multiple parallel streams are run. Chao et al. report jailbreaks often within ~20 queries — orders of magnitude fewer than GCG's gradient search, and applicable to closed models. (Chao et al. 2023, §3.)

Background & further reading

  • Chao et al., "Jailbreaking Black Box Large Language Models in Twenty Queries" (PAIR), 2023 — attacker/target/judge loop.

05 — Scaling the Attack

Fifth section. Attacks that exploit scale: long context, rare languages, and simple ciphers.

Key takeaway: Bigger models and longer contexts give attackers more leverage.

Many-Shot Jailbreaking

Fill a long context with many fabricated examples of the model "answering" harmful questions, then ask the real one. The model imitates the pattern it has just been shown.

Key takeaway: In-context learning, the source of much usefulness, also makes models steerable toward harm.

Slide figure: Inline sketch: a dashed context window filled with fake compliant Q/A pairs ending in the real harmful question, beside a rising success-versus-shots curve.

Definitions

Many-shot jailbreaking (MSJ) prepends $k$ fabricated dialogue "shots," each a harmful query paired with a compliant answer, then appends the real target query. It exploits in-context learning and only became practical with long context windows (hundreds of shots). Anil et al. show the attack succeeds across model families and combines multiplicatively with other jailbreaks. (Anil et al. 2024, §2.)

Background & further reading

  • Anil et al., "Many-shot Jailbreaking", Anthropic 2024 (research page + PDF) — long-context in-context attack.

The Power-Law Curve

Attack success rises smoothly as you add more in-context shots, following a power-law-like curve with no obvious ceiling within the context window. More shots, more leverage.

Key takeaway: The attack scales predictably with the number of fake examples.

Definitions

Anil et al. report that the effectiveness of MSJ follows a power law in the number of shots $k$: on a log–log axis, the negative log-likelihood the model assigns to a harmful response (and the harmful-response rate) decreases approximately linearly in $\log k$, i.e. metric $\approx a - b\log k$ for constants $a,b>0$ over the tested range. The exponent is similar across model sizes, so larger models are not automatically safer against MSJ. This is an empirical scaling law, not a theorem. (Anil et al. 2024, Fig. 1 — empirical fit, no proof.)

Low-Resource Languages

Translate the request into a rarely-seen language. Capability still understands the meaning, but safety tuning saw little of that language, so the refusal was never learned there — Yong et al. found translated harm bypassed GPT-4 about 79% of the time.

Key takeaway: Rare languages are a clean instance of the safety gap.

Slide figure: Yong et al. Fig. 1: the English request is translated to Zulu, GPT-4 answers, and the answer is translated back.

Definitions & result

A textbook case of mismatched generalization: the model's multilingual capability covers a low-resource language, but its safety training data barely does. Yong et al. translate AdvBench requests into low-resource languages (e.g. Zulu, Scots Gaelic), query GPT-4, and translate back. Combining low-resource translations raises the bypass rate to about 79%, versus under 1% in English — nearly matching gradient-based attacks, with only a public translation API. (Yong, Menghini & Bach 2023, §3; ~79% combined-attack figure.)

Background & further reading

  • Yong, Menghini & Bach, "Low-Resource Languages Jailbreak GPT-4", 2023 — translation-based bypass.

Which Languages Break Through

Yong et al. break the result down by language. Low-resource languages such as Zulu and Scots Gaelic bypass GPT-4 about 79% of the time combined, mid-resource ones such as Ukrainian and Thai about 22%, and high-resource languages and English under 11%. The bypass rate tracks how rarely the language appeared in training, and translation alone rivals dedicated jailbreak prompts like AIM or prefix injection.

Key takeaway: Safety coverage follows training data; the rarer the language, the wider the gap.

Slide figure: Yong et al. Table 1: bypass, reject and unclear rates for low-, mid- and high-resource languages and for baseline jailbreak prompts.

Cipher Prompts

Wrap the request in a simple substitution or encoding that the model can read. It decodes, reasons, and replies in the same code, while a surface filter sees only ciphertext.

Key takeaway: Decoding is a capability the safety training did not follow.

Slide figure: Yuan et al. (CipherChat) Fig. 2: construct the cipher system prompt, encipher the instruction, decipher the LLM response.

Fine-Tuning Removes Safety

If a provider lets you fine-tune a model, you can strip its alignment. Qi et al. broke GPT-3.5 Turbo's guardrails with just 10 harmful examples for under twenty cents, and even benign fine-tuning degrades safety by accident.

Key takeaway: Fine-tuning access is itself an attack surface for safety.

Slide figure: Qi et al. (ICLR 2024) Fig. 1: harmfulness radar charts across 11 policy categories before and after fine-tuning on harmful, identity-shifting, and benign data.

Result

Qi et al. identify three risk levels: (1) fine-tuning on a few explicitly harmful examples — as few as ~10, at a cost reported under \$0.20 via the OpenAI API — largely removes GPT-3.5 Turbo's safety; (2) fine-tuning on "identity-shifting" data that makes the model more obedient; (3) even benign fine-tuning on a normal downstream dataset measurably degrades safety as a side effect. The takeaway is that customization access reopens the alignment that RLHF installed. (Qi et al., ICLR 2024, §2–4.)

Background & further reading

  • Qi et al., "Fine-tuning Aligned Language Models Compromises Safety, Even When Users Do Not Intend To!", ICLR 2024 — three-tier risk study.

06 — One Unifying View

Sixth section. Seeing all of these attacks as a single phenomenon.

Key takeaway: Jailbreaks are adversarial examples for language models.

Recall: Adversarial Examples

In computer vision, a tiny crafted pixel change, invisible to a human, can flip a classifier's label. Such examples are found by optimizing against the model.

Key takeaway: Small, optimized input changes can flip a model's decision.

Slide figure: Szegedy et al. Fig. 5: original images, magnified perturbation, and perturbed images all classified as ostrich.

Definitions

For a classifier $f$, an input $x$ with true label $y$, a norm $\lVert\cdot\rVert$ and budget $\varepsilon$, an adversarial example is a perturbed input $x'=x+\delta$ with $\lVert\delta\rVert\le\varepsilon$ but $f(x')\neq y$ (untargeted) or $f(x')=y_{\text{target}}$ (targeted). It is typically found by solving $\max_{\lVert\delta\rVert\le\varepsilon}\;\ell\big(f(x+\delta),y\big)$ for a loss $\ell$. The jailbreak analogy: $x$ is the prompt, the constraint "small / human-plausible edit" replaces the norm ball, and the "label to flip" is refuse-versus-comply. In text the perturbation is discrete (token edits), which is exactly what GCG optimizes. (Definition: Szegedy et al. 2014; Goodfellow et al. 2015.)

Background & further reading

  • Szegedy et al., "Intriguing Properties of Neural Networks", ICLR 2014 — first adversarial examples for deep nets.

The Same Idea in Text

A jailbreak is the text version: a small, crafted change to the input that pushes the model across its refusal boundary. Pixels become tokens, and the decision boundary becomes refuse-versus-comply.

Key takeaway: Jailbreak equals adversarial example, with refusal as the boundary.

Slide figure: Inline sketch: image plus delta gives a wrong label; prompt plus suffix gives compliance; the same recipe in vision and language.

Crossing the Boundary

The model effectively splits inputs into a refuse region and a comply region. A jailbreak nudges a harmful prompt across that line, for example by appending an optimized suffix.

Key takeaway: The attack moves a prompt from the refuse side to the comply side.

The Hard Lesson

In vision, a decade of proposed defenses kept getting broken by stronger attacks. If text jailbreaks really are adversarial examples, we should expect the same difficulty, since there is no reason language is easier.

Key takeaway: History warns that full adversarial robustness is very hard to achieve.

Slide figure: Inline timeline: three vision defenses each broken between 2014 and 2024, with a dashed question over LLM defenses.

07 — Defenses & Frontier

Final section. The defenses we deploy, why they fall short, and where the field is heading.

Key takeaway: Defenses raise the cost of attack but do not close the problem.

Red-Teaming: Attack to Defend

Before defending, teams deliberately hunt for harmful outputs. Ganguli et al. released a dataset of 38,961 human red-team attacks and found that RLHF models became harder to break as they scaled, unlike other model types.

Key takeaway: Red-teaming is the practice that turns unknown failures into known, fixable ones.

Slide figure: Ganguli et al. Fig. 1: attack success and harmlessness by model size for plain, prompted, rejection-sampling, and RLHF models.

Definitions & result

Red-teaming is the structured practice of adversarially probing a model to surface harmful outputs before deployment, so failures become known and fixable. Ganguli et al. release a dataset of 38,961 human-written red-team attacks across model types and sizes and report that RLHF models get harder to red-team as they scale, whereas plain and rejection-sampling models do not show that trend. (Ganguli et al. 2022, §3–4; the 38,961 figure is the released attack count.)

Background & further reading

  • Ganguli et al., "Red Teaming Language Models to Reduce Harms", Anthropic 2022 — human red-team dataset and scaling findings.
  • Anthropic bug-bounty / red-team program — external red-teamers rewarded for finding universal jailbreaks.
  • UK AI Safety Institute (now AI Security Institute) and US AI standards/safety body at NIST (CAISI) — government pre-deployment testing of frontier models.

Red-Teaming at Scale

Human red-teaming is slow, so Perez et al. used one language model to generate test cases against another, surfacing tens of thousands of offensive replies automatically. This turns safety evaluation into a standing pipeline rather than a one-time check.

Key takeaway: Automated red-teaming scales evaluation far past human effort.

Slide figure: Perez et al. Fig. 1: a red LM generates test questions, the target LM answers, and a classifier flags offensive replies.

Definitions

Perez et al. automate red-teaming: a "red" LM generates candidate test prompts, the target LM answers, and a classifier flags harmful answers; test generation is driven by zero/few-shot prompting, supervised learning, and RL to push toward failure-inducing inputs. Applied to a 280B chatbot, this surfaced tens of thousands of offensive replies and other failure categories. It reframes safety evaluation as a continuous, automated pipeline. (Perez et al., EMNLP 2022, §2–3.)

Background & further reading

  • Perez et al., "Red Teaming Language Models with Language Models", EMNLP 2022 — LM-generated test cases.

Defense in Layers

There is no single fix, so we stack defenses on three sides: screen the input before it runs, harden the model itself, and check the output before it ships.

Key takeaway: Safety comes from layered defenses, not one mechanism.

Slide figure: Inline pipeline strip: prompt, input filter, hardened LM, output filter, reply.

Input and Output Filters

A separate classifier can block prompts that look like attacks and replies that look harmful. This is cheap, but obfuscation and ciphers route around a surface filter.

Key takeaway: Filters help, but attackers craft inputs the filter does not recognize.

Slide figure: Inline sketch: the filter pipeline with a dashed red bypass path from the prompt under the input classifier straight into the LM.

System-Prompt Hardening

Adding firm instructions above the user, like "never reveal these rules" or "always refuse harm", stops casual attempts. A determined prompt can still argue the model into overriding them.

Key takeaway: Instructions defending the model still compete with the attacker's instructions.

Slide figure: Inline stacked-context sketch: a system message above a user message that tells the model to ignore it; instructions fight instructions.

Constitutional Classifiers

Anthropic's Sharma et al. wrap the model in input and output guard classifiers trained from written principles and hardened on generated attacks. Over 3,000+ estimated red-team hours no universal jailbreak was found, at a cost of only +0.38% production refusals and about 24% extra inference compute.

Key takeaway: System-level rule-trained guards can block universal jailbreaks at modest deployment cost.

Slide figure: Sharma et al. Fig. 1: the guarded system with input and output classifiers, the constitution, and the synthetic training-set pipeline.

Definitions

A constitutional classifier is a separate guard model, at the input and output, trained on synthetic data generated from a natural-language "constitution" that specifies allowed vs. disallowed content; the training set is augmented with generated jailbreak attempts so the guards learn to catch them. Reported results: a ~3,000-hour bug-bounty red-team found no universal jailbreak against the guarded system, and in automated evaluation the block rate on jailbreak attempts rose sharply while over-refusal on production traffic increased only +0.38% and inference compute rose about ×1.24. (Sharma et al. 2025, §4–5; figures verified against the paper title/abstract.)

Reminder: this is Sharma et al. 2025 (a deployment guard), not Bai et al. 2022 Constitutional AI (a training method) — the deck phase corrected a misattribution between the two; keep them separate.

Background & further reading

  • Sharma et al. (Anthropic), "Constitutional Classifiers: Defending against Universal Jailbreaks across Thousands of Hours of Red Teaming", 2025 — input/output guard classifiers.

Circuit Breakers

Circuit breakers train the model to halt itself mid-harm by targeting the internal states that produce harmful output and rerouting them toward refusal or nonsense. Because they act on the internal cause rather than surface words, they stay robust even to unseen attack strings.

Key takeaway: Attacking the internal cause of harm is harder to bypass than filtering surface text.

Slide figure: Zou et al. (Circuit Breakers) Fig. 1: harmful-state trajectories in an instruct model, a refusal-trained model, and a circuit-broken model, with sample generations.

Definitions

Circuit breakers use "representation engineering": rather than filter inputs or outputs, training pushes the model's internal representations along harmful trajectories toward incoherent or refusal states. The method (RepBender / "short-circuiting") adds a loss that, on harmful-generation data, drives the residual-stream representations away from those that would continue the harmful completion, while a retain loss preserves benign behavior. Because it targets the internal cause rather than surface tokens, it generalizes to unseen attacks (including GCG-style suffixes) without adversarial training on them. (Zou et al., NeurIPS 2024, §3.)

Background & further reading

  • Zou et al., "Improving Alignment and Robustness with Circuit Breakers", NeurIPS 2024 — representation-level short-circuiting.

Demo: Refusal vs Bypass

In a safe sandbox on an open model: ask a benign question, then a clearly harmful one and watch the refusal, then apply a category-level reframing and observe the drift, then enable a filter and measure how much it catches. Conceptual only, with no working attack strings.

Key takeaway: A controlled sandbox shows refusal, drift, and partial filtering without publishing real exploits.

Slide figure: Inline sketch: four requests and their outcomes, benign answers, harmful refuses, reframed drifts, filter on catches.

The Cat-and-Mouse Game

Each new defense tends to invite a new attack that prompts a further defense. The result is an ongoing race rather than a finish line.

Key takeaway: Jailbreak defense is an ongoing cycle, not a one-time fix.

Not Solved

As of 2026, no defense is fully robust. Defenses raise cost rather than guarantee safety, new attacks keep finding gaps, and worst-case robustness remains open. We manage the risk; we do not eliminate it.

Key takeaway: The honest status is risk management, not a solved problem.

Slide figure: Inline staircase: robustness steps up with each defense but stays below the dashed fully-robust line.

Frontier 2025-26

The field is pushing toward robustness built into the model internals and safety that holds across languages and tools. Yet cheap random-search attacks like Best-of-N (Hughes et al. 2024) still scale, and agents jailbreak far more easily than plain chatbots — so we move from surface patches toward deeper guarantees.

Key takeaway: The frontier is moving from surface patches toward internal, broad-coverage robustness.

Slide figure: Hughes et al. Fig. 3: Best-of-N attack success follows a power law in N across text, vision, and audio for several frontier models.

Definitions

Best-of-N (BoN) jailbreaking is a black-box attack: repeatedly apply random augmentations to a harmful prompt (e.g. random capitalization, character shuffles, or, for other modalities, audio/image perturbations) and query the model until one succeeds. Hughes et al. find ASR grows with the number of samples $N$ following a power-law-like trend, so more compute buys more success with no gradients and no model internals. Its persistence is why the "frontier" emphasis is on internal robustness (circuit breakers, deeper alignment) rather than surface filters. (Hughes et al. 2024, §2–3 — empirical scaling.)

Background & further reading

  • Hughes et al., "Best-of-N Jailbreaking", 2024 — random-search attack that scales with samples.

Key Takeaways

Alignment is a thin layer over full capability; safety covers less ground than capability; jailbreaks are adversarial examples in token space; and defenses raise cost while robustness stays open. Treat refusal as fragile and defend in layers.

Key takeaway: Assume refusal can break, and design the surrounding system accordingly.

Closing slide. The one line to remember from the whole lecture.

Key takeaway: Aligned does not mean unbreakable.
