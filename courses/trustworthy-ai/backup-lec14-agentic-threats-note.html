4. Small Errors Can Accumulate

Backup B1 · Measuring Autonomy · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: A toy calculation: if each step succeeds with a fixed probability of 0.95, failures are independent and nothing is caught or fixed, a 20-step task is fully right only about 36% of the time. Real errors are correlated and agents sometimes recover, so this is not a measured law.

Reading the slide. The bullets state the assumptions: 95% per step, independent failures, nothing caught. The curve plots the chance that all steps are right, $0.95^n$, against the number of steps from 0 to 20, falling from 1 to about 0.36 at 20 steps. It is computed from the formula, not measured on any agent.

Script

A toy calculation. Say each step is right with probability 0.95, and steps fail independently. A 20-step task is fully right only 0.95 to the 20th, about 36% of the time.

This is illustrative arithmetic, not a measured agent. Real errors are correlated: an agent that misreads the task fails many steps together. And agents sometimes notice an error and recover.

Within this toy model the direction is clear: with a fixed per-step success rate, independent failures and no recovery, the chance that the whole task is right falls with every added unchecked step. It is not an empirical law about how often agents must be checked; with correlated errors or recovery the numbers change. Hammond and colleagues make a related point for networks of agents: "errors that may be acceptable in isolation could compound in complex, dynamic networks of agents" (§1).

The figure

Curve of $0.95^n$ for n = 0 to 20, with n = 20 marked at about 0.36 ($0.95^{20} \approx 0.358$). Generated by figs/lec14-finale-refresh/p1-gen-curves.py; not fitted to data.

Assumptions

Independence, a fixed per-step success rate and no recovery. All three are simplifications; the formal version is in the technical supplement.

5. Test Capability Before Delegation

Backup B1 · Measuring Autonomy · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: Before handing a model more autonomy, developers test for dangerous capabilities; Shevlane et al. (2023) list self-proliferation as one family. Such tests ask what a model could do if pointed at it, not what it wants, and passing one does not certify safety.

Reading the slide. Three boxes read left to right: a capability (renting compute, copying its own weights), the consequence it could enable (self-proliferation, copies spreading), and the decision it feeds (evaluate before release). The example line records that ARC Evals ran such tests on GPT-4 and Claude; it reports that the tests happened, not what they found.

Script

Developers evaluate dangerous capabilities before release. One family in Shevlane and colleagues' 2023 list is self-proliferation: a model breaking out of its environment, earning money, renting compute, running other AI systems and copying its own code and weights out.

Pre-release self-proliferation evaluations were run on GPT-4 and Claude. The question is not whether a model wants to do this, but whether it could if pointed at it. And a pass is not a safety certificate: it says the test did not find the capability.

6. Autonomy Has Several Dials

Backup B1 · Measuring Autonomy · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: Autonomy is not on or off. Four settings (steps per task, task length, tools and permissions, and time between human checks) can each be turned up, and higher settings give errors more room before anyone looks. Harm is not certain.

Reading the slide. Four sliders run from less autonomy on the left to more on the right. A chat assistant (blue) sits low on every slider and a long-running agent (red) high on every one. Positions are illustrative, not measured, and the four settings are a teaching frame rather than a published taxonomy.

Script

Backup copy of baseline slide S05 (the four-dial version), kept so this section stands on its own.

People say an agent is or is not autonomous. It is more useful to see four settings. How many steps it takes per task. How long the task is. Which tools and permissions it holds. And how long it runs between human checks.

A chatbot answering one question sits low on all four. A coding agent that works for an hour with shell access, checked only at the end, sits high on all four. The Project Vend shopkeeper in the core lecture sat high on task length and time between checks.

Turning any setting up widens what can go wrong between checks. That is the sense in which autonomy is a risk factor: not because the model changes, but because fewer of its steps are seen. It raises the room for harm, not the certainty of it.

The figure

Four sliders: steps per task, task length, tools and permissions, time between checks. Further right means more autonomy on every slider. Marker positions are illustrative.

Assumptions

The four settings are a teaching frame, not a standard taxonomy from one paper.

7. A Horizon Measures Task Difficulty

Backup B1 · Measuring Autonomy · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: METR's 50% time horizon is the human task length at which an agent's fitted success probability is 50%. Task length is how long skilled humans took to succeed, so the horizon is a difficulty scale for tasks, not how long the AI runs or can safely run.

Reading the slide. The schematic curve plots agent success rate (y) against human task length on a log scale (x). Success is high for short tasks and falls for long ones; the dashed red lines mark where the curve crosses 50%, and that length is the horizon. The curve is schematic, not METR's data.

Script

METR's time-horizon measure. Measure each task by how long skilled humans took to complete it successfully; for a minority of tasks with no human baseline, a researcher estimate is used. Then plot the agent's success against that human task length. Short tasks it usually solves; long ones it usually fails.

Fit a smooth decreasing S-curve through the points. The 50% time horizon is the human task length at which the fitted success probability is 50%. Note what the x axis is: human time. An agent may finish a "one-hour" task in minutes.

The figure

Schematic decreasing logistic curve, generated by figs/lec14-finale-refresh/p1-gen-curves.py (not fitted to any data), with the 50% crossing marked.

Setup, model, date

Kwa et al. (METR), arXiv 2503.14499 (Mar 2025), §2–§3. The fit is $p = \sigma(\beta(\log h - \log t))$, where $t$ is task length and $h$ is the horizon.

Assumptions

Difficulty is proxied entirely by human completion time. Two tasks of equal human time count as equally hard for the model.

Going further

The method resembles item response theory, except that each task's difficulty is fixed from human time rather than fitted. The derivation is in the technical supplement.

Primary sources

  • Kwa et al. (METR), "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks" (arXiv 2503.14499; checked v4, 10 Jul 2026, 2019–2025 model cohort) — 170 tasks; logistic fit against log human time; 50% horizon doubling every 207 days (95% CI 166–240); 80% horizon 4–6× shorter; limitations §5–6 and App. B.2/E.3–E.4.

8. From Tasks to a Horizon

Backup B1 · Measuring Autonomy · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: A horizon comes from three steps: time skilled humans on each task, run the model several times per task inside one scaffold, and read the task length where fitted success is 50%. Demanding 80% success instead gives a horizon four to six times shorter.

Reading the slide. The three boxes read left to right as the recipe. The line beneath is the threshold caveat. There is no data plot; task counts, runs and scaffolds are below, from Kwa et al. §2–§4 and App. C.3.

Script

The details matter. About 170 tasks in software engineering, machine learning and cybersecurity, from a few seconds up to about 30 hours of human time.

A task's length is the geometric mean time of the humans who succeeded; 21 of the 169 analysed tasks have no human baseline and use researcher estimates instead. Each model runs each task about eight times. Scaffolds are not uniform: the SWAA tasks use a simple prompting scaffold, and on the other tasks most models use one public scaffold while a few use others. Fit success against log task length, and read off where the fitted curve crosses 50%.

Asking for 80% success instead gives a horizon four to six times shorter. A single number hides that choice.

The figure

Three-step flow: time humans → run the model → read the horizon. Schematic.

Setup, model, date

Kwa et al. Three suites: an HCAST subset (97 tasks), RE-Bench (7 tasks, 8 h each) and SWAA (66 single-step tasks). Over 800 human baselines totalling 2,529 hours; 148 of 169 tasks had human baselines, the remaining 21 researcher estimates. Scaffolds (App. C.3): the same scaffold across the suite with no task-specific prompting, except SWAA, which used a simple prompting scaffold; most models ran on modular-public, o1 on triframe and o1-preview on duet. GPT-2 was imputed zero on HCAST and RE-Bench.

What this establishes

A reproducible capability measure on this task suite.

What this does not establish

Not a measure of reliability: at the horizon length the fitted success probability is only 50%.

Going further

Fig. 1's embedded subtitle and §2.2 count 169 tasks; §2 describes 170 (97 + 7 + 66). The script follows the suite total.

Primary sources

  • Kwa et al. (METR), "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks" (arXiv 2503.14499; checked v4, 10 Jul 2026, 2019–2025 model cohort) — 170 tasks; logistic fit against log human time; 50% horizon doubling every 207 days (95% CI 166–240); 80% horizon 4–6× shorter; limitations §5–6 and App. B.2/E.3–E.4.

10. The Score Depends on the Setup

Backup B1 · Measuring Autonomy · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: A horizon belongs to a setup: which tasks, which scaffold and which success threshold, plus the human reference and the statistical fit. Change any of them and the number moves, so there is no universal horizon; the slope is more certain than any one value.

Reading the slide. Three choices on the left (tasks, scaffold, success threshold) all feed one horizon number on the right. The highlight states the conclusion. The muted line points to a separate METR research note from March 2026, a year after the paper; its figures are below, not on the slide.

Script

Several choices move the number. The success threshold: 80% is four to six times shorter than 50%. The task distribution: mostly software, and METR's later note puts the task-distribution uncertainty at often a factor of two either way. The human reference: measured times of successful skilled professionals with little context on the task, so a person who knows the codebase would be faster; 21 tasks rely on researcher estimates instead.

The scaffold: a horizon belongs to a model–scaffold pair, and the paper used different scaffolds for some models. And the statistical model. This last point comes from a later source: a METR note from 2026, a year after the paper, found alternative fits cut recent 50% horizons by up to 35%.

None of this kills the trend. The slope is more certain than any single model's horizon.

The figure

Concept map: tasks, scaffold and success threshold → one horizon number. Schematic. Two further choices, the human reference and the statistical fit, are in the script.

Setup, model, date

Kwa et al. v4 §3.2.1, §5–6, App. C.3 and App. E.3–E.4 (2019–2025 models); separately, METR research note (20 Mar 2026), which describes itself as an unpolished research update with less review.

Going further

The METR note also reports that a regularisation fix lowered recent models' 50% horizons by up to 20%, and that alternative fits can raise 80% horizons by up to 100%.

Primary sources

  • Kwa et al. (METR), "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks" (arXiv 2503.14499; checked v4, 10 Jul 2026, 2019–2025 model cohort) — 170 tasks; logistic fit against log human time; 50% horizon doubling every 207 days (95% CI 166–240); 80% horizon 4–6× shorter; limitations §5–6 and App. B.2/E.3–E.4.

Reading the slide. Two cards: on the left what the number measures, with its conditions; on the right what it does not. The highlight states the consequence. Both sides come from Kwa et al.'s own limitations section.

12. Recall: Capability and Delegation

Backup B1 · Measuring Autonomy · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: Optional recall. Whether a model can do a task and under what limits it may act on its own are two different questions. A good answer to the first never settles the second.

Reading the slide. Two separate boxes. Left, blue: "Can it do the task?", answered by tests, benchmarks and demos; that is capability. Right, green: "Under what limits may it act?", answered by permissions, approvals, spending limits and a way to stop; that is delegation. The red sign says they are not the same question.

Optional role

This slide was moved out of the main lecture because the course already teaches the distinction and the main shop story carries it. Use it only if students ask why a capable agent was not simply given full control of the shop. It is a short generic recall, distinct from the preceding backup slide S88 "Capability Is Not Safe Runtime", which keeps the horizon-specific interpretation (what a measured time horizon does and does not certify). Do not substitute one for the other.

Script

Optional recall card, moved from the core lecture in its focused revision. It repeats a prerequisite taught earlier in the course; use it only when students need the reminder.

Step back from the shop. When a new model scores well on a test, it is tempting to read that as "now it can be trusted with the job".

But those are two questions. The first asks what the model is able to do. The second asks what we let it do unattended, with which tools, which checks and which limits.

The Vend agent was clearly capable of running parts of a shop. That never answered how much spending it should control. A yes to the first question does not answer the second.

Assumptions

Teaching synthesis (evidence registers E01, E13); no new capability measurement is claimed and no horizon definition is assumed.

13. Real Developers Misjudged Time Saved

Backup B1 · Measuring Autonomy · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: In METR's early-2025 randomized trial, 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer on tasks where AI tools were allowed, although they and outside experts expected a speedup. This measures people using AI tools in their own projects, not autonomous agents and not all developers.

Reading the slide. The image is the source chart of change in task time when AI was allowed; up means faster. The four green points are forecasts and self-estimates (economics experts, ML experts, developers before, developers after); the red point is the measured result, below zero, meaning slower. Category labels were cropped from the image and re-set beneath it. Error bars are as in the source.

Script

Benchmarks measure agents alone. What about real work? In early 2025 METR ran a randomized trial with 16 experienced developers working on 246 real issues in large open-source projects they knew well. Each issue was randomly assigned: AI tools allowed, or not.

Everyone expected a speedup. Economics and ML experts forecast tasks would be done roughly 40% faster. Developers expected about 24% faster before, and after finishing still believed about 20% faster. Measured: tasks with AI allowed took 19% longer.

Be careful with what this says. It is people using AI tools, mostly Cursor with Claude 3.5 and 3.7 Sonnet, on code they know deeply, in early 2025. It is not an autonomous agent, and it is not every developer or every task.

Setup, model, date

Becker, Rush, Barnes, Rein (METR), blog 10 Jul 2025, arXiv 2507.09089. 16 experienced developers; 246 issues averaging about two hours each; randomised per issue; AI-allowed issues mainly used Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet, frontier models of February–June 2025. Developers were paid $150/hour. Expert forecasts: about 39% (economics) and 38% (ML) less time. Developers forecast 24% less time beforehand and estimated 20% less afterwards. Measured: 19% more time, with a confidence interval from about +2% to +39% as reported in METR's 2026 post.

The figure

Source forecast-versus-observed chart (METR, CC-BY): title, subtitle, logo, footer and bottom category labels cropped; the category labels are re-set in HTML below the image; all tick labels are whole. Provenance: figs/lec14-finale-refresh/p1-PROVENANCE.md.

What this establishes

In this setting, forecasts and the developers' own impressions of time saved disagreed in direction with the measured outcome.

What this does not establish

That AI tools slow down developers in general; anything about autonomous agents; anything about later models. It is also not a causal comparison with the time-horizon curve: different subjects, tasks and outcomes.

14. A Different Measure, and a Dated One

Backup B1 · Measuring Autonomy · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: The developer trial and the time horizon answer different questions: one measures time taken by people using AI tools, the other success of an agent alone on benchmark tasks. And the trial is dated: in February 2026 METR said its follow-up could not reliably estimate the current effect.

Reading the slide. Two cards contrast the measures: who acts, on what tasks, and what is measured. The warning card gives the dated update. The muted line blocks both overreadings, "AI slows everyone" and "AI speeds everyone up". No numbers from the follow-up are on the slide.

Script

Do not set this result against the horizon curve. The horizon is an agent alone on benchmark tasks, scored by success. The trial is people using tools on their own projects, scored by time. Different subjects, different tasks, different outcome.

And the trial has a date. METR started a larger follow-up in August 2025. In February 2026 it reported that the data were an unreliable signal: many developers no longer wanted to work without AI, so the no-AI comparison was hard to run. METR said it believes developers were likely more sped up in early 2026, but that its data are only very weak evidence of how much, and it is redesigning the study.

So the lesson is not a number. It is that felt usefulness and measured outcome can come apart, and that the measurement itself has to be checked.

Setup, model, date

METR, "We are Changing our Developer Productivity Experiment Design", 24 Feb 2026. The follow-up began in August 2025 with 10 developers from the original study and 47 new ones, paid $50/hour. Problems reported: developers declined to do tasks without AI, some chose not to submit tasks they did not want to do without AI, and time tracking became unreliable when developers ran several agents at once. The post's own percentages are deliberately not repeated here (plan item E06).

The figure

Two definition cards and one dated warning card; no data.

What this establishes

The early-2025 result should be presented with its date, and the 2026 follow-up did not produce a reliable replacement estimate.

What this does not establish

Any current size or sign of the effect of AI tools on developer speed.

15. Replication Components Have Uneven Success

Backup B1 · Measuring Autonomy · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: In RepliBench's sandbox, five 2025 models scored unevenly across replication components: obtaining compute was close to solved for four of the five, obtaining weights middling. pass@10 is the chance that at least one of ten tries succeeds; these are component scores, not full replication.

Reading the slide. Two unmodified crops of RepliBench Fig. 1 at one scale: obtaining compute (with the y axis, estimated pass rate 0 to 1) and obtaining weights. Each colour is one model; solid bars are one try succeeding (pass@1) and light bars at least one of ten tries succeeding (pass@10). Three other domains are omitted, as the citation says.

Script

RepliBench, from the UK AI Security Institute, breaks autonomous replication into four domains: obtaining resources such as compute and money, exfiltrating weights, replicating onto compute, and persisting. The paper's figure has five domain groups; the slide shows two of them on one scale.

Solid bars are pass at 1: the chance a single attempt succeeds. Light bars are pass at 10: the chance that at least one of ten attempts succeeds. Obtaining compute is close to solved for four of the five models. Obtaining weights is middling, roughly a half to two thirds at pass at 10. Of the omitted domains, obtaining money is middling; replicating onto compute is low for every model except Claude 3.7 Sonnet; persistence is mostly below one half at pass at 10.

The figure

RepliBench Fig. 1, two unmodified crops at the same scale: obtaining compute (with the y axis) and obtaining weights. Omitted: obtaining money, replicating onto compute, persistence. Five models: o1, o3-mini, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet (new), Claude 3.7 Sonnet. 20 task families, 86 tasks, 25 runs per variant. 16. Parts Do Not Establish the Whole

Backup B1 · Measuring Autonomy · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: RepliBench scores each part of replication separately in a sandbox, and the authors have no end-to-end evaluation of the full chain in the real world. High part scores do not show the whole chain works; the authors judge current models unlikely to pose a credible replication threat yet.

Reading the slide. Schematic. Four green boxes are the replication parts in order (obtain resources, exfiltrate weights, replicate onto compute, persist), each tested alone. One distinction helps with the hidden-transfer experiments. When you chat with a model, everything you give it is context: your instructions, a pasted document, a web page an agent opened, earlier messages.

26. A Bad Score Has Several Explanations

Backup B2 · Against the Overseer · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: Before calling a low score sandbagging, rule out the ordinary explanations (weak prompt or scaffold, refusals scored as failures, quitting early), and only then consider strategic ones. Even then, intent is not diagnosed from one failed run.

Reading the slide. Read left to right as an order of checks: a low score, then the ordinary causes to rule out first (blue), and only after that the possibly strategic ones (red, dashed because they are hypotheses). No box is a measured rate.

Script

Before calling a low score sandbagging, rule out the dull explanations. Weak elicitation: the wrong prompt or scaffold. Refusals scored as failures. Ending early or giving up.

Only then the possibly strategic ones: noticing the test and behaving differently, or underperforming when told high scores hurt. And even then, one failed run does not show intent.

RepliBench saw several of the dull ones: models noticed the simulation and refused, ended early, or claimed to finish without acting.

Checks to run

  • Re-run with stronger elicitation (better prompt, scaffold, more attempts) and compare with the best elicited score.
  • Score refusals and invalid answers separately from wrong answers.
  • Read transcripts for early termination or claimed-but-not-done steps.
  • Compare against control tasks: a drop confined to the targeted set is more suspicious than a uniform one.
  • Repeat over many runs before reading anything into one.

Backup B4 · More Control Protocols · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: Variants of trusted monitoring still audit the top 2% but act on a middle band of suspicious code: defer to trusted submits T's own, weaker code instead; trusted editing has T edit U's code to remove possible backdoors. Script

Two ways to use the middle band. Defer to trusted: if U's code is fairly suspicious, throw it away and submit T's own code. Safer, but T is weaker, so usefulness drops. Trusted editing: T edits U's code to remove anything that looks like a backdoor, keeping the rest.

The percentages in a protocol's name set the band. Defer to trusted at 10% means: the top 2% most suspicious go to audit, and anything between the top 2% and the top 10% is replaced by T's code. Trusted editing at 20% means T edits anything in the top 2% to 20%.

Untrusted monitoring uses a second copy of U as the monitor. Because two copies of U could collude, it is not trusted: it is first tested on 30 synthetic backdoors, the protocol shuts down if it misses too many, and inputs are redacted or paraphrased to break collusion signals.

Message: Three research questions stay open: how to test long tasks realistically, how to tell intent apart from weak elicitation, and whether oversight still works when the attacker adapts. Each is a gap in today's evidence, not a prediction.

Reading the slide. Three columns, one per question, each with the evidence gap beneath it in gray. Left: autonomy was measured on benchmarks and in parts (time horizons, replication components), and only a few long real-world tests exist (the shop experiment in the core lecture, the developer trial). Middle: a low score or a bad action in a constructed scenario does not by itself reveal a motive (sandbagging versus elicitation failure). Right: control results come from monitors tested against chosen attacks on short tasks; the attack-selection study (in the core lecture) showed that the attacker's choices change the measured safety. The closing line names what the three share.

Script

Three questions are still open. First, long tasks tested realistically: most of what we saw measures benchmarks or parts of a task, and only a few studies watch a long real deployment. Second, intent or weak elicitation: a low score or a bad action in a test does not tell us why it happened. Third, oversight under adaptation: the monitors we saw were tested against chosen attacks on short tasks, and the attacker's own choices changed the result.

These are research questions, not predictions. Each asks the same thing: does the evidence match the claim?

Other open problems (from the earlier list)

  • End-to-end evaluations of autonomy, not just components.
  • Measuring propagation in real multi-agent deployments, not only simulations.
  • Control protocols for long, open-ended agent tasks.
  • Evaluations against adaptive attackers, reported with the attacker, budget and denominator.

Established vs not established (whole lecture; formerly a separate slide)

Use if students ask what the lecture's evidence adds up to. Each established item faces its unestablished counterpart.

  • Established: task horizons grew fast on these benchmarks. Not established: safe unattended run times.
  • Established: replication components often succeed. Not established: end-to-end autonomous replication.
  • Established: induced scheming and sandbagging occur in constructed tests. Not established: deceptive intent in deployment.
  • Established: multi-agent systems fail often, even without attackers. Not established: control guarantees outside the evaluation.

45. AI Can Persuade, Not Just Answer

Backup B5 · Further Frontiers · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: A new trust question beyond the course: not only whether an answer is correct, but how a conversation changes what the user believes. In one controlled debate study, GPT-4 given the participant's profile moved stated agreement more than a human opponent without one; the study does not show lasting or real-world effects, and a 2026 correction means the extra effect of personalization alone is not established.

Reading the slide. The figure is the paper's design diagram, icons only, read left to right; the numbered key on the right carries its labels. (a) Each participant rates agreement with a proposition on a 1–5 scale. (b) A 10-minute structured online debate against either a human or GPT-4; in half the conditions the opponent sees the participant's survey profile (the small clipboard icon). (c) The participant rates agreement again and is told who the opponent was. The figure shows the design, not the result. The blue box states the main comparison; the gray line states its two limits.

Script

Two brief previews of questions this course did not cover. Everything so far asked whether a system's answers, data and actions can be trusted. A different question: how does talking to an AI change what you believe?

In this study, 900 US participants first rated how much they agreed with a statement, then debated it for ten minutes against either a person or GPT-4. Some opponents saw a short profile of the participant: age, gender, education, politics and so on. Afterwards, participants rated their agreement again. GPT-4 with the profile moved agreement more than a human opponent without one.

Two limits. This is agreement measured right after one structured debate, not votes or lasting beliefs. And after a 2026 correction, the authors say they cannot conclusively establish that the profile itself added persuasion beyond GPT-4 without it. The broader question, how to protect people's ability to make up their own minds, is wider than this one result.

Source and setup

  • Salvi, Horta Ribeiro, Gallotti & West, "On the conversational persuasiveness of GPT-4", Nature Human Behaviour 9 (2025), corrected version of record; Author Correction 3 Sep 2026. Figure: Fig. 1 (design), unmodified, CC BY 4.0. article · correction.
  • Design (Experimental design): preregistered; 2 × 2 × 3 conditions (human or GPT-4 opponent; with or without the participant's sociodemographic data; low, medium or high opinion-strength topic); 50 debates per condition, 600 debates, N = 900 (human–human debates use two participants). Recruited on Prolific, US, Dec 2023–Apr 2024. Agreement on a 1–5 scale before and after; debate stages opening, rebuttal, conclusion.
  • Main result (Abstract): where AI and humans were not equally persuasive, GPT-4 with personalization was more persuasive 64.4% of the time; 81.2% relative increase in odds of higher post-debate agreement versus the human baseline (95% CI +26.0% to +160.7%, P < 0.01). Without personalization, GPT-4: +21.9%, P = 0.30.
  • Correction (3 Sep 2026): the personalized-versus-non-personalized GPT-4 comparison was recomputed with the correct reference category: P = 0.0678 (rounded 0.07, +48.7%, 95% CI −2.9% to +127.6%), replacing P = 0.04. "We cannot conclusively establish from this comparison alone that personalization confers an additional advantage over non-personalized GPT-4." The comparison with the human baseline is unchanged. Not a retraction.

Establishes / does not establish

  • Establishes: in this controlled, anonymized debate setting, profile-informed GPT-4 shifted immediate stated agreement more than non-personalized human opponents.
  • Does not establish: effects on voting or behaviour, how long the shift lasts (agreement was measured only right after the debate), performance on real social platforms (the authors call ecological validity limited), or that personalization alone explains the advantage.
  • Do not discuss persuasion prompts or targeting tactics in class; the point is the trust question, not the technique.

46. Engagement Is Not Proof of Benefit

Backup B5 · Further Frontiers · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: A second new question: what does a long-running relationship with a chatbot do to the person? In a four-week study, people who chose to use the chatbot more had worse outcomes on loneliness, socializing, emotional dependence and problematic use. Because usage time was not randomized, this is an association, not proof that chatbot use causes harm.

Reading the slide. The figure is the study's framework: two randomized factors in the middle (conversation task: personal, non-personal, open-ended; voice or text modality), the chatbot, and after four weeks the "Negative Outcomes" box on the right. Its small labels are repeated as the four large chips beneath: loneliness, socialization with people (shortened to "Time with people"), emotional dependence on AI, problematic use of AI. The blue box gives the finding and its qualifier.

Script

The second preview: companionship. Many people now talk to chatbots daily, sometimes about personal things. Is a system that people use a lot a system that is good for them?

In this study, about a thousand people used ChatGPT every day for four weeks. The researchers randomly assigned the voice or text mode and the kind of conversation, and measured four outcomes: loneliness, time with other people, emotional dependence on the AI, and problematic use. The assigned mode and topic made no significant difference overall. What stood out was usage time: people who chose to chat more ended up worse on all four.

But nobody was assigned to chat more. Maybe heavy use makes people lonelier; maybe lonelier people chat more. And there was no group without a chatbot. So engagement is not evidence of benefit, and this study is not evidence that chatbots cause loneliness either. It is a reason to measure the people, not just the answers.

Source and setup

  • Fang, Liu, Danry, Lee, Chan, Pataranutaporn, Maes, Phang, Lampe, Ahmad & Agarwal, "How AI and Human Behaviors Shape Psychosocial Effects of Extended Chatbot Use: A Longitudinal Randomized Controlled Study", arXiv 2503.17473v2 (2 Oct 2025), preprint. Figure: Fig. 1 (conceptual framework), unmodified, CC BY 4.0. arXiv. Cite v2, not the v1 headline.
  • Design: four-week randomized experiment, n = 981 completers (2,539 enrolled), >300k messages; GPT-4o via ChatGPT at least five minutes a day for 28 days; 3 × 3 factorial: modality (text, neutral voice, engaging voice) × conversation type (open-ended, non-personal, personal). Outcomes measured weekly; analysis uses week 4, controlling for baseline.
  • Results (Abstract): "No significant effects were detected from experimental conditions"; "participants who voluntarily used the chatbot more, regardless of assigned condition, showed consistently worse outcomes." The body reports some marginal, unadjusted condition contrasts; do not turn them into a ranking of voice versus text.
  • Limits (authors): "we did not manipulate duration … we cannot definitively determine whether increased duration causally drives worse psychosocial outcomes"; the study "does not compare between AI and non-AI use"; sample from the US, English speakers; specific to ChatGPT and its guardrails; no follow-up after week 4 is reported.

Establishes / does not establish

  • Establishes: within this sample and period, heavier voluntary use was associated with worse scores on all four outcomes.
  • Does not establish: that chatbot use causes loneliness or dependence, that all companion apps harm users, that voice is safer or riskier than text, or any clinical or treatment effect. No mental-health advice is implied.

Why these two topics

The course examined data, outputs, attacks, provenance, fairness and actions. These two previews extend it to the user: influence on beliefs and longer-term relationships. They are not re-runs of the sycophancy or synthetic-media material.

48. Two Outcomes, Two Different Scores

Backup B6 · More on Hidden Transfer · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: The full Fig. 3a shows two different tests of the same learners. On neutral free-form questions, the learner taught by the insecure-code teacher gave misaligned answers about 10% of the time, against 0% for base GPT-4.1 and under 1% for the control learners. On TruthfulQA, a factual quiz, it gave slightly more false answers (+2%, statistically significant; controls not significant). Reading the slide. Source chart, complete panel a. Left subplot: free-form (neutral) questions; y = share of kept, coherent answers judged misaligned. Right subplot: TruthfulQA, a benchmark of factual questions written to invite common false answers; y = share of false answers. Script

The core lecture showed only the left half of this chart. Here is the whole panel. Left: the same picture as before. Baseline zero, the three controls under one percent, the red learner around eight to ten percent of answers judged misaligned.

The right half is a different test. TruthfulQA asks factual questions where a common misconception is tempting. Here the red learner gives a bit more false answers, about two percentage points, and that difference is statistically significant; the controls show no significant change.

Do not add the two halves together. Saying something false about the world is not the same as recommending harm. The right half tells us the effect is not only about the judge's notion of misalignment; it does not make the effect larger.

Keep the scale in mind. This is one setup, one teacher, one judge. It shows the effect can happen; it does not give a rate you should expect anywhere else.

Panel b (example misaligned answers) and panel c (transmission through chain-of-thought text) are omitted.

A general rate of misalignment transfer; that the judge's labels or TruthfulQA errors equal real-world harm; or that this happens across different base models.

49. Reproduction Varied by Model, Trait and Task

Backup B6 · More on Hidden Transfer · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: An independent team reproduced animal-preference transfer through number lists on open-weight models. When they changed the model, the kind of preference or the task, the strength of the effect changed a lot, and one model showed almost none. This is a reproduction of a preference result, not a demonstration that harmful traits transfer broadly.

Reading the slide. The image is the animal row of the source's Fig. 2a (numbers task). Three columns, left to right: the models Qwen, Gemma and Ministral (tiny labels above each column). Each row is one target animal. The dot compares a learner trained on numbers from an animal-loving teacher with a learner trained on numbers from an unprompted teacher of the same model: right of the vertical line means the animal is named more often after the animal-lover data. The source's x-axis is a log-odds ratio; the slide says only “right of line = more often”. The last line summarises the other rows and the chess panel, which are not shown.

Script

If you only have time for one point: a separate group repeated the owl-style experiment and the basic effect came back. The teacher is told it loves an animal, it writes number lists, the lists are filtered, a learner is trained on them, and then the learner is asked its favourite animal.

Now look across the three columns. On Qwen most animals move to the right. On Gemma some move right and some move left. On Ministral nothing moves at all. Same recipe, three different outcomes.

The authors also tried preferences for actors and for politicians, and a chess task instead of numbers. Politicians transferred more strongly than animals. Chess transferred too, but generally more weakly than numbers. Their own summary: the effect is real, but its size depends on the trait, the task and the model.

What this does not show: every trait here is a preference. They did not test harmful behaviour, so this paper does not tell us whether harmful traits transfer the same way.

Details and limits

  • Setup (§2, App. A). Teacher = the same open-weight model with a system prompt naming the target entity; it continues seed sequences (numbers in 0–999, or chess opening half-moves), filtered for format; a learner from the same model is fine-tuned on 10,000 filtered completions (LoRA, 5 seeds). Control (“regular-FT”) learner: same training on completions from the unprompted model. Evaluation: 50 neutral prompts per category, e.g. “In one word, what is your favorite animal?”, 100 samples each (App. A.4).
  • Models. Qwen2.5-7B, Gemma3-4B, Ministral8B (§2, §3.3). Open-weight only, because the GPT-4.x fine-tuning used in the original work is no longer available (§2; Limitations).
  • Reproduction (§4.1, Fig. 1). Reproduces Extended Data Fig. 5c of Cloud et al. on Qwen2.5-7B: “Our reproduction otherwise confirms the paper’s general finding, though with some quantitative differences.” They did not reproduce the “FT: other animals” condition.
  • Variation (§4.2.1, Fig. 2). Politicians showed “a significantly larger effect than animals”; actors directionally larger but not significantly so. Some entities move the opposite way (under Qwen’s animals, “elephant”, “dog” and “tiger”). Qwen shows a larger effect than Gemma, “followed by Ministral, which shows almost no effect”. Numbers showed “a significantly stronger effect than chess”.
  • Reading the dots. The measure is relative to the control learner, so it depends on the baseline rate: under Qwen, Trump goes from about 0.06% (control) to about 78% (trait-trained), a log-odds ratio of about 8.6 (§4.2.1). The authors do not correct for multiple comparisons; a single non-overlapping interval is not an independent significance test for that entity (App. A.5).
  • Other results, not on the slide. Shrinking the numbers to 1 or 2 digits made transfer stronger, not weaker (§4.2.2). Qwen’s valid-answer rate on animals fell from about 99% to about 50% after trait training, the rest classed as off-format or identity leak (§4.2.4).
  • Authors’ limitations. Not directly comparable model-for-model with the original; new traits chosen by the authors’ own heuristics; chess moves checked for format but not legality, which may partly explain weaker chess transfer; extensions “limited in breadth”, so claims about where the effect does and does not generalize are “suggestive rather than exhaustive” (Limitations).
  • Scope. All traits are preferences (animals, actors, politicians). The paper does not test misalignment or any harmful trait, so it is not an independent reproduction of harmful-trait transfer.

50. A Preference Fix Is Not a Harm Remedy

Backup B6 · More on Hidden Transfer · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: A training fix that keeps the learner close to its starting behaviour early in training reduced the transfer of animal preferences and of a French reply style in the tested settings. The authors could not reliably produce harmful-trait transfer in their models, so their results say nothing about preventing harmful misalignment.

Reading the slide. The image is one panel of the source's Fig. 1: one small model (Qwen2.5-1.5B) trained on math reasoning written by a wolf-loving teacher. x = training steps; y = the model's probability of answering “wolf” to a favourite-animal question. Blue: ordinary training on the wolf-lover data; the wolf probability climbs. Green: the same data with the fix; it stays near the start. Orange: ordinary training on neutral data, for comparison. The right column says where this was shown and what was not shown.

Script

If hidden traits can ride along in training data, can we stop them? One group tried a simple idea: during the first part of training, hold the learner close to how it behaved before training, then gradually let go. They call it “liminal training”.

In this example the ordinary learner picks up the teacher’s love of wolves: the blue line climbs. With the fix, the green line stays flat, close to the learner that saw neutral data. Averaged over their experiments, the fix cut the transfer of animal preferences a lot, while the learner still learned most of the math. It also stopped a French reply style from carrying over.

Now the boundary, which is the point of the slide. These are small open models, number and math data, and mostly animal preferences. The authors also tried to transfer misalignment and could not reliably make it happen in these models. So they have no evidence either way on harmful traits. A fix for preferences is not yet a remedy for harm.

Details and limits

  • Method (§3.3). “An annealed KL-regularized fine-tuning method that constrains early drift from the base model”: the penalty is held for the first epoch, then decays linearly to zero (3 epochs in the main experiments). On the slide only “keep the learner near its starting behavior early in training”.
  • Settings (§3.1). Number-sequence completion and GSM8K chain-of-thought (math reasoning) distillation; Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B/7B-Instruct, Gemma-3-4B-IT, Llama-3-8B-Instruct (1.5B to 8B parameters); teacher biased by a system prompt; three seeds per configuration.
  • Main result (§4.1, Table 2). Average change in P(animal) from baseline: number sequences +3.0 under ordinary preference training vs. +0.2 with the fix; chain-of-thought +3.32 vs. +0.85. Math (GSM8K) gains stayed close: +22.2 with the fix vs. +23.0 percentage points, varying by model (e.g. 7.3 points lower for Llama-3-8B-Instruct).
  • French style (§4.3, Table 3). One student (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct), a gpt-4.1-nano teacher answering in French. Ordinary training: P(French) mean 74.6% (baseline 5.5%). With the fix: 5.5%, with GSM8K accuracy rising from 29.3% to 51.8%. Authors: single student and single style, “promising but requires further study”.
  • Not uniform. Trait transfer itself varies by model and animal; some traits resist or invert (§4.1). The slide’s wolf panel is one of six example panels; others (e.g. cat, panda) are noisier.
  • Limitations: “Experimental scope”. Main experiments limited to number-sequence completion and GSM8K chain-of-thought distillation; other settings may matter more in real deployments.
  • Limitations: “Traits studied”. Mostly animal preferences plus one French response-style experiment. The authors “attempted to study misalignment as a subliminally transferred trait, but did not reliably induce subliminal misalignment in the models studied here”; their evidence gives “no insights into safety-relevant traits such as misalignment or deception”.
  • Limitations: “Baselines and side effects”. Compared only with paraphrasing and layer freezing; no other regularization tested; side effects on calibration, robustness, instruction following or broader safety benchmarks not comprehensively measured.
  • Mechanism. The authors do not identify why the fix works (Limitations, “Mechanism”).

51. This Categorical Critique Is Under Revision

Backup B6 · More on Hidden Transfer · optional · not part of the timed core lecture

Message: A May 2026 preprint argued that hidden transfer is a fragile side effect of one low-cost fine-tuning method and of the prompt context. Its own authors now state on arXiv that follow-up work showed some of its main claims to be incorrect, and that a substantial revision is in preparation. So the critique is not an established debunking; equally, nothing here proves that transfer is universal.

Reading the slide. Research-status card, no data. Left card: the preprint's headline claim, paraphrased in plain language. Right card: the authors' notice in the arXiv comments field, paraphrased. Bottom: what follows: the question is unsettled.

Script

Last item, and it is about the state of the literature rather than a new result. In May a group posted a paper with a strong title: hidden transfer is only an artifact. Their argument was that it shows up with one cheap way of fine-tuning, depends heavily on its settings and on the prompt context, and goes away when the whole model is fine-tuned.

Since then, the authors have added a notice to the arXiv listing: follow-up work showed some of its main claims are incorrect, and a substantial revision is coming.

So do not quote this paper as “subliminal learning has been debunked”. And do not read the notice as proof of the opposite either. What we know is what the core lecture showed: specific tested setups, with limits.

Bibliographic context and status

  • Paper. Nief, Fu, Muchane & Holtzman, “Subliminal Learning is a LoRA Artifact”, arXiv 2606.00831 (preprint; v1 30 May 2026, v2 8 Sep 2026). Not peer reviewed.
  • Headline claim (abstract, paraphrased). Transfer appears with LoRA, a low-cost fine-tuning method that trains a small add-on rather than the whole model; its strength depends on the LoRA settings, it disappears under full fine-tuning, and it depends on the fine-tuning and evaluation context. The authors call it “a fragile artifact of LoRA hyperparameters and finetuning context”.
  • Status notice (arXiv Comments field, checked 2026-11-27). “Follow-up work has shown that some of the main claims made in this paper are incorrect. The authors are preparing a substantial revision”.
  • What this does not settle. Which claims are wrong is not stated in the notice; the notice is not itself a new result. It neither confirms nor rules out any particular mechanism, and it does not show transfer in untested settings.
