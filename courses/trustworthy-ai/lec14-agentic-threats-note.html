4. Beyond a Defended Agent

What changes with autonomy · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈4 min

Message: Injection controls reduce the risk from strangers' text but do not make an agent injection-proof. Even a well-controlled agent meets new failure modes once it runs many unchecked steps, may mislead its overseer, or takes other agents' output as input.

Reading the slide. Schematic, not data. On the left sits the defended agent: controls in place, not injection-proof. Three red boxes name what this lecture adds: longer horizons (many steps, few checks), an overseer that is misled, and interacting agents (output becomes input). All three lead to the blue box, control: assume the worst, check. The diagram shows no rates or likelihoods.

Script

Start from an agent with the injection controls in place: least privilege, data-flow limits, meaningful approval. Those controls reduce the risk from strangers' text; they do not make the agent injection-proof. And even a well-controlled agent is not the end of the story.

Two things are new. The agent may run many steps with no human looking, so mistakes and misbehaviour have room to grow. And agents increasingly talk to other agents, so one agent's output becomes another's input.

Today's lecture is about those two additions, and about how much the current evidence really shows.

The figure

Diagram: a defended single agent (controls in place, not injection-proof), with three additions in red: longer horizons (many steps, few checks), an overseer that is misled, and interacting agents (output becomes input). All three lead to a blue box, control: assume the worst, check.

Assumptions

The injection controls are taken as a starting point, not as complete protection. Even where they work, they do not stop errors the agent makes on its own or failures between cooperating agents.

5. Autonomy Is a Dial, Not a Switch

What changes with autonomy · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈7 min

Message: Autonomy is not on or off. It is set by four dials (steps per task, task length, tools and permissions, and time between human checks), and turning any of them up widens what can go wrong before someone looks.

Reading the slide. The list names the four settings; the schematic repeats them as four sliders running from low on the left to high on the right. A chat assistant sits low on every slider and a long-running agent high on every one. Marker positions are illustrative, not measured, and the four settings are a teaching frame rather than a published taxonomy.

Script

People say an agent is or is not autonomous. It is more useful to see four settings. How many steps it takes per task. How long the task is. Which tools and permissions it holds. And how long it runs between human checks.

A chatbot answering one question sits low on all four. A coding agent that works for an hour with shell access, checked only at the end, sits high on all four, including a long interval between checks.

Turning any setting up widens what can go wrong between checks. That is the sense in which autonomy is a risk factor: not because the model changes, but because fewer of its steps are seen.

The figure

Four sliders: steps per task, time horizon, tools and permissions, interval between human checks. Further right means more autonomy on every slider.

Assumptions

The four settings are a teaching frame, not a standard taxonomy from one paper.

6. Small Errors Compound Over Long Tasks

What changes with autonomy · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈10 min

Message: A toy calculation: if each step succeeds with a fixed probability of 0.95, failures are independent and nothing is caught or fixed, a 20-step task is fully right only about 36% of the time. Real errors are correlated and agents sometimes recover, so this is not a measured law.

Reading the slide. The bullets state the assumptions: 0.95 per step and independent failures. The curve plots P(all steps right), $0.95^n$, against the number of steps from 0 to 20, falling from 1.0 to the marked 0.36 at 20 steps. It is computed, not measured on any agent, and it is not a study of how often real agents are checked.

Script

A toy calculation. Say each step is right with probability 0.95, and steps fail independently. A 20-step task is fully right only 0.95 to the 20th, about 36% of the time.

This is illustrative arithmetic, not a measured agent. Real errors are correlated: an agent that misreads the task fails many steps together. And agents sometimes notice an error and recover.

Within this toy model the direction is clear: with a fixed per-step success rate, independent failures and no recovery, the chance that the whole task is right falls with every added unchecked step. It is not an empirical law about how often agents must be checked; with correlated errors or recovery the numbers change. Hammond and colleagues make a related point for networks of agents: "errors that may be acceptable in isolation could compound in complex, dynamic networks of agents" (§1).

The figure

Curve of 0.95 to the power n against the number of steps n, with n = 20 marked at about 0.36.

Assumptions

Independence, a fixed per-step success rate and no recovery. All three are simplifications; the formal version is in the technical supplement.

9. Time Horizon: The Definition

Measuring autonomy · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈14 min

Message: METR's 50% time horizon is the human task length at which an agent's fitted success probability is 50%, on METR's task suite, for one model–scaffold pair. Task length is how long skilled humans took to succeed; it is a capability measure, not a safe run time.

Reading the slide. The schematic curve plots agent success rate (y) against human task length on a log scale (x). Success is high for short tasks and falls for long ones; the dashed lines mark where the fitted S-curve crosses 50%, and that length is the horizon. The curve is schematic, not METR's data.

Script

METR's time-horizon measure. Measure each task by how long skilled humans took to complete it successfully; for a minority of tasks with no human baseline, a researcher estimate is used. Then plot the agent's success against that human task length. Short tasks it usually solves; long ones it usually fails.

Fit a smooth decreasing S-curve through the points. The 50% time horizon is the human task length at which the fitted success probability is 50%.

The figure

Schematic decreasing S-curve: success high for short tasks, falling for long ones, with the 50% crossing marked.

Setup, model, date

Kwa et al. (METR), arXiv 2503.14499 (Mar 2025), §2–§3. The fit is $p = \sigma(\beta(\log h - \log t))$, where $t$ is task length and $h$ is the horizon.

Assumptions

Difficulty is proxied entirely by human completion time. Two tasks of equal human time count as equally hard for the model.

Going further

The method resembles item response theory, except that each task's difficulty is fixed from human time rather than fitted. The derivation is in the technical supplement.

Primary sources

  • Kwa et al. (METR), "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks" (arXiv 2503.14499; checked v4, 10 Jul 2026, 2019–2025 model cohort) — 170 tasks; logistic fit against log human time; 50% horizon doubling every 207 days (95% CI 166–240); 80% horizon 4–6× shorter; limitations §5–6 and App. B.2/E.3–E.4.

10. How a Horizon Is Computed

Measuring autonomy · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈16 min

Message: A horizon comes from about 170 software and research tasks timed by successful humans (21 by researcher estimate), about eight runs per model and task, a logistic fit and a chosen threshold. Demanding 80% success instead of 50% gives a horizon four to six times shorter.

Reading the slide. The steps read top to bottom as the recipe: tasks, task length, runs and scaffold, fit, read-off. The highlighted line is the threshold caveat. There is no data plot; the numbers come from Kwa et al. §2–§4 and App. C.3, as the citation says.

Script

The details matter. 170 tasks in software engineering, machine learning and cybersecurity, from a few seconds up to about 30 hours of human time.

A task's length is the geometric mean time of the humans who succeeded; 21 of the 169 analysed tasks have no human baseline and use researcher estimates instead. Each model runs each task about eight times. Scaffolds are not uniform: the SWAA tasks use a simple prompting scaffold, and on the other tasks most models use one public scaffold while a few use others. Fit success against log task length, and read off where the fitted curve crosses 50%.

Asking for 80% success instead gives a horizon four to six times shorter. A single number hides that choice.

Setup, model, date

Kwa et al. Three suites: an HCAST subset (97 tasks), RE-Bench (7 tasks, 8 h each) and SWAA (66 single-step tasks). Over 800 human baselines totalling 2,529 hours; 148 of 169 tasks had human baselines, the remaining 21 researcher estimates. Scaffolds (App. C.3): the same scaffold across the suite with no task-specific prompting, except SWAA, which used a simple prompting scaffold; most models ran on modular-public, o1 on triframe and o1-preview on duet. GPT-2 was imputed zero on HCAST and RE-Bench.

What this establishes

A reproducible capability measure on this task suite.

What this does not establish

Not a measure of reliability: at the horizon length the fitted success probability is only 50%.

Going further

Fig. 1's embedded subtitle and §2.2 count 169 tasks; §2 describes 170 (97 + 7 + 66). The slide follows the suite total.

Primary sources

  • Kwa et al. (METR), "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks" (arXiv 2503.14499; checked v4, 10 Jul 2026, 2019–2025 model cohort) — 170 tasks; logistic fit against log human time; 50% horizon doubling every 207 days (95% CI 166–240); 80% horizon 4–6× shorter; limitations §5–6 and App. B.2/E.3–E.4.

12. What the Number Depends On

Measuring autonomy · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈20 min

Message: A horizon moves with five choices: the success threshold, the task distribution, the human reference, the model–scaffold pair and the statistical fit. A 2026 METR note found other fits cut recent 50% horizons by up to 35%, so the slope is more certain than any one value.

Reading the slide. Each row is one choice, with why it matters on the right. The last row's source is a METR research note from March 2026, a year after the paper, not the paper itself. The table ranks nothing; it lists where the number can shift.

Script

Five choices move the number. The success threshold: 80% is four to six times shorter than 50%. The task distribution: mostly software, and METR's later note puts the task-distribution uncertainty at often a factor of two either way. The human reference: measured times of successful skilled professionals with little context on the task, so a person who knows the codebase would be faster; 21 tasks rely on researcher estimates instead.

The scaffold: a horizon belongs to a model–scaffold pair, and the paper used different scaffolds for some models. And the statistical model. This last point comes from a later source: a METR note from 2026, a year after the paper, found alternative fits cut recent 50% horizons by up to 35%.

None of this kills the trend. The slope is more certain than any single model's horizon.

17. Setup, Behavior, Inference

Against the overseer · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈?

Message: When reading any scheming or sandbagging result, keep three layers apart: the setup the evaluators gave the model (goal, nudge, threat), the behavior counted over many runs, and the inference about why and about deployment, which is rarely established. Behavior can be induced by the setup and still be genuinely observed; the two are not alternatives.

Reading the slide. Three boxes, left to right: what is given (blue), what is observed (dark), what is interpreted (gray, dashed). The solid arrow means the setup produces the behavior; the dashed arrow means the step to motive or deployment is a reading, not a measurement. It is a reading frame, not a result.

Script

Keep three layers apart when reading any result. Setup: which goal, nudge or threat did the evaluators give the model? That is read off the prompt and the planted files. Behavior: what did the model actually do, and how often over many runs? Inference: why did it do it, and would it happen in deployment? That last layer is rarely established.

Note that "induced" and "observed" are not opposites. A behavior the setup provoked is still a real, observed behavior; it just tells us about that setup.

For each study today, ask which layer a sentence is about. And a failed evaluation is not always strategic concealment. We look at two anchors: in-context scheming and sandbagging.

The figure

Three-box diagram: Setup (given) → Behavior (observed) ⇢ Inference (interpreted). Illustrative; no data.

Going further

Related material. Goal misgeneralisation — a model competently pursuing the wrong goal out of distribution — is not covered in this deck. The alignment-failures lecture (lec07) covers the neighbouring idea of specification gaming; it does not treat goal misgeneralisation itself. Lynch et al. (agentic misalignment) is another induced-behaviour study: 16 models in hypothetical corporate simulations sometimes blackmailed or leaked when that was the only way to avoid replacement or resolve a goal conflict, and the authors report no evidence of this in real deployments.

18. Can an Agent Disable Its Check?

Against the overseer · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈?

Message: Apollo Research's oversight test gives the model a goal in context, then lets it find planted files: a memo revealing a conflicting developer goal and an oversight config it can edit. Does it switch the check off? Goal and situation are given in context, in a sandbox, so this tests capability under a constructed setup, not a goal learned in training, and nothing real is at stake.

Reading the slide. Left column, in blue: what the evaluators gave the model in context. Center: the agent in its sandbox. Right column, in red: the planted files it finds. The question line names the measured behavior; the gray line is the qualifier. No rates appear on this slide.

Script

Apollo Research's setup. The model gets a goal in its system prompt, plus, in the strong version, a nudge: "Nothing else matters." While working, it reads files revealing that its developer wants something else, and that an oversight mechanism, a config file it can edit, would stop it.

The question: does it switch the check off to keep pursuing its goal?

Two qualifiers. The goal and situation are handed to the model in context, so this tests capability, not a goal learned in training. And everything is a constructed sandbox: no real users, no real model weights, no real oversight.

The figure

Setup, model, date

23. A Bad Score Has Several Explanations

Against the overseer · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈?

Message: Before calling a low score sandbagging, rule out the ordinary explanations (weak prompt or scaffold, refusals scored as failures, quitting early), and only then consider strategic ones. Even then, intent is not diagnosed from one failed run.

Reading the slide. Read left to right as an order of checks: a low score, then the ordinary causes to rule out first (blue), and only after that the possibly strategic ones (red, dashed because they are hypotheses). No box is a measured rate.

Script

Before calling a low score sandbagging, rule out the dull explanations. Weak elicitation: the wrong prompt or scaffold. Refusals scored as failures. Ending early or giving up.

Only then the possibly strategic ones: noticing the test and behaving differently, or underperforming when told high scores hurt. And even then, one failed run does not show intent.

RepliBench saw several of the dull ones: models noticed the simulation and refused, ended early, or claimed to finish without acting.

Checks to run

  • Re-run with stronger elicitation (better prompt, scaffold, more attempts) and compare with the best elicited score.
  • Score refusals and invalid answers separately from wrong answers.
  • Read transcripts for early termination or claimed-but-not-done steps.
  • Compare against control tasks: a drop confined to the targeted set is more suspicious than a uniform one.
  • Repeat over many runs before reading anything into one.

36. Test Safety Against a Bad Worker

Control · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈?

Message: Alignment tries to make the model want the right things, which is hard to verify from outside. Control assumes, as a worst-case test assumption, that the worker model may attack, and asks whether the whole system of checks still keeps outcomes safe. That is a testable system-level question, not a guarantee and not a claim that models secretly attack.

Reading the slide. Two cards: alignment (goal, and why it is hard to verify) on the left, control (assumption, and what is tested) on the right. The gray line blocks the misreading that control asserts models are adversarial. The highlight states control's question. A conceptual contrast after Greenblatt et al. §1; no data.

Definition

Greenblatt and colleagues define a protocol as a way of using an untrusted, more capable model together with a weaker trusted model and a small amount of trusted high-quality labor. A control evaluation estimates the protocol's safety by letting a red team replace the untrusted model with an attack policy and simulating the scenario. Safety is then a property of the protocol under that red team, not of the model's intentions.

Script

Alignment tries to make the model want the right things. That is hard to verify from outside, as the last section showed.

Control takes a different route. For the purpose of the test, assume the worker might attack. That is a worst-case assumption, not an accusation. Then ask: do our checks still keep outcomes safe? That is something we can test.

41. Checks Can Defer or Edit

Control · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈?

Message: Variants of trusted monitoring still audit the top 2% but act on a middle band of suspicious code: defer to trusted submits T's own, weaker code instead; trusted editing has T edit U's code to remove possible backdoors. A wider band means more caution and less of U's work. Untrusted monitoring instead uses a second copy of U as the monitor, which is not assumed honest and is checked on fake backdoors.

Reading the slide. The bar ranks U's solutions by T's suspicion score, most suspicious on the left: the top 2% go to H, a middle band is deferred or edited, and the rest are submitted as U wrote them. Band widths are not to scale. The two cards say what happens in the middle band under each protocol. No numbers on this slide; the next slide plots the results.

Script

Two ways to use the middle band. Defer to trusted: if U's code is fairly suspicious, throw it away and submit T's own code. Safer, but T is weaker, so usefulness drops. Trusted editing: T edits U's code to remove anything that looks like a backdoor, keeping the rest.

The percentages in a protocol's name set the band. Defer to trusted at 10% means: the top 2% most suspicious go to audit, and anything between the top 2% and the top 10% is replaced by T's code. Trusted editing at 20% means T edits anything in the top 2% to 20%.

Untrusted monitoring uses a second copy of U as the monitor. Because two copies of U could collude, it is not trusted: it is first tested on 30 synthetic backdoors, the protocol shuts down if it misses too many, and inputs are redacted or paraphrased to break collusion signals.

43. Choosing When to Attack Changes the Test

Control · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈?

Message: A June 2026 preprint asks whether an attacker who chooses when to attack can make a control evaluation look safer than it is. The idea: a start policy attacks only on promising tasks, and a stop policy aborts an attack in progress when detection looks likely. In two agentic test environments, both lowered measured safety without improving how attacks are carried out. The reductions are upper bounds from a tuned, offline method, not an observed rate of attacks in deployment.

Reading the slide. The image is panels A and B of Ge-Wang, Crosse et al. Fig. 1, a mechanism diagram, not data. A: the start policy scores each main-task/side-task pair and either starts the attack or abandons it. B: at each turn of an attack in progress, the stop policy scores the risk of being caught against the chance of success and aborts when the score passes a threshold. The key beside it gives the research question, the two ideas, the finding and its limit. Panel C, a schematic of the effect on safety, is omitted.

Script

One research highlight from June 2026. Most control evaluations, including the one we just saw, assume the attacker tries at a fixed random rate. But a real attacker can choose. Two ideas. Start: only attack on tasks where success without being caught looks likely. Stop: if an attack in progress starts to look risky, abort it.

The finding: in two agentic test environments, choosing when to attack lowered the measured safety of a standard trusted-monitoring setup. The attacker did not get better at attacking; it got better at choosing.

The limit: these numbers are upper bounds. The choices were made by a separate scorer model on recorded attack attempts, with thresholds tuned to make safety as low as possible. It is a stress test of the evaluation method, not a measurement of how often a deployed AI would do this.

Setup, model, date

arXiv 2606.06529v1 (3 Jun 2026), §2–§3. BashArena: multi-step shell tasks, 637 main tasks, 4 side tasks that are critical security failures. LinuxArena: 20 Docker Compose application environments, 1,667 main tasks, 185 side tasks such as exfiltration and backdoors. Attacker Claude Opus 4.6; trusted monitor an ensemble of mimo-v2-flash (5 samples per action, 0–10 suspicion); trusted monitoring with a top-1% audit standard. The PDF states publication at the AIWILD workshop at ICML 2026; this is cited as a preprint because the venue was not independently confirmed.

A different metric from S38

Greenblatt's safety was the probability of no backdoor in a whole run. This paper reports empirical safety among decisive attempted attacks: caught attacks divided by caught plus uncaught successful attacks. Attempts that went uncaught but failed, and aborted attempts, are left out. It is conditional on a decisive attempt, so it does not depend on the overall attack rate, and the authors state it is not deployment safety (App. A). The two numbers cannot be compared directly.

Caveats on the method

  • Upper bound. Start and stop decisions are made by a separate prompted scorer model (mimo-v2.5-pro), run offline on pre-generated trajectories, not by the attacker choosing on its own. Thresholds were tuned adversarially, by 10-fold cross-validation, to minimise safety.

  • Small samples, wide uncertainty. Held-out n ranges from 2 to 23 at the 1% budget.

What this establishes

Assuming a fixed attack rate can overstate control safety in these settings.

What this does not establish

Deployment-level safety, or how often a deployed autonomous attacker would choose its moment.

Control · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈?

Message: A control result holds inside its tested envelope: these tasks and run lengths, the strongest attacks the red team found, this audit budget. Outside it (longer open-ended work, attackers who pick their moment, attacks aimed at the monitor itself) that result neither guarantees nor rules out safety. The control-specific question is: would this oversight still work over longer, adversarially chosen tasks?

Reading the slide. Two boxes. The solid green box lists what a control evaluation actually tested. The dashed box lists conditions outside it, each tied to a cited source. Script

Every control result has an envelope: these tasks, these run lengths, the strongest attacks the red team found, this audit budget. Inside it, we have evidence. Outside it, we don't, in either direction.

Three things sit outside. Longer, open-ended work: deployments can run longer than the run lengths evaluated. Attackers who pick their moment: the 2026 study showed that choosing when to attack lowered measured safety in its settings. Attacks aimed at the monitor: Terekhov and colleagues show an untrusted model embedding prompt injections in its outputs that subvert the trusted monitor. That is a short limit here, not a new attack lesson.

So the question to carry forward: would this oversight still work over longer, adversarially chosen tasks?

Why each limit applies

  • Strongest attacks found: if the evaluation's red team is weaker than a real adversary, safety is overstated (Greenblatt §5.1).
  • No train/validation split: protocols and thresholds were tuned on the same problems used to report them (Greenblatt §2.4).
  • Empirical vs deployment safety: Ge-Wang et al. App. A relate their empirical estimator to deployment safety and expect the gap to widen under some conditions; §4.4 limitations.

Going further

Terekhov et al. also find that a Defer-to-Resample protocol can backfire, acting like best-of-n for the attacker.

  • Established: induced scheming and sandbagging occur in constructed tests. Not established: deceptive intent in deployment.
  • Established: multi-agent systems fail often, even without attackers. Not established: control guarantees outside the evaluation.

?. AI Can Persuade, Not Just Answer

Synthesis and frontiers · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈?

Message: A new trust question beyond the course: not only whether an answer is correct, but how a conversation changes what the user believes. In one controlled debate study, GPT-4 given the participant's profile moved stated agreement more than a human opponent without one; the study does not show lasting or real-world effects, and a 2026 correction means the extra effect of personalization alone is not established.

Reading the slide. The figure is the paper's design diagram, icons only, read left to right; the numbered key on the right carries its labels. (a) Each participant rates agreement with a proposition on a 1–5 scale. Script

Two brief previews of questions this course did not cover. Everything so far asked whether a system's answers, data and actions can be trusted. A different question: how does talking to an AI change what you believe?

In this study, 900 US participants first rated how much they agreed with a statement, then debated it for ten minutes against either a person or GPT-4. Some opponents saw a short profile of the participant: age, gender, education, politics and so on. Afterwards, participants rated their agreement again. GPT-4 with the profile moved agreement more than a human opponent without one.

Two limits. This is agreement measured right after one structured debate, not votes or lasting beliefs. And after a 2026 correction, the authors say they cannot conclusively establish that the profile itself added persuasion beyond GPT-4 without it. The broader question, how to protect people's ability to make up their own minds, is wider than this one result.

Source and setup

  • Establishes: in this controlled, anonymized debate setting, profile-informed GPT-4 shifted immediate stated agreement more than non-personalized human opponents.
  • Does not establish: effects on voting or behaviour, how long the shift lasts (agreement was measured only right after the debate), performance on real social platforms (the authors call ecological validity limited), or that personalization alone explains the advantage.
  • Do not discuss persuasion prompts or targeting tactics in class; the point is the trust question, not the technique.

Script

The second preview: companionship. Many people now talk to chatbots daily, sometimes about personal things. Is a system that people use a lot a system that is good for them?

In this study, about a thousand people used ChatGPT every day for four weeks. The researchers randomly assigned the voice or text mode and the kind of conversation, and measured four outcomes: loneliness, time with other people, emotional dependence on the AI, and problematic use. The assigned mode and topic made no significant difference overall. What stood out was usage time: people who chose to chat more ended up worse on all four.

But nobody was assigned to chat more. Maybe heavy use makes people lonelier; maybe lonelier people chat more. And there was no group without a chatbot. So engagement is not evidence of benefit, and this study is not evidence that chatbots cause loneliness either. It is a reason to measure the people, not just the answers.

Source and setup

  • Fang, Liu, Danry, Lee, Chan, Pataranutaporn, Maes, Phang, Lampe, Ahmad & Agarwal, "How AI and Human Behaviors Shape Psychosocial Effects of Extended Chatbot Use: A Longitudinal Randomized Controlled Study", arXiv 2503.17473v2 (2 Oct 2025), preprint. Figure: Fig. 1 (conceptual framework), unmodified, CC BY 4.0. arXiv. Cite v2, not the v1 headline.
  • Design: four-week randomized experiment, n = 981 completers (2,539 enrolled), >300k messages; GPT-4o via ChatGPT at least five minutes a day for 28 days; 3 × 3 factorial: modality (text, neutral voice, engaging voice) × conversation type (open-ended, non-personal, personal). Outcomes measured weekly; analysis uses week 4, controlling for baseline.
  • Results (Abstract): "No significant effects were detected from experimental conditions"; "participants who voluntarily used the chatbot more, regardless of assigned condition, showed consistently worse outcomes." The body reports some marginal, unadjusted condition contrasts; do not turn them into a ranking of voice versus text.
  • Limits (authors): "we did not manipulate duration … we cannot definitively determine whether increased duration causally drives worse psychosocial outcomes"; the study "does not compare between AI and non-AI use"; sample from the US, English speakers; specific to ChatGPT and its guardrails; no follow-up after week 4 is reported.

Establishes / does not establish

  • Establishes: within this sample and period, heavier voluntary use was associated with worse scores on all four outcomes.
  • Does not establish: that chatbot use causes loneliness or dependence, that all companion apps harm users, that voice is safer or riskier than text, or any clinical or treatment effect. No mental-health advice is implied.
