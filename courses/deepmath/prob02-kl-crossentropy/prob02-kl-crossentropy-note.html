1  Why Cross-Entropy?

The deck opens with the one line of code every classification project contains: nn.CrossEntropyLoss(). Writing $f(x) = \bigl(f(x)_1, \dots, f(x)_M\bigr)$ for the model's predicted probability vector over $M$ labels and $y$ for the true label, the loss on one sample is

\[ \ell\bigl(f(x), y\bigr) = \log \frac{1}{f(x)_y} = -\log f(x)_y. \]

The lecture's question is why this particular decreasing function of $f(x)_y$. Any decreasing function — $1 - f(x)_y$, $(1 - f(x)_y)^2$, … — rewards putting belief on the true label; nothing about "penalize wrong answers" forces a logarithm or a reciprocal. The answer developed below: $\log 1/Q$ is surprisal priced by a model pmf $Q$, its expectation under the truth is the cross-entropy $H(P, Q)$, the excess of that over the entropy $H(P)$ is exactly the KL divergence $D(P \Vert Q)$, and minimizing cross-entropy is therefore minimizing the KL divergence from the model to the truth. The same quantity also has an operational meaning with money attached (Section 5): $D(P \Vert Q)$ is the exponential rate at which a gambler who believes $Q$ loses wealth to a gambler who knows $P$.

A units remark the deck omits. These notes, like the deck, use $\log_2$; PyTorch's CrossEntropyLoss uses $\ln$ (and takes raw logits, applying log-softmax internally). Since $\log_2 t = \ln t / \ln 2$, the two losses differ by the constant factor $1/\ln 2 \approx 1.4427$ — the same minimizer, the same gradients up to scale. Every theorem below is base-independent.

2  Recall & Teaser

2.1 What is imported from Lecture 1

Setting: $X$ is a discrete random variable with true pmf $p_X$ on a finite alphabet $\mathcal{X}$, $|\mathcal{X}| = M$. Three facts from the prob01 note are used as lemmas (see its Sections 3.3, 4.3, 5.3 for the proofs):

  • Surprisal and entropy. $S(x) = \log \tfrac{1}{p_X(x)}$, and $H(X) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\log \tfrac{1}{p_X(X)}\bigr] = \sum_x p_X(x) \log \tfrac{1}{p_X(x)}$, with the continuity convention $0 \log \tfrac10 = 0$ (equivalently, sums run over the support). Range: $0 \leq H(X) \leq \log M$.
  • LOTUS. $\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \sum_x f(x)\, p_X(x)$ for any function $f$ — used silently every time an expectation is expanded as a sum below.
  • Jensen's inequality. If $f$ is concave and $Z$ takes finitely many values in its domain, then $\mathbb{E}[f(Z)] \leq f(\mathbb{E}[Z])$; if $f$ is strictly concave, equality holds if and only if $Z$ is constant (all mass on one point). The key instance is $f = \log$, strictly concave on $(0,\infty)$ because $(\log t)'' = -\tfrac{1}{t^2 \ln 2} \lt 0$.

The proof pattern to remember from Lecture 1 (max-entropy): trap an expectation under a $\log$, use Jensen to move the $\log$ outside, and let the pmf weight $p_X(x)$ cancel whatever ratio sits inside. Both moves recur verbatim in Theorem 1 below.

2.2 The teaser

Surprisal prices outcome $x$ by the true pmf. A learned model does not have $p_X$; it has a guess $Q$. Outcomes still arrive with frequencies $p_X$, but the model pays the bill $\log \tfrac{1}{Q(x)}$. The question: how does the average mismatched bill

\[ \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\log \frac{1}{Q(X)}\Bigr] = \sum_x p_X(x) \log \frac{1}{Q(x)} \]

compare to the honest bill $H(X)$? A priori it could seem to go either way — $Q$ might under-price some outcomes ($Q(x) \gt p_X(x)$ makes that term cheaper) and over-price others. The mismatch theorem says the savings can never beat the losses: the true pmf is the unique cheapest pricing.

3  The Mismatch Theorem

3.1 Statement

Theorem 1 (mismatch). Let $X$ have pmf $p_X$ on a finite alphabet $\mathcal{X}$, and let $Q$ be any pmf on $\mathcal{X}$ (i.e. $Q(x) \geq 0$, $\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} Q(x) = 1$). Then

\[ H(X) \;\leq\; \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\log \frac{1}{Q(X)}\Bigr] = \sum_{x} p_X(x) \log \frac{1}{Q(x)}, \]

with equality if and only if $Q = p_X$. Conventions: terms with $p_X(x) = 0$ contribute $0$ (the $0 \log$ convention); if $Q(x) = 0$ for some $x$ with $p_X(x) \gt 0$, the right-hand side is $+\infty$ and the inequality holds trivially.

Both conventions are continuity statements: $t \log \tfrac1t \rightarrow 0$ as $t \rightarrow 0^+$ (proved by l'Hôpital in the prob01 note, Section 4.3), while $p \log \tfrac{1}{q} \rightarrow +\infty$ as $q \rightarrow 0^+$ for fixed $p \gt 0$. The second one is not a technicality: it says a model that declares an outcome impossible pays an infinite bill the first time that outcome occurs. This is why classifiers end in a softmax — it keeps every $Q(x)$ strictly positive.

3.2 Numeric example, verified

Truth $P = (0.01, 0.99)$, model $Q = (0.1, 0.9)$. The mismatched bill:

\[ \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\log \tfrac{1}{Q(X)}\Bigr] = 0.01 \log \tfrac{1}{0.1} + 0.99 \log \tfrac{1}{0.9} \] \[ = 0.01 \times 3.3219 + 0.99 \times 0.1520 \approx 0.0332 + 0.1505 \approx 0.1837 \text{ bits}. \]

The honest bill (computed in the prob01 note, Section 4.4):

\[ H(X) = 0.01 \log \tfrac{1}{0.01} + 0.99 \log \tfrac{1}{0.99} \approx 0.0664 + 0.0144 \approx 0.0808 \text{ bits}. \]

Overpayment: $0.1837 - 0.0808 \approx 0.103$ bits per draw — the model pays more than double the optimum. (The deck's $0.184$, $0.081$, $0.103$ check out.) Section 4 will identify the gap as $D(P \Vert Q)$.

3.3 Proof (the deck's three steps, expanded)

Assume for now that $p_X(x) \gt 0$ for all $x \in \mathcal{X}$ and $Q(x) \gt 0$ wherever needed; the general case is Section 3.4. The target is $H(X) - \mathbb{E}\bigl[\log \tfrac{1}{Q(X)}\bigr] \leq 0$.

Step 1 (merge the gap). Both terms are expectations of functions of $X$ under the same pmf $p_X$, so linearity of expectation combines them, and $\log a - \log b = \log \tfrac{a}{b}$ merges the logs:

\[ H(X) - \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\log \tfrac{1}{Q(X)}\Bigr] = \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\log \tfrac{1}{p_X(X)} - \log \tfrac{1}{Q(X)}\Bigr] = \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\log \tfrac{Q(X)}{p_X(X)}\Bigr]. \]

Step 2 (Jensen). Apply Jensen's inequality with the strictly concave $f = \log$ to the positive random variable $Z = \tfrac{Q(X)}{p_X(X)}$:

\[ \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\log \tfrac{Q(X)}{p_X(X)}\Bigr] \;\leq\; \log\, \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tfrac{Q(X)}{p_X(X)}\Bigr]. \]

Step 3 (cancellation). Expand the inner expectation by LOTUS; the weight $p_X(x)$ cancels the denominator, and $Q$ is a pmf:

\[ \log\, \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tfrac{Q(X)}{p_X(X)}\Bigr] = \log \sum_x p_X(x)\, \frac{Q(x)}{p_X(x)} = \log \sum_x Q(x) = \log 1 = 0. \]

Chaining the three displays: $H(X) - \mathbb{E}\bigl[\log \tfrac{1}{Q(X)}\bigr] \leq 0$. $\blacksquare$

Equality. The only inequality used was Jensen with strictly concave $\log$, which is tight if and only if $Z = \tfrac{Q(X)}{p_X(X)}$ is constant: $Q(x) = c\, p_X(x)$ for all $x$. Summing over $x$ gives $1 = c \cdot 1$, so $c = 1$ and $Q = p_X$. Conversely $Q = p_X$ plainly gives equality. $\blacksquare$

3.4 The edge case, solved (the deck's exercise)

The proof above divides by $p_X(x)$, which is illegal where $p_X(x) = 0$. Here is the rigorous version, following the deck's hints.

Rigorous proof of Theorem 1. If $Q(x) = 0$ for some $x$ with $p_X(x) \gt 0$, the right-hand side is $+\infty$ and there is nothing to prove; so assume $Q(x) \gt 0$ whenever $p_X(x) \gt 0$. Let $\mathcal{S} = \{x : p_X(x) \gt 0\}$ be the support. By the $0\log$ convention, both $H(X)$ and the cross term are sums over $\mathcal{S}$ only, so

\[ H(X) - \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\log \tfrac{1}{Q(X)}\Bigr] = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{S}} p_X(x) \log \frac{Q(x)}{p_X(x)}. \]

Every ratio $\tfrac{Q(x)}{p_X(x)}$, $x \in \mathcal{S}$, is now well-defined and positive. Jensen (over the pmf $p_X$ restricted to $\mathcal{S}$, where it sums to $1$) still applies:

\[ \sum_{x \in \mathcal{S}} p_X(x) \log \frac{Q(x)}{p_X(x)} \;\overset{(\text{Jensen})}{\leq}\; \log \sum_{x \in \mathcal{S}} p_X(x)\, \frac{Q(x)}{p_X(x)} = \log \sum_{x \in \mathcal{S}} Q(x). \]

The final sum is no longer necessarily $1$: it omits any mass $Q$ puts outside the support of $p_X$. But it is at most $1$, and $\log$ is increasing, so

\[ \log \sum_{x \in \mathcal{S}} Q(x) \;\leq\; \log 1 = 0, \]

and the theorem follows. Equality now requires both inequalities tight: (i) Jensen tight $\Leftrightarrow$ $\tfrac{Q(x)}{p_X(x)} = c$ for all $x \in \mathcal{S}$; (ii) $\sum_{x \in \mathcal{S}} Q(x) = 1$ $\Leftrightarrow$ $Q(x) = 0$ for every $x \notin \mathcal{S}$. Given (ii), summing (i) over $\mathcal{S}$ gives $1 = c \cdot 1$, so $c = 1$ and $Q(x) = p_X(x)$ on $\mathcal{S}$; combined with (ii), $Q = p_X$ everywhere. $\blacksquare$

Note the asymmetry of the two directions of "putting zeros": $Q$ vanishing off the support of $p_X$ costs nothing (inequality (ii) can still be tight); $Q$ vanishing on the support costs everything (infinite bill).

3.5 Cross-entropy, defined

Definition (cross-entropy). For pmfs $P, Q$ on $\mathcal{X}$,

\[ H(P, Q) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \log \frac{1}{Q(x)} \;\in\; [0, +\infty], \]

with the conventions of Theorem 1: $0 \cdot \log \tfrac{1}{Q(x)} = 0$ even when $Q(x) = 0$, and $H(P,Q) = +\infty$ if $P(x) \gt 0 = Q(x)$ for some $x$.

It is the expected surprisal when outcomes are drawn from $P$ but priced by $Q$ — the two arguments are "crossed", hence the name. In this notation Theorem 1 reads: $H(P, Q) \geq H(P, P) = H(P)$, with equality iff $Q = P$. Note $H(P,Q)$ is not symmetric in its arguments, and $H(P, P) = H(P)$ recovers ordinary entropy (the deck writes $H(X)$ and $H(p_X)$ interchangeably, an abuse of notation flagged in the source notes).

4  KL Divergence

4.1 Definition and conventions

Definition (Kullback–Leibler divergence / relative entropy [3]). For pmfs $P$ and $Q$ on $\mathcal{X}$,

\[ D(P \,\Vert\, Q) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \;\in\; [0, +\infty], \]

with conventions $0 \log \tfrac{0}{q} = 0$ for any $q \geq 0$ (including $0 \log \tfrac00 = 0$), and $p \log \tfrac{p}{0} = +\infty$ for $p \gt 0$.

Spelled out: $D(P \Vert Q)$ is finite only if $Q(x) = 0 \Rightarrow P(x) = 0$, i.e. only if the support of $P$ is contained in the support of $Q$ (for general measures this is called absolute continuity, written $P \ll Q$). Outcomes outside the support of $P$ never contribute. $D(P \Vert Q)$ is the expected log-likelihood-ratio of truth to model, weighted by the truth:

\[ D(P \,\Vert\, Q) = \mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}\Bigl[\log \frac{P(X)}{Q(X)}\Bigr]. \]

4.2 Worked example in both directions, verified

Take $P = (0.5, 0.4, 0.1)$ and uniform $Q = (\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$. Forward direction, term by term:

\[ D(P \,\Vert\, Q) = 0.5 \log \frac{0.5}{1/3} + 0.4 \log \frac{0.4}{1/3} + 0.1 \log \frac{0.1}{1/3} \] \[ = 0.5 \log 1.5 + 0.4 \log 1.2 + 0.1 \log 0.3 \] \[ \approx 0.2925 + 0.1052 - 0.1737 \approx 0.2240 \text{ bits}. \]

Individual terms may be negative (here the third: $P$ under-weights outcome $3$ relative to $Q$, so its log-ratio is negative); only the sum is guaranteed non-negative. Reverse direction:

\[ D(Q \,\Vert\, P) = \tfrac13 \log \frac{1/3}{0.5} + \tfrac13 \log \frac{1/3}{0.4} + \tfrac13 \log \frac{1/3}{0.1} \] \[ \approx -0.1950 - 0.0877 + 0.5790 \approx 0.2963 \text{ bits}. \]

So $D(P \Vert Q) \approx 0.224 \neq 0.296 \approx D(Q \Vert P)$, confirming the deck's numbers. Uniform-$Q$ shortcut, proved in one line: if $Q$ is uniform on $M$ symbols,

\[ D(P \,\Vert\, Q) = \sum_x P(x) \log \bigl(M P(x)\bigr) = \log M - H(P), \]

and indeed $\log 3 - H(P) = 1.5850 - 1.3610 = 0.2240$ (here $H(P) = 0.5 \cdot 1 + 0.4 \log 2.5 + 0.1 \log 10 \approx 0.5 + 0.5288 + 0.3322 = 1.3610$). The max-entropy theorem $H(P) \leq \log M$ from Lecture 1 is thus the special case "$D(P \Vert \text{uniform}) \geq 0$" of today's Theorem 2.

Asymmetry can be extreme. Let $P = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ and $Q = (1, 0)$. Then

\[ D(P \,\Vert\, Q) = \tfrac12 \log \frac{1/2}{1} + \tfrac12 \log \frac{1/2}{0} = +\infty, \] \[ D(Q \,\Vert\, P) = 1 \cdot \log \frac{1}{1/2} + 0 \cdot \log \frac{0}{1/2} = 1 \text{ bit}. \]

One direction infinite, the other one bit: believing an impossible thing is possible is mildly wrong; believing a possible thing is impossible is infinitely wrong (under the first argument's draws, the "impossible" outcome eventually happens). Keeping track of which argument holds the truth is therefore not pedantry.

4.3 Divergence, not distance

$D$ fails two of the three metric axioms: it is not symmetric (just computed), and it does not satisfy the triangle inequality (it also fails to be finite everywhere). It does satisfy the separation axiom — $D(P \Vert Q) \geq 0$ with equality iff $P = Q$ (Theorem 2) — which is exactly enough for its main use: a quantity that is zero precisely at "model = truth" and positive otherwise can be minimized to drive a model toward the truth. Objects with separation but without symmetry/triangle are called divergences.

4.4 Theorem 2 — the information inequality

Theorem 2 (information inequality). For any pmfs $P, Q$ on $\mathcal{X}$: $D(P \Vert Q) \geq 0$, with equality if and only if $P = Q$.

Proof. This is Theorem 1 re-read. Take $X \sim P$ in Theorem 1 (rigorous version, Section 3.4); its two sides differ by exactly $-D(P \Vert Q)$:

\[ -D(P \,\Vert\, Q) = \sum_{x:\,P(x) \gt 0} P(x) \log \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} = H(P) - H(P, Q) \;\leq\; 0, \]

with equality iff $Q = P$ by the equality analysis already done. $\blacksquare$

Alternative self-contained proof via the log-sum inequality. The following lemma is worth knowing; it handles all zero-mass edge cases at once and recurs throughout information theory [1, Thm 2.7.1].

Lemma (log-sum inequality). For non-negative numbers $a_1, \dots, a_n$ and $b_1, \dots, b_n$, with $a = \sum_i a_i$ and $b = \sum_i b_i$,

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \log \frac{a_i}{b_i} \;\geq\; a \log \frac{a}{b}, \]

with the conventions of Section 4.1; equality iff $\tfrac{a_i}{b_i}$ is the same for all $i$ (with $b_i \gt 0$).

Proof. If some $b_i = 0$ with $a_i \gt 0$ the left side is $+\infty$; discard indices with $a_i = b_i = 0$; so assume all $b_i \gt 0$. The function $f(t) = t \log t$ is strictly convex on $[0, \infty)$ (for $t \gt 0$, $f''(t) = \tfrac{1}{t \ln 2} \gt 0$). Apply Jensen for convex functions with weights $\tfrac{b_i}{b}$ to the points $t_i = \tfrac{a_i}{b_i}$:

\[ \sum_i \frac{b_i}{b}\, f\Bigl(\frac{a_i}{b_i}\Bigr) \;\geq\; f\Bigl(\sum_i \frac{b_i}{b} \cdot \frac{a_i}{b_i}\Bigr) = f\Bigl(\frac{a}{b}\Bigr). \]

Multiplying both sides by $b$ gives the claim, and strict convexity makes Jensen tight iff all $t_i$ coincide. $\blacksquare$

Now take $a_i = P(x_i)$, $b_i = Q(x_i)$ over all of $\mathcal{X}$: $a = b = 1$, so $D(P \Vert Q) \geq 1 \cdot \log \tfrac11 = 0$, with equality iff $\tfrac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ is constant, which (both summing to $1$) forces $P = Q$.

4.5 The gap, named: CE = entropy + KL

Splitting the log-ratio inside the cross-entropy sum (each term finite or $+\infty$ consistently, by the conventions):

\[ H(P, Q) = \sum_x P(x) \log \frac{1}{Q(x)} = \sum_x P(x) \log \Bigl(\frac{1}{P(x)} \cdot \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\Bigr) \] \[ = \underbrace{\sum_x P(x) \log \frac{1}{P(x)}}_{H(P)} + \underbrace{\sum_x P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}}_{D(P \Vert Q)}. \]

So $H(P, Q) = H(P) + D(P \Vert Q)$: the mismatch overpayment is the KL divergence. This identity is stated again as Theorem 6 in Section 6 (the deck proves it there in the direction $D = H(P,Q) - H(P)$; it is the same computation). In the Section 3.2 example, $D(P \Vert Q) = 0.1837 - 0.0808 \approx 0.103$ bits.

5  Gambling & Doubling Rate

5.1 Setup — red or black

A binary game: RED wins with probability $p$, BLACK with probability $1-p$; the deck takes $p \leq \tfrac12$ (BLACK is the favorite — this is only used to fix which corner maximizes $\mathbb{E}[S]$ below, not for any theorem). Odds are $2$-for-$1$ on both colors: the money bet on the winning color is doubled, the rest is lost. With budget $B$, bet $qB$ on red and $(1-q)B$ on black, $q \in [0,1]$. The wealth after one round is the random variable

\[ S = \begin{cases} 2qB & \text{if red wins (prob. } p\text{)}, \\ 2(1-q)B & \text{if black wins (prob. } 1-p\text{)}. \end{cases} \]

5.2 First try: maximize expected wealth — and why it ruins you

\[ \mathbb{E}[S] = p \cdot 2qB + (1-p) \cdot 2(1-q)B = 2B\bigl[(1-p) + q\,(2p - 1)\bigr]. \]

This is linear in $q$ with slope $2B(2p-1)$, which is negative for $p \lt \tfrac12$. A linear function on $[0,1]$ is maximized at an endpoint: $q = 0$, i.e. all-in on black, with $\mathbb{E}[S] = 2(1-p)B \gt B$.

Now repeat the all-in strategy over $n$ independent rounds, reinvesting everything each time. You are still solvent after $n$ rounds only if black won every round:

\[ \Pr(\text{alive after } n \text{ bets}) = (1-p)^n \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad (n \rightarrow \infty, \; p \gt 0), \] \[ S_n = \begin{cases} 2^n B & \text{w.p. } (1-p)^n, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

So ruin is certain in the limit, even though $\mathbb{E}[S_n] = \bigl(2(1-p)\bigr)^n B$ grows exponentially. There is no contradiction: the expectation is carried entirely by the single all-wins path, whose probability vanishes. Expected wealth is the wrong objective for repeated betting because it is blind to ruin.

5.3 The fix: maximize expected log-wealth

Two reasons to score wealth on the log scale:

  • $\log 0 = -\infty$: any strategy with positive ruin probability gets objective $-\infty$, so maximizing $\mathbb{E}[\log S]$ automatically excludes ruin.
  • Wealth multiplies across rounds, so log-wealth adds: $\log S_n = \log B + \sum_{k=1}^n \log(\text{growth factor of round } k)$. Averages of sums are what the law of large numbers controls, which is what makes "growth rate" a meaningful per-round quantity (Section 5.5).

Compute the objective, splitting the logs of products:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\log S] = p \log (2qB) + (1-p) \log \bigl(2(1-q)B\bigr) \] \[ = p\bigl[\log 2B + \log q\bigr] + (1-p)\bigl[\log 2B + \log (1-q)\bigr] \] \[ = \log 2B \;-\; \Bigl(p \log \frac{1}{q} + (1-p) \log \frac{1}{1-q}\Bigr). \]

The bracket is exactly the cross-entropy $H\bigl((p, 1-p),\, (q, 1-q)\bigr)$ of the betting fractions against the true win probabilities. Theorem 1 already tells us its unique minimizer.

5.4 Theorem 3 — proportional betting (binary), with proof

Theorem 3 (proportional betting). In the binary game with odds $2$ and $0 \lt p \lt 1$, $\mathbb{E}[\log S]$ is maximized over $q \in [0,1]$ uniquely at $q = p$, and

\[ \max_q \mathbb{E}[\log S] = \log 2B - h_2(p), \]

where $h_2(p) = p \log \tfrac1p + (1-p) \log \tfrac{1}{1-p}$ is the binary entropy function. Moreover, for any $q$,

\[ \mathbb{E}[\log S_{\text{opt}}] - \mathbb{E}[\log S_q] = D\bigl((p, 1{-}p) \,\Vert\, (q, 1{-}q)\bigr). \]

Proof. Apply the decomposition $H(P, Q) = H(P) + D(P \Vert Q)$ of Section 4.5 to the bracket, with $P = (p, 1-p)$ and $Q = (q, 1-q)$:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\log S] = \log 2B - h_2(p) - D\bigl((p, 1{-}p) \,\Vert\, (q, 1{-}q)\bigr). \]

Only the last term depends on $q$; by Theorem 2 it is $\geq 0$ with equality iff $(q, 1-q) = (p, 1-p)$, i.e. $q = p$. Both claims follow. (Endpoints are consistent: $q \in \{0, 1\}$ with $0 \lt p \lt 1$ gives $D = +\infty$ and indeed $\mathbb{E}[\log S] = -\infty$ — the all-in strategies are infinitely bad on the log scale.) $\blacksquare$

"Bet your beliefs, in exact proportion": the optimal split copies the probability vector, regardless of the (fair) odds. And the cost of betting the wrong beliefs $Q$ is precisely the KL divergence from your beliefs to the truth — the third costume of the same quantity, after guessing bills and (in Cover & Thomas Ch. 5) code lengths. This log-optimal criterion is due to Kelly [4], whose 1956 paper set up exactly this horse-race correspondence between information rate and wealth growth.

5.5 Doubling rate: what the "≈" means

Definition (doubling rate, binary game). For betting fractions $(q, 1-q)$,

\[ W = \log 2 - \Bigl(p \log \frac{1}{q} + (1-p) \log \frac{1}{1-q}\Bigr) = \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\log \frac{S}{B}\Bigr], \]

the expected log growth factor of one round (the budget-free part of $\mathbb{E}[\log S] = \log B + W$).

The deck writes $S \approx B \times 2^W$ and $S_n \approx B \times 2^{nW}$. Precisely: repeat the same strategy over $n$ i.i.d. rounds, reinvesting the full bankroll. The per-round growth factors $G_k = S_k / S_{k-1}$ are i.i.d. with $\mathbb{E}[\log G_k] = W$, and

\[ \frac{1}{n} \log \frac{S_n}{B} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \log G_k \;\longrightarrow\; W \quad \text{almost surely,} \]

by the strong law of large numbers (LLN is treated later in this course, prob05; the statement is standard). Equivalently $S_n = B \cdot 2^{n(W + o(1))}$: log-wealth grows linearly at slope $W$ bits per bet, so wealth grows (or decays) exponentially at rate $W$. Three regimes: $W \gt 0$ exponential growth, $W = 0$ treading water, $W \lt 0$ exponential ruin. Note it is the typical (almost-sure) exponent, not the exponent of $\mathbb{E}[S_n]$ — Section 5.2 showed those can disagree wildly.

5.6 Theorem 4 — horse racing, with proof

Generalize to $M$ horses: horse $i$ wins with probability $p_X(i)$; odds are $M$-for-$1$ on every horse (uniform fair odds); the gambler splits the budget as $\bigl(Q(1)B, \dots, Q(M)B\bigr)$ where $Q$ is a pmf. Winner takes all: if horse $i$ wins, $S = M\, Q(i)\, B$.

Theorem 4 (proportional betting, $M$ horses).

\[ \mathbb{E}[\log S] = \log B + \log M - \sum_{i=1}^{M} p_X(i) \log \frac{1}{Q(i)} \] \[ = \log B + \log M - H(p_X) - D(p_X \,\Vert\, Q), \]

maximized uniquely at $Q = p_X$, with maximum $\log B + \log M - H(p_X)$; the loss for betting $Q$ instead is exactly $D(p_X \Vert Q)$. The doubling rate of strategy $Q$ is

\[ W(Q) = \log M - H(p_X) - D(p_X \,\Vert\, Q). \]

Proof. Expand by LOTUS and split the log of the triple product:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\log S] = \sum_{i=1}^{M} p_X(i) \log \bigl(M\, Q(i)\, B\bigr) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} p_X(i) \Bigl[\log M + \log B + \log Q(i)\Bigr]. \]

The constants pull out ($\sum_i p_X(i) = 1$), leaving

\[ \mathbb{E}[\log S] = \log B + \log M - \sum_{i=1}^{M} p_X(i) \log \frac{1}{Q(i)} = \log B + \log M - H(p_X, Q). \]

Apply $H(p_X, Q) = H(p_X) + D(p_X \Vert Q)$ (Section 4.5); only $D$ depends on $Q$, and Theorem 2 finishes as before. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 3 is the case $M = 2$. Note $W^{*} = \log M - H(p_X)$ can be negative: at uniform fair odds, the game is only profitable if the race is predictable enough ($H(p_X) \lt \log M$, i.e. $p_X$ non-uniform); against a perfectly uniform race the best doubling rate is $0$. Uniform-$Q$ check: $D(p_X \Vert \text{uniform}) = \log M - H(p_X) = W^*$, so the uniform bettor has $W = W^* - W^* = 0$ exactly — betting $B/M$ on each horse at $M$-for-$1$ odds returns the budget unchanged, deterministically.

5.7 The 3-horse race, fully verified

Win probabilities $p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)$, odds $3$-for-$1$. First, $H(p) = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 = 1.5$ bits, so

\[ W^{*} = \log 3 - H(p) = 1.58496 - 1.5 = 0.08496 \approx 0.085 \text{ bits/race}. \]

Over $100$ races the optimal bettor ($Q = p$) multiplies wealth by

\[ 2^{100\, W^{*}} = 2^{8.496} \approx 361 \]

(the deck's $\times 362$ comes from rounding the exponent to $100 \times 0.085 = 8.5$; $2^{8.5} \approx 362.0$).

The three gamblers of the deck's table, with each $D(p \Vert Q)$ computed from the definition:

  • $Q = p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)$: $D = 0$, $W = W^* \approx +0.085$, $100$ races $\rightarrow \times\, 2^{8.496} \approx 361$ ($\approx 362$ with the deck's rounding).
  • $Q = (\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$: $D(p \Vert Q) = \log 3 - H(p) = 0.08496 \approx 0.085$ (uniform-$Q$ shortcut), so $W = W^* - D = 0$ exactly: treading water, $\times 1$ forever. As noted above, this is deterministic, not just on average.
  • $Q = (\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac12)$ (favorite and longshot swapped): \[ D(p \,\Vert\, Q) = \tfrac12 \log \frac{1/2}{1/4} + \tfrac14 \log \frac{1/4}{1/4} + \tfrac14 \log \frac{1/4}{1/2} \] \[ = \tfrac12 (1) + \tfrac14 (0) + \tfrac14 (-1) = 0.25 \text{ bits}, \] so $W = 0.08496 - 0.25 = -0.16504 \approx -0.165$, and $100$ races multiply wealth by $2^{-16.504} \approx 1.08 \times 10^{-5} \approx 1/92{,}900$ — the deck's $\times 1/93000$. Same race, same odds; beliefs alone separate a $\times 361$ fortune from near-total ruin.

The classifier analogy is now exact: a model that outputs label probabilities is a gambler betting a pmf on the outcome, and the next section charges it the same penalty.

6  Cross-Entropy Loss

6.1 Classification setup

Inputs $x \in \mathcal{X}$ (e.g. $\mathbb{R}^n$ for images); labels $y \in [M] = \{1, \dots, M\}$. A (probabilistic) classifier is a map $f : \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{M}$, where $\mathcal{M}$ is the set of pmfs on $[M]$ (the probability simplex): $f(x) = \bigl(f(x)_1, \dots, f(x)_M\bigr)$ with $f(x)_i \geq 0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^M f(x)_i = 1$, and $f(x)_i$ read as "the model's believed probability that $x$ has label $i$". In practice a network produces arbitrary real scores (logits) and the softmax layer maps them into $\mathcal{M}$; softmax outputs are strictly positive, a fact that matters below.

Definition (cross-entropy loss). For prediction $f(x)$ and true label $y$,

\[ \ell\bigl(f(x), y\bigr) = \log \frac{1}{f(x)_y}, \]

the surprisal of the true label under the model's pmf ($= +\infty$ if $f(x)_y = 0$).

Only the probability assigned to the true label enters; how the remaining mass is spread among wrong labels is irrelevant to this sample's loss.

6.2 The 3-class table, verified

True label $y = \text{cat}$; three models bet on (cat, dog, ship):

ModelBetLoss $\log 1/f(x)_{\text{cat}}$
confident, right$(0.99, 0.005, 0.005)$$\log \tfrac{1}{0.99} \approx 0.0145$ bits
decent$(0.7, 0.2, 0.1)$$\log \tfrac{1}{0.7} \approx 0.5146$ bits
confused$(0.3, 0.4, 0.3)$$\log \tfrac{1}{0.3} \approx 1.7370$ bits

6.3 One-hot labels are pmfs; Theorem 5

Definition (one-hot vector). For $y \in [M]$, the one-hot vector $y^{(o)} \in \mathbb{R}^M$ has coordinates

\[ y^{(o)}_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i = y, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

It is a valid pmf on $[M]$ — the ground-truth distribution, deterministic at $y$, with $H(y^{(o)}) = 0$.

Both the label and the prediction are now pmfs on $[M]$, so their KL divergence is a natural performance measure — and it turns out to be the loss.

Theorem 5 (CE loss is a KL divergence).

\[ \ell\bigl(f(x), y\bigr) = D\bigl(y^{(o)} \,\Vert\, f(x)\bigr). \]

Proof. Expand the definition and use the convention $0 \log \tfrac{0}{q} = 0$ (Section 4.1) on every coordinate $i \neq y$, where $y^{(o)}_i = 0$:

\[ D\bigl(y^{(o)} \,\Vert\, f(x)\bigr) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} y^{(o)}_i \log \frac{y^{(o)}_i}{f(x)_i} = 1 \cdot \log \frac{1}{f(x)_y} = \ell\bigl(f(x), y\bigr). \qquad \blacksquare \]

Exactly one term survives: the true label's surprisal. (The absolute-continuity requirement of Section 4.1 here reads $f(x)_y \gt 0$; softmax guarantees it, and both sides equal $+\infty$ otherwise, so the identity holds in all cases.)

Mind the argument order: the truth sits in the first slot, $D(\text{truth} \Vert \text{model})$ — the direction in which zeros of the model are punished infinitely (Section 4.2), which is the behavior a loss should have. The reverse direction $D(f(x) \Vert y^{(o)})$ would be $+\infty$ whenever the model hedges at all (any $f(x)_i \gt 0$ with $i \neq y$), which is useless as a loss.

6.4 Theorem 6 — the decomposition, and soft labels

Theorem 6 (cross-entropy decomposition). For any pmfs $P, Q$ on $[M]$ (with the usual conventions, and both sides possibly $+\infty$ together):

\[ H(P, Q) = H(P) + D(P \,\Vert\, Q). \]

Proof. Split the log-ratio inside $D$ (over the support of $P$; zero-mass terms vanish on both sides):

\[ D(P \,\Vert\, Q) = \sum_i P(i) \log \frac{P(i)}{Q(i)} = \underbrace{\sum_i P(i) \log P(i)}_{-H(P)} + \underbrace{\sum_i P(i) \log \frac{1}{Q(i)}}_{H(P,\,Q)}, \]

i.e. $D = -H(P) + H(P, Q)$. Rearranging gives the claim. $\blacksquare$

(This is the identity already derived in Section 4.5; the source notes' displayed derivation drops a $p_X(x)$ factor in one intermediate line — a typo — and the version above is the corrected computation.) Two specializations:

  • One-hot label: $H(y^{(o)}) = 0$, so $H\bigl(y^{(o)}, f(x)\bigr) = D\bigl(y^{(o)} \Vert f(x)\bigr)$ — cross-entropy and KL coincide, and both equal the loss of Theorem 5. This is why the loss is legitimately called either name.
  • Soft label $y^{(\text{soft})}$ (label smoothing [6], distillation [7]): now \[ D\bigl(y^{(\text{soft})} \,\Vert\, f(x)\bigr) = H\bigl(y^{(\text{soft})}, f(x)\bigr) - H\bigl(y^{(\text{soft})}\bigr), \] so CE and KL differ by the constant $H(y^{(\text{soft})})$, fixed by the data.

The deck's soft-label numbers, verified. $y^{(\text{soft})} = (0.6, 0.3, 0.1)$, $f(x) = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1)$:

\[ H\bigl(y^{(\text{soft})}, f(x)\bigr) = 0.6 \log \tfrac{1}{0.7} + 0.3 \log \tfrac{1}{0.2} + 0.1 \log \tfrac{1}{0.1} \] \[ \approx 0.3087 + 0.6966 + 0.3322 \approx 1.3375 \text{ bits}, \] \[ H\bigl(y^{(\text{soft})}\bigr) = 0.6 \log \tfrac{1}{0.6} + 0.3 \log \tfrac{1}{0.3} + 0.1 \log \tfrac{1}{0.1} \] \[ \approx 0.4422 + 0.5211 + 0.3322 \approx 1.2955 \text{ bits}, \] \[ D\bigl(y^{(\text{soft})} \,\Vert\, f(x)\bigr) = 0.6 \log \tfrac{0.6}{0.7} + 0.3 \log \tfrac{0.3}{0.2} + 0.1 \log \tfrac{0.1}{0.1} \] \[ \approx -0.1334 + 0.1755 + 0 \approx 0.0421 \text{ bits}. \]

Indeed $1.3375 = 1.2955 + 0.0421$ up to rounding — the deck's $1.338 = 1.295 + 0.042$. (Note again a negative individual KL term.)

6.5 Punchline: minimizing CE is minimizing KL

Fix the label distribution $P$ (one-hot or soft) and optimize the model $Q = f(x)$. Since $H(P)$ contains no $Q$,

\[ \arg\min_{Q}\; H(P, Q) = \arg\min_{Q}\; \bigl[H(P) + D(P \,\Vert\, Q)\bigr] = \arg\min_{Q}\; D(P \,\Vert\, Q), \]

and by Theorem 2 the common minimizer is $Q = P$, where $D = 0$ and CE attains its floor $H(P)$ (Theorem 1 again).

So training with cross-entropy loss is pulling the model's pmf toward the label distribution, with disagreement measured in KL. Details the deck compresses:

  • Achievability of zero loss. For a one-hot label, zero loss requires $f(x) = y^{(o)}$ exactly. A softmax over finite logits outputs strictly positive coordinates, so it can approach but never reach zero loss — the infimum is $0$, not attained. (This is one practical motivation for label smoothing: a smoothed target lies in the interior of the simplex and is attainable.)
  • Base and scale invariance. Changing the log base multiplies the loss by a positive constant (bits vs nats, Section 1); adding the constant $H(P)$ or not (CE vs KL) shifts it. Neither changes the argmin, so PyTorch's nat-based CE, the bit-based formulas here, and "KL to the label" all train identically.
  • Why log, why reciprocal — the answer assembled. $\log \tfrac{1}{Q}$ is not an arbitrary decreasing function: it is the surprisal, the unique (up to base) pricing under which the true distribution is the cheapest model (Theorem 1), wrong beliefs bleed wealth at rate $D$ (Theorems 3–4), and the loss of a classifier is exactly the divergence to the truth (Theorem 5). The alternatives $1 - f(x)_y$ and $(1 - f(x)_y)^2$ admit no such identity — and, unlike the log, they assign a finite penalty to declaring the truth impossible.

Left open, deliberately. Why measure disagreement between pmfs by this divergence rather than another ($\chi^2$, total variation, Wasserstein, …)? KL's privileges — the chain rule, its role as the exponent in large-deviation and hypothesis-testing limits, and its appearance in GAN training as (a relative of) the Jensen–Shannon divergence game — are developed later in the course; see [1, Ch. 2, 11] and [2] for the classical answers.
