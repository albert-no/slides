1. Jailbreaks: Getting Around Safety Training, and Measuring It Honestly

What safety training adds · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈0.5 min

Welcome to Lecture 10. In Lecture 8 an attacker nudged pixels to fool a classifier. Today the target is a chat model that has been trained to refuse some requests, and the attacker's tool is the prompt itself.

The second half of the title matters as much as the first. Jailbreak numbers are easy to inflate, so we will learn to read them as carefully as we learn the attacks.

Key takeaway: Today: getting around refusal training, and counting it honestly.

3. Section 01 — What Safety Training Adds

What safety training adds · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈1.5 min

A chat model learns to refuse some requests. What would it mean to get around that, and how would we count whether we succeeded? Those are the two questions of this section.

Key takeaway: Define the attack and the scoreboard before looking at attacks.

4. Same Request: Refused, Then Answered

What safety training adds · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈3 min

Here is the phenomenon in one figure. On the left, a request is refused. In panel (a), the same request comes with an instruction that the reply must open with a particular line. In panel (b), the request is written in Base64. In both cases the model answers.

Notice what the request is: deliberately mild, chosen by the authors so the figure can be published. The point is the pattern, not the content.

Read the setup strip: the attacker only types prompts into a black-box chat and wants an answer the model refused. Every study today gets that strip.

Key takeaway: Rewording alone, with no special access, turned a refusal into an answer.

The figure

Fig. 1 of Wei et al.: a refusal on a plain request, then compliance under (a) a demanded opening line ("prefix injection") and (b) a Base64-encoded request. The figure uses a deliberately mild example chosen by the authors.

Setup, model, date

Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt (UC Berkeley), first posted July 2023. Models: GPT-4 and Claude v1.3, the versions available in spring 2023.

What this establishes

That simple input transformations, with no gradient access and no weights, could flip refusal into compliance on frontier chat models of 2023.

What this does not establish

It does not show that these particular prompts still work; vendors patch known patterns. It does not measure how often they work; that is Table 1 (slide 14).

Assumptions

Black-box chat access only; the attacker can send arbitrary text.

Primary sources

  • Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, "Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?" (NeurIPS 2023) — competing objectives and mismatched generalization (§3); Fig. 1; Table 1 on 32 curated prompts.

5. A Jailbreak Bypasses Safeguards Through Inputs

What safety training adds · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈4.5 min

Our working definition: a jailbreak is an input that gets a safety-trained model to produce what its safeguards are meant to block.

Two words do the work. "Input": the attacker acts through what the model reads, not through its weights. "Safeguards": we are attacking a behavior someone deliberately trained or attached.

The table separates neighbours. Prompt injection, where text from a third party the app reads hijacks the model, is a different threat and is covered in the prompt-injection lecture. Data poisoning, which corrupts training data, was Lecture 9. Removing safety by fine-tuning the weights appears today only as a labelled contrast, because it is not a prompt attack.

Key takeaway: A jailbreak acts through inputs; weight-level safety removal is a different threat model.

Assumptions

The deployer, not the user, decides what is blocked. The definition says nothing about whether the blocked content is actually dangerous; that judgment is part of evaluation (Section 05).

Going further

Usage varies in the literature: some papers call any successful harmful-output attack a "jailbreak", including fine-tuning. We keep the narrower, input-only meaning so that results compare within one threat model.

6. Recap: Refusal Is Taught After Pretraining

What safety training adds · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈6 min

A short refresher from Lecture 7. A pretrained model only predicts the next token. Instruction tuning teaches it to follow requests; preference training teaches it which answers are preferred, including when to refuse.

Where do the preference labels come from? In RLHF, from human raters comparing answers. In Constitutional AI, largely from an AI model judging answers against a written list of principles. These are two approaches to the same step, not two stages you must run in sequence.

Either way, refusal is a learned behavior layered on top of a capable model. Like any learned behavior, it generalizes only as far as its training data reaches.

Key takeaway: Refusal is learned late and generalizes only as far as its training data.

Primary sources

  • Bai et al., "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback" (arXiv 2022) — AI feedback guided by a written list of principles in place of human harmlessness labels.

7. Two Ways to Fail: Refusing Too Little or Too Much

What safety training adds · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈7.5 min

A two-by-two. Rows: is the request benign or harmful? Columns: does the model answer or refuse? Answering benign requests is helpful. Refusing harmful ones is a safe refusal.

The two off-diagonal cells are both failures. Answering a harmful request is under-refusal: that is the jailbreak's target. Refusing a benign request is over-refusal: the cost of caution.

Keep both cells in view all lecture. A defense that only shrinks the red cell may quietly grow the grey one.

Key takeaway: Safety has two error types; a defense must report both.

The figure

A confusion-matrix sketch drawn for this lecture. Red marks under-refusal (jailbreak success); grey marks over-refusal.

Assumptions

That "benign" and "harmful" can be labelled. In practice the boundary is contested and policy-dependent; XSTest (slide 47) builds prompts near that boundary on purpose.

8. What the Attacker Can Touch Sets the Threat

What safety training adds · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈9 min

Attacks differ in access, and access changes what is possible. The lowest rung is chat only: type prompts, read replies. Hand-written jailbreaks, PAIR, many-shot and Best-of-N all live here.

One rung up, the attacker controls part of the response or has gradients from an open model: prefilling the start of the reply, or GCG suffix search.

The top rung is weights: fine-tuning or editing the model. That is not a prompt attack, and we will label it as a contrast when it appears.

The rule: compare attacks on the same rung. An attack with gradients beating an attack without them tells you little.

Key takeaway: Compare attacks only within the same access level.

The figure

An access ladder drawn for this lecture, with today's attacks placed on their rungs.

Going further

Prefilling sits on the middle rung because some commercial APIs let the user write the start of the assistant turn; with open weights it is always possible.

9. Every Attack Success Rate Needs Four Labels

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈10 min

ASR, attack success rate, is the share of requests counted as successful. Every ASR needs four labels before it means anything.

The denominator: which requests, and how many? The budget: one try per request, or the best of N tries? The model: name and dated version. The judge: who decided the reply was harmful, and how often is that judge wrong?

Two ASRs that differ on any of these labels are not directly comparable. We will check all four on every number today.

Key takeaway: An ASR without denominator, budget, model and judge is not a result.

Going further

The budget label is the one most often omitted. "At least one success in N attempts" grows quickly with N even when the per-attempt rate is tiny; slide 32 makes this concrete, and lec10tech gives the formula.

10. Section 02 — Why Refusal Is Fragile

Why refusal is fragile · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈10.5 min

Safety training works on its own test prompts. Why does a reworded request still get through? This section collects explanations, each tested in a particular setting.

Key takeaway: Several explanations, each with its own evidence and access level.

11. Two Hypotheses for Why Safety Training Fails

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈12 min

Wei and colleagues proposed two failure modes. Competing objectives: the model was trained both to be helpful and to be safe, and a prompt can make the helpful objective win. Mismatched generalization: pretraining gave the model skills, such as reading Base64, that safety training never covered, so the prompt leaves the safe ground.

Note the status of these ideas. They were proposed from tests on GPT-4 and Claude v1.3 in 2023. They organise the evidence well, but they are explanations, not proofs.

Key takeaway: Two failure modes: objectives that conflict, and safety that does not cover all skills.

Setup, model, date

Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, NeurIPS 2023, Section 3.

What this does not establish

They do not show a mechanism inside the model; they are behavioral hypotheses that predict which attacks should work. Slides 15–18 look for mechanisms in open models.

Primary sources

  • Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, "Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?" (NeurIPS 2023) — competing objectives and mismatched generalization (§3); Fig. 1; Table 1 on 32 curated prompts.

12. Competing Objectives: Make Refusing Costly

Why refusal is fragile · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈14 min

A chat model is trained to follow instructions and trained to refuse harmful asks. Usually those agree. A competing-objectives attack adds instructions that a refusal would break.

Three families from Wei et al.: prefix injection demands a particular opening line; refusal suppression forbids apologies or disclaimers; role-play asks the model to stay in a character. Each makes refusing look like a failure to follow instructions.

We name the families; working prompts are not shown, here or anywhere in this course.

Key takeaway: Competing-objective attacks turn the model's instruction-following against its refusal.

The figure

A tug-of-war schematic drawn for this lecture: "follow instructions" versus "refuse harmful asks", with the attack adding weight on the instruction side.

Setup, model, date

Primary sources

  • Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, "Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?" (NeurIPS 2023) — competing objectives and mismatched generalization (§3); Fig. 1; Table 1 on 32 curated prompts.

13. Mismatched Generalization: Skills Outrun Safety

Why refusal is fragile · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈16 min

The outer region is everything the model can understand, learned in pretraining. The inner region is what safety training covered: mostly plain English chat.

In between lie inputs the model can still read: Base64 and other encodings, simple ciphers, low-resource languages, unusual output formats. A request there is understood, but the refusal never learned to fire.

The picture is a sketch, not to scale. It predicts something testable: attacks that move into understood-but-uncovered territory should work, and they should work better on more capable models that read more encodings.

Key takeaway: If the model can read it but safety training never saw it, refusal may not fire.

The figure

A nested-region sketch drawn for this lecture; not to scale.

Setup, model, date

Wei et al., Section 3.2.

Going further

Wei et al. report that GPT-3.5 Turbo cannot follow Base64-encoded instructions (their Fig. 2 and Table 3), while GPT-4 can: capability enables the attack. This is why "smarter" can mean "more attack surface".

Primary sources

  • Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, "Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?" (NeurIPS 2023) — competing objectives and mismatched generalization (§3); Fig. 1; Table 1 on 32 curated prompts.

14. Combined Attacks Beat Single Ones

Why refusal is fragile · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈18 min

Now the numbers. Each cell is the share of outputs hand-labelled as harmful compliance (the paper's BAD BOT rate) on 32 curated prompts.

A plain request almost never works: 3% on GPT-4, 0% on Claude v1.3. Refusal suppression alone: 25% and 16%. Base64 alone: 34% and 38%. Combining several tricks, exploiting both failure modes: 94% and 81%.

The last row is "adaptive": count success if any of 28 attacks works. That reaches 100% on both. Notice the budget label changed: that row is any of 28, not one try.

Key takeaway: Combining the two failure modes was far stronger than either alone.

The figure

Selected rows of Table 1 of Wei et al. Values are fractions of outputs on the curated set.

Setup, model, date

32 curated harmful prompts (all 16 used to evaluate GPT-4 in its technical report, plus 16 adapted from the red-teaming dataset of Ganguli et al.), GPT-4 and Claude v1.3 as of 2023, outputs hand-labelled by the authors.

What this establishes

That on 2023 frontier models, combined attacks produced harmful compliance on most curated prompts.

What this does not establish

32 prompts is a small denominator; the adaptive row is a best-of-28 budget; the models have since been updated.

Assumptions

The authors' labels of "harmful compliance" (BAD BOT) are the judge.

Primary sources

  • Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, "Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?" (NeurIPS 2023) — competing objectives and mismatched generalization (§3); Fig. 1; Table 1 on 32 curated prompts.

15. Aligned and Base Models Differ Mostly in the First Few Tokens

Why refusal is fragile · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈20 min

Qi and colleagues asked where safety training changed the model. They compared each aligned model with its base model, token by token, on harmful responses.

The x-axis is the position in the response; the y-axis is how different the two models' predictions are, measured by KL divergence. Most of the difference sits in the first few tokens.

The authors call this shallow safety alignment: the aligned model mainly learned to start with a refusal. Two open 7B model pairs; a finding in those models, not a law.

Key takeaway: In the tested open models, alignment changed mostly the first few tokens.

The figure

Fig. 1 of Qi et al. (2025): per-token KL divergence between aligned and base models on harmful HEx-PHI responses.

Setup, model, date

Qi et al. (Princeton and Google DeepMind), ICLR 2025. Pairs: Llama-2-7B vs Llama-2-7B-Chat, Gemma-7B vs Gemma-1.1-7B-IT. HEx-PHI: 330 harmful instructions across 11 categories.

What this establishes

In these pairs, alignment's measurable effect is concentrated at the start of the reply.

What this does not establish

It does not show that all aligned models are shallow, nor that depth alone predicts robustness.

Primary sources

  • Qi et al., "Safety Alignment Should Be Made More Than Just a Few Tokens Deep" (ICLR 2025) — per-token KL (Fig. 1), prefilling (Fig. 2), recovery-example augmentation (Table 2, §3.1).

16. Prefill a Few Harmful Tokens and Refusal Collapses

Why refusal is fragile · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈22 min

If safety lives in the first few tokens, what happens if the attacker writes those tokens? That is a prefilling attack: the attacker supplies the start of the reply, possible with open weights or with an API that allows prefilling.

The x-axis is how many harmful tokens are written for the model; the y-axis is ASR judged by GPT-4 on 330 prompts. Aligned models start near 0% and rise to about 55%. Base models stay high throughout.

Note the rung: this needs response control, not chat only.

Key takeaway: Writing the opening of the reply bypassed refusal in the tested aligned models.

The figure

Fig. 2 of Qi et al. (2025): ASR vs number of prefilled harmful tokens, aligned vs base models.

Setup, model, date

Llama-2-7B-Chat and Gemma-1.1-7B-IT, HEx-PHI, GPT-4 judge.

What this establishes

Prefilling a short harmful start sharply raised harmfulness, consistent with the shallow-alignment picture.

Assumptions

The attacker can control the first tokens of the assistant turn.

Primary sources

  • Qi et al., "Safety Alignment Should Be Made More Than Just a Few Tokens Deep" (ICLR 2025) — per-token KL (Fig. 1), prefilling (Fig. 2), recovery-example augmentation (Table 2, §3.1).

17. Training on Recovery Examples Deepened Refusal

Why refusal is fragile · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈24 min

The fix they tested: train on replies that start harmful and then recover into a refusal. The model learns that refusing is possible at any point, not only at the first token.

Read the table. Prefilling 5 harmful tokens: ASR 42.1% before, 2.8% after. Prefilling 40 tokens: 57.0% to 4.5%. GCG suffix search: 36.5% to 18.4%. A decoding-parameter exploit: 54.9% to 11.3%.

Utility roughly held: AlpacaEval win rate 51.8% to 49.5%. And the caveat: these are the attacks they tested. A new attack designed against the augmented model was not part of this table.

Key takeaway: Teaching recovery made refusal deeper against the tested attacks.

The figure

Selected rows of Table 2 of Qi et al. (2025); ASR % on HEx-PHI, mean of 3 runs.

Setup, model, date

Llama-2-7B-Chat fine-tuned with safety-recovery augmentation (Section 3.1).

What this does not establish

Not a guarantee: adaptive attacks against the augmented model were not the focus of the table.

Primary sources

  • Qi et al., "Safety Alignment Should Be Made More Than Just a Few Tokens Deep" (ICLR 2025) — per-token KL (Fig. 1), prefilling (Fig. 2), recovery-example augmentation (Table 2, §3.1).

18. Removing One Direction Removed Most Refusals

Why refusal is fragile · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈26 min

Arditi and colleagues looked inside. They computed one direction in activation space, the difference between average activations on harmful and harmless instructions, and removed it.

Orange bars are refusal measured by phrase matching; blue bars are a separate safety classifier; hatched bars are with the direction removed. Refusal drops across models.

Two cautions. This is white-box activation editing: an explanation, not an attack on a chat app. And it is a finding in 13 open models up to 72B parameters, not a universal mechanism.

Key takeaway: In tested open models, refusal was largely carried by one direction.

The figure

Fig. 1 of Arditi et al.: refusal and safety scores with and without ablating the refusal direction, 100 harmful JailbreakBench instructions.

Setup, model, date

Arditi et al., NeurIPS 2024; 13 open chat models up to 72B parameters.

What this establishes

Refusal in these models is low-dimensional enough that one direction mediates much of it.

What this does not establish

It does not show that closed models work the same way, nor that a chat-only attacker can exploit this.

Assumptions

Full access to activations.

Primary sources

  • Arditi et al., "Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction" (NeurIPS 2024) — difference-in-means refusal direction; ablation across 13 open chat models up to 72B (Fig. 1).

19. Contrast: Fine-Tuning Access Removed Safety Cheaply

Why refusal is fragile · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈28.5 min

A clearly labelled contrast. Here the attacker fine-tunes the model, through a tuning API or with open weights. That is not a jailbreak in our sense; it is safety removal at the weight level.

Ten harmful examples for five epochs took GPT-3.5 Turbo from 1.8% to 88.8% harmfulness, and Llama-2-7B-Chat from 0.3% to 50%. Ten "obedient persona" examples, with nothing harmful in them, did similar damage. Even benign Alpaca data raised harmfulness.

The GPT-3.5 ten-example run cost under twenty cents. The lesson for us: once an attacker has the weights, prompt-level safeguards are not the line of defense.

Key takeaway: Fine-tuning access removed safety training cheaply; that is a different threat from jailbreaking.

The figure

Selected cells of Tables 1–3 of Qi et al. (2024): harmfulness rate before → after fine-tuning, GPT-4 judge on HEx-PHI (330 prompts).

Setup, model, date

Qi et al., ICLR 2024; GPT-3.5 Turbo via OpenAI's fine-tuning API in 2023 and Llama-2-7B-Chat.

What this establishes

Small fine-tuning runs can undo safety training, even with benign data.

What this does not establish

It does not describe what a chat-only user can do.

Primary sources

  • Qi et al., "Fine-tuning Aligned Language Models Compromises Safety, Even When Users Do Not Intend To!" (ICLR 2024) — harmful, identity-shifting and benign fine-tuning; Tables 1–3 on HEx-PHI.

20. Each Finding Comes With Its Access Level

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈30 min

A summary table so we do not merge the section into one story. Competing objectives and mismatch: chat only, on GPT-4 and Claude v1.3. Shallow alignment: prefilling the reply, on Llama-2 and Gemma 7B. One refusal direction: activation access, on 13 open models. 21. Section 03 — Searching for Jailbreaks

Searching for jailbreaks · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈30.5 min

People found the first jailbreaks by hand. Can an algorithm search for them, and what does the search cost?

22. Hand-Written Jailbreaks Fall Into Families

Searching for jailbreaks · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈32.5 min

Hand-written jailbreaks cluster into families: role-play personas, forced openings and no-refusal rules on the competing-objectives side; encodings, ciphers and low-resource languages on the mismatched-generalization side.

Families are named, prompts are not shown.

Human red-teaming is slow, and each new model version needs new hand-crafted prompts. That motivates automated search.

Key takeaway: Manual jailbreaks group into the two failure modes, and they do not scale.

23. Automated Attacks Are a Search Loop

Searching for jailbreaks · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈34 min

Every automated attack has the same loop: propose a candidate prompt, send it to the target, score the response, propose a better candidate.

Three design choices distinguish attacks. How to propose: gradients or an attacker LLM. What access: weights or only queries. How to score: likelihood of a target text, or a judge model.

Every trip round the loop spends budget. Count the queries.

Key takeaway: Automated jailbreaking is search; its cost is the number of trips round the loop.

Going further

Perez et al. (2022) used a language model to generate test cases for another language model, an early version of this loop for red teaming in general.

Primary sources

  • Perez et al., "Red Teaming Language Models with Language Models" (EMNLP 2022) — an LM generates test cases for another LM; an early automated red-teaming loop.

24. GCG Optimizes a Suffix Toward an Agreeable Start

Searching for jailbreaks · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈36.5 min

GCG, Greedy Coordinate Gradient, attaches a suffix of 20 tokens to the harmful request and optimizes those tokens.

The objective: make an agreeable opening, like "Sure, here is", as likely as possible. The bet is that once the reply starts agreeing, it tends to keep going, the same intuition as shallow alignment.

Rung: the attacker needs an open model's weights and gradients. The goal is a reusable suffix.

Key takeaway: GCG searches token space for a suffix that makes the model start by agreeing.

The figure

A schematic drawn for this lecture: request + optimized suffix → model → target opening.

Setup, model, date

Zou et al. (CMU, Center for AI Safety, Google DeepMind), July 2023, Section 2.

Primary sources

  • Zou et al., "Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models" (arXiv 2023) — Greedy Coordinate Gradient (Algorithm 1), transfer to closed chatbots (Fig. 1, Table 2).

25. Each Step Tests Hundreds of One-Token Swaps

Searching for jailbreaks · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈39 min

One GCG step has three parts. Use the gradient to list the top 256 promising token swaps at each suffix position. Sample 512 candidates, each with one swap. Run all 512 forward passes and keep the best by exact loss.

Repeat for 500 steps. That is 500 × 512, about 256 thousand model evaluations per suffix. Hold on to that number for the comparison with PAIR.

Key takeaway: GCG costs on the order of 256K model evaluations per suffix.

Setup, model, date

Algorithm 1 and Section 3 settings of Zou et al.; the 256K query count as tabulated in Chao et al. (PAIR), Table 2.

Going further

The gradient is only a guide: token swaps are discrete, so GCG evaluates candidates exactly. lec10tech writes out the objective and the budget.

Primary sources

  • Zou et al., "Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models" (arXiv 2023) — Greedy Coordinate Gradient (Algorithm 1), transfer to closed chatbots (Fig. 1, Table 2).
  • Chao et al., "Jailbreaking Black Box Large Language Models in Twenty Queries" (arXiv 2310.08419) — PAIR (Fig. 2), judge comparison (Table 1), results on JailbreakBench (Table 2).

27. Transfer Worked Unevenly Across Closed Models

Searching for jailbreaks · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈43.5 min

The rates. With nothing added, ASR was 1.8% on GPT-3.5, 8% on GPT-4, 0% on Claude 2 and PaLM-2. One GCG suffix from Vicuna: about 34% on both GPT models, 32% on PaLM-2, still 0% on Claude 2.

The ensemble row counts a success if any of four suffixes works: 86.6% on GPT-3.5, 46.9% on GPT-4, 2.1% on Claude 2, 66% on PaLM-2. That is a bigger budget, not a better single attack.

Also read the judge: success meant a "reasonable attempt", which may involve human judgment.

Key takeaway: Transfer was real but uneven, and the headline row used a best-of-4 budget.

28. PAIR: An Attacker LLM Rewrites the Prompt

Searching for jailbreaks · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈45.5 min

PAIR, Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement, replaces gradients with an attacker language model. The attacker LLM reads the target's refusal, reasons about why it failed, and rewrites the prompt. A judge model scores each reply.

Rung: black-box, queries only. And the output is a readable prompt, unlike GCG's token string.

Key takeaway: PAIR searches with an attacker LLM and a judge, using only queries.

The figure

Fig. 2 of Chao et al.: the attacker–target–judge loop.

Setup, model, date

30. Gradient Search vs. LLM Search

Searching for jailbreaks · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈50 min

Side by side. GCG needs weights and gradients, about 256K queries per suffix, outputs an unreadable token string, and reaches closed models only by transfer. PAIR needs queries only, at most 90 per request, outputs a readable prompt, and attacks closed models directly.

Neither is "better" in general. Compare attacks at the same access and the same budget.

Key takeaway: Access and budget define the comparison.

Going further

32. Many Tries Turn a Small Rate Into a Large One

Scaling the attack budget · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈52.5 min

An illustration, not from a study. Suppose each try succeeds with probability 1%, independently. The chance of at least one success in N tries is one minus 0.99 to the N.

N = 1: 1%. N = 10: 9.6%. N = 100: 63%. N = 1,000: essentially 100%.

This is why "any of N" and "per try" must never be mixed. Real tries are not independent and do not share one p, so treat this as intuition, not a model.

Key takeaway: "At least once in N tries" grows fast even from a tiny per-try rate.

The figure

Bar chart drawn for this lecture from the formula $1-(1-p)^N$ with $p=0.01$.

Assumptions

Independent tries with equal success probability, which real attacks violate.

Going further

lec10tech derives the formula and the inverse question: how many tries until a target success level.

33. Many-Shot Jailbreaking Fills the Context With Fake Dialogues

The mechanism is in-context learning, the same ability that makes few-shot prompting useful. Long context windows made hundreds of shots possible.

Key takeaway: Best-of-N needs no cleverness: random variants and many tries.

38. A Found Jailbreak Often Fails When Resent

Scaling the attack budget · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈65.5 min

Here is the twist. Take each augmented prompt that succeeded and resend it 100 times. How often is it harmful again?

At temperature 1, Claude 3.5 Sonnet: 46%; GPT-4o: 24%; Gemini Pro: 15%. At temperature 0 the numbers barely change, because outputs were still not deterministic.

The authors conclude that Best-of-N exploits sampling randomness more than it finds a fixed weakness. "Any of N succeeded" and "this prompt works" are different claims.

Key takeaway: A Best-of-N success is often a lucky sample, not a reusable prompt.

The figure

39. Languages and Ciphers Exploit the Same Gap

Scaling the attack budget · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈67.5 min

Back to mismatched generalization. On GPT-4-0613, a plain English request from AdvBench succeeded under 1% of the time. Translating into low-resource languages, combined, reached 79%. Writing the request in an ASCII cipher reached 72%.

Now the judge. These studies counted "not refused" as success. When Souly et al. re-checked one language, Scots Gaelic, with a stricter judge, three samples contained no useful harmful content.

Lesson: whether the reply is actually useful needs its own check.

Key takeaway: Languages and ciphers bypassed refusal, but "not refused" overstated harm.

Setup, model, date

Yong et al. (Brown, 2023): AdvBench, 520 requests, 12 languages; 79.04% combined across low-resource languages. Yuan et al. (CipherChat, ICLR 2024): ASCII cipher 72.1% on gpt-4-0613, 23.7% on gpt-3.5-turbo-0613.

What this does not establish

High non-refusal does not mean high harmful usefulness; translation and decoding may degrade the answer.

Primary sources

40. Jailbreaks Are Adversarial Inputs Under a Looser Threat Model

Scaling the attack budget · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈69 min

Connect to Lecture 8. An adversarial example changes a classifier's input by a tiny amount within ε, and the label flips; success is easy to check.

A jailbreak changes a text generator's input with no size limit, and blocked content appears; success needs a judge.

Same game: search for inputs that break a learned rule. The looser threat model makes attacks easier and evaluation harder.

Key takeaway: Jailbreaks are adversarial examples with no size limit and a harder success test.

41. Budget Changes Every Number

Scaling the attack budget · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈70 min

Summary of the section. Many-shot: success grows with shots. Best-of-N: success grows with tries. And a found prompt may not work twice.

Whenever you read a jailbreak rate, ask: one try or any of N? And how large is N?

Key takeaway: Always ask for the budget.

42. Section 05 — Evaluation and Defenses

43. "Success" Depends on Who Judges It

Evaluation and defenses · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈71.5 min

A ladder of judges. A keyword rule: no "I'm sorry" means success. A classifier: is toxic text present? An LLM judge with a rubric. Human experts: is the answer truly useful? Cheap and scalable at the bottom, costly and closer to real harm at the top.

The same response can pass one judge and fail another. And not refusing is not the same as giving harmful help.

Key takeaway: The judge is part of the result.

44. Automated Judges Disagree With Humans in Both Directions

Evaluation and defenses · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈73 min

Chao et al. compared judges with human experts on 100 prompt–response pairs. Three experts labelled each pair; they agreed 95% of the time, and their majority is the reference.

GPT-4 as judge: 88% agreement, 16% false positives, 7% false negatives. The keyword rule from GCG: 80%, 23% false positives. Llama Guard: 76% agreement, but 47% false negatives. A BERT classifier: 66%, missing 74%.

Errors go both ways: some judges inflate ASR, others hide real jailbreaks.

Key takeaway: Each judge has its own false-positive and false-negative profile.

49. Defenses Sit at Three Places

Defenses sit in three places: an input filter screens the prompt, changes to the model itself alter its internals, and an output filter screens the reply.

Our two case studies: classifiers at both ends, and circuit breakers inside the model. Each layer adds cost: latency, false refusals, or lost capability.

Key takeaway: Every defense layer has a cost.
