When It Goes Wrong

Real cases, seven kinds of failure

Bias in the Courtroom

What happened

2016, Broward County, Florida. ProPublica re-analyzed 7,000+ COMPAS risk scores used in court.

What the evidence showed

False-positive and false-negative rates differed sharply by race. Northpointe disputed ProPublica’s fairness metric.

Why it matters

Fairness. Journalists' analysis; the vendor disputed the metric. Scores stayed in use.

Angwin, Larson, Mattu, and Kirchner, "Machine Bias" and "How We Analyzed the COMPAS Recidivism Algorithm", ProPublica 2016 — Broward County, FL.

Whose Face Does It See?

What happened

2018 audit of Microsoft, IBM, and Face++ gender classifiers on 1,270 faces.

What the audit showed

Error 0.8% for lighter-skinned men, 34.7% for darker-skinned women. Vendors’ training data was not examined.

Why it matters

Fairness. Peer-reviewed (FAT* 2018). Two vendors shipped fixes within months.

Buolamwini and Gebru, "Gender Shades: Intersectional Accuracy Disparities in Commercial Gender Classification", FAT* 2018 — Table 4.

A Tool That Learned to Discriminate

What happened

Amazon, internal résumé ranker, 2014–17. Reported by Reuters 2018; project scrapped.

What was reported

Sources said ten years of mostly male hires served as labels; the tool penalized “women's” and two women's colleges.

Why it matters

Fairness. Insider accounts; Amazon says recruiters never relied on the rankings.

Dastin, "Amazon Scraps Secret AI Recruiting Tool That Showed Bias Against Women", Reuters 2018 — reported, not adjudicated.

Models That Memorize People

What happened

2021, Carlini et al. A few-word prompt; GPT-2 emits a real name, address, phone.

How the failure worked

Strings seen once in training, recited verbatim. Bigger models memorized more.

Why it matters

Privacy. Peer-reviewed (USENIX Security 2021). Public web data, still identifying.

Carlini et al., "Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models", USENIX Security 2021 — Figure 1.

"Repeat the Word Poem Forever"

What happened

2023, ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo). “Repeat poem forever” spills raw training text, names included.

How the attack worked

It drifts out of chat mode into raw generation. $200 of queries, 10,000+ passages.

Why it matters

Privacy. Authors' paper and blog; OpenAI blocked the prompt, not the memorization.

Nasr et al., "Scalable Extraction of Training Data from (Production) Language Models", arXiv 2311.17035, 2023.

Your Prompts Are Data Too

Samsung, Korea, April 2023

Engineers pasted internal source code into ChatGPT. Company restricted generative-AI use (reported).

Italy, March 2023

Garante suspended ChatGPT for a month over personal data. €15M fine followed, December 2024.

Privacy cuts both ways: what the model learned, and what you type in.
Bloomberg, "Samsung Bans Staff's AI Use After Spotting ChatGPT Data Leak", May 2023 (reported).  ·  Garante, order of 30 March 2023; decision of 20 December 2024 (€15M).

Confident Fabrications, Real Costs

Mata v. Avianca, S.D.N.Y., 2023

Six ChatGPT-supplied precedents in a brief. All six fake. $5,000 sanction.

Deloitte Australia, 2025

A$440,000 government report cited sources that don't exist. Final instalment refunded.

Reliability: fluent, authoritative, wrong. Confirmed by court order and department statement

Mata v. Avianca, S.D.N.Y., sanctions order, 22 June 2023.  ·  Deloitte Australia / Dept. of Employment and Workplace Relations, October 2025.

A Chatbot That Made Promises

Moffatt v. Air Canada, Feb 2024

Bot invented a bereavement refund. BC tribunal: negligent misrepresentation, about C$812.

Chevrolet dealer, USA, Dec 2023

Prompted bot agreed to a "$1 car, no takesies-backsies." Viral; never enforced.

Reliability and security: the deployer owns the output

Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149.  ·  Chevrolet of Watsonville chatbot (Fullpath), December 2023, screenshots reported by Business Insider and others.

Too Agreeable to Be Honest

What happened

April 2025: OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update after users reported flattery.

What the evidence showed

Preference data favored belief-matching answers over truthful ones. OpenAI blamed over-weighted thumbs-up signals.

Why it matters

Reliability. OpenAI post-mortem: short-term thumbs-up feedback over-weighted.

Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models", ICLR 2024 — Figure 5.  ·  OpenAI, "Sycophancy in GPT-4o: what happened and what we're doing about it", 29 April 2025.

A Tiny Push Fools It

What happened

ICLR 2015, Goodfellow et al. Invisible noise: “panda” 57.7% becomes “gibbon” 99.3%.

How the attack worked

One gradient step; thousands of tiny pixel shifts add up. Authors attribute this to near-linear models.

Why it matters

Robustness. Reproduced across models and labs. Attacker needs pixel-level access.

Goodfellow, Shlens, and Szegedy, "Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples", ICLR 2015 — Figure 1.

Stickers on a Stop Sign

What happened

CVPR 2018, Eykholt et al. Stickers on a real stop sign read as Speed Limit 45.

How the attack worked

Perturbation optimized to survive distance, angle, light. 100% lab, 84.8% drive-by.

Why it matters

Robustness. Lab classifier, not a production car. Attacker needs a printer, not weights.

Eykholt et al., "Robust Physical-World Attacks on Deep Learning Visual Classification", CVPR 2018 — Figure 1, Table 2.

Talking the Model Out of Its Rules

What happened

NeurIPS 2023, Wei et al. GPT-4 and Claude v1.3 refuse in English, answer in Base64.

What the evidence showed

Encoded requests bypassed refusals. Authors’ hypothesis: pretraining covers Base64, safety tuning did not.

Why it matters

Security. These prompts are patched; the class of gap remains.

Wei, Haghtalab, and Steinhardt, "Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?", NeurIPS 2023 — Figure 1.

Hijacking the Assistant

What happened

June 2025, Microsoft 365 Copilot. One crafted email leaked private data. Zero clicks.

How the attack worked

Copilot retrieved the attacker's email and obeyed its text: prompt injection via retrieval.

Why it matters

Security. CVE-2025-32711, CVSS 9.3, fixed server-side. No known exploitation.

EchoLeak, CVE-2025-32711 (Microsoft 365 Copilot), disclosed by Aim Security, June 2025; Microsoft Security Response Center advisory.

The Car Did Not Brake

What happened

Tempe, Arizona, 18 March 2018. An Uber test vehicle killed a pedestrian at night.

Why it happened (NTSB)

Detected 5.6 s out, never classified as a person. Factory braking off. Driver on a phone.

Why it matters

Safety. NTSB final report 2019: every fallback failed at once.

NTSB, Highway Accident Report HWY18MH010, 2019. Photo: NTSB, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

What happened

24 March 2023. Midjourney v5 image of Pope Francis in a white puffer coat goes viral.

How it worked

Photographic realism in minutes from a prompt. Shared widely as real, per platform counts.

Why it matters

Provenance. Harmless here; the same pipeline now produces video and voice.

Seeing Is No Longer Believing

What happened

New Hampshire, January 2024. A cloned presidential voice robocalled voters: stay home.

How it worked

Seconds of public audio clone a voice. A text prompt yields a minute of video.

Why it matters

Provenance. FCC $6M forfeiture, Sept 2024; state jury acquitted the operative, June 2025.

Every Model Ate a Library

What happened

Bartz v. Anthropic, N.D. Cal., June 2025. Bought books: fair use. Pirated library: not.

What the court found

Millions of pirated copies downloaded to build a central library. Settlement covers about 500,000 works.

Why it matters

Data ownership. $1.5B class settlement, final approval July 2026. Binds nobody else.

Bartz v. Anthropic, N.D. Cal., fair-use order 23 June 2025; settlement final approval 20 July 2026. Status as of 2026-09.

The Young Are Hit First

What happened

US payroll records (ADP) through mid-2026, Stanford Digital Economy Lab.

What the evidence showed

Ages 22–25 in the most AI-exposed jobs fall well below trend; older workers do not. Correlational.

Why it matters

Society. Working paper, revised Aug 2026 (19% gap). Mechanism debated.

Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen, "Canaries in the Coal Mine?", Stanford Digital Economy Lab, Aug 2025, rev. Aug 2026 — Fig. 2, ages 22–25.

Threat-Model Thinking

The one habit that unifies the course

What Is a Threat Model?

  • Who is the adversary, and their goal?
  • What do they know about the system?
  • What can they actually do?

Answer these before "is it safe?"

Who

Anyone who can email you

Knows

The assistant reads the inbox

Can do

Send one message, nothing else

Worked example: EchoLeak, the threat model in three boxes

The Attack Map

Two axes, knowledge × timing, locate almost any attack.

No Threat Model, No Answer

59% vs 3%

Same model, same task. One tool removed from the wrapper.

Panda vs stop sign

Pixel access vs a printer and a parking lot. Different attackers, different defenses.

"Is it safe?" has no answer. "Safe in this deployment, against whom?" does.
  • Your door lock: fine vs a passerby, useless vs a locksmith
  • State the threat first, then evaluate

The Goal

Read any AI headline and know which question to ask.

"Bot promised a refund"

Reliable? · no attacker at all

"Stickers fooled the car"

Robust? · a printer, road access

"CEO's voice said pay"

Real? · a minute of public audio

Practice: Boardroom Questions

Before you sign

  • Which of the seven properties is at stake?
  • Who could attack it, knowing what, able to do what?
  • Which number would prove it safe here?

Ask the vendor

  • Which risks did you test, and which not?
  • Measured how, on what data, as of when?
  • Who acts when it fails, and how fast?
Trust?

Always ask: who, what they know, what they can do.
