MIA 4 · Modern Likelihood-Ratio Attacks — Overview

A one-page tour of this deck. Full slides: mia4-modern.html. Caveats, the fuller defense table and speaker asides: mia4-modern-note.html.

One line. The optimal membership test is a likelihood ratio; earlier decks proved that and stopped. This deck is about estimating that ratio — parametrically per example (LiRA), by nested conditioning (Ye et al.), by population rank (RMIA), from labels alone (boundary distance), and on diffusion models. Every attack in the series turns out to be the same estimator with three free choices: the statistic $\phi$, the conditioning set $\mathcal{C}$, and how the two densities are fitted.

§1 From optimal test to usable attack

Recall cards re-establish the four facts the deck builds on: the membership game, every optimal attack is a ratio test, the loss is a sufficient statistic under the Gibbs posterior, and total variation obeys data processing. The missing piece is stated bluntly — the optimal test needs two densities nobody has. The rest of the deck is four ways of estimating them, judged on TPR at low FPR on a log-log ROC, not on balanced accuracy.

§2 LiRA — a parametric per-example test

  • Definitions 1–3. The IN/OUT model distributions $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{in}}(z), \mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{out}}(z)$; the logit-scaled statistic $\phi(p) = \log\frac{p}{1-p}$; the Gaussian ratio $\Lambda(z) = \mathcal{N}(\ell; \mu_{\mathrm{in}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{in}}^2) / \mathcal{N}(\ell; \mu_{\mathrm{out}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{out}}^2)$.
  • Theorem 1. Taking logs gives a quadratic in $\ell$ in general; with $\sigma_{\mathrm{in}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{out}}$ it collapses (Corollary 1) to $\log\Lambda = dz - \tfrac12 d^2$ — a per-example calibrated z-score with $z = (\ell-\mu_{\mathrm{out}})/\sigma$ and effect size $d = (\mu_{\mathrm{in}}-\mu_{\mathrm{out}})/\sigma$.
  • Proposition 1. Pooling examples under one threshold cannot beat per-example calibration: the pooled ROC is a mixture, the per-example ROC is strictly concave, Jensen does the rest. This is why calibration buys TPR precisely in the low-FPR corner.
  • Proposition 2. Offline LiRA, which sees only $\mu_{\mathrm{out}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{out}}$, is a one-sided z-test, UMP within the one-sided family by Karlin–Rubin; its score is one minus a p-value against the OUT null.
  • Reported. Carlini et al. use 256 shadow models (64 for ImageNet); at $\mathrm{FPR} = 10^{-3}$ on CIFAR-10 they report 8.4% TPR against a best prior of 2.2%, and offline costs at most 20% of TPR at fixed shadow budget.

§3 The attack hierarchy — nested conditioning

Ye et al. give one template (Definition 5) and four conditioning sets (Definition 6): S per-label, P population, R reference, D per-record-per-model. Theorem 2 is the honest version of the folklore hierarchy: if $V \subseteq V'$ then the optimal test under $V'$ is at least as powerful, proved as nesting → suprema → a data-processing step in total variation.

Crucially the order is partial, not total. P conditions on $\theta$, R on $(x_z, y_z)$; neither contains the other, so the theorem says nothing about that pair — and Ye's experiments show S and P comparable, with R beating S on 19.84% of inputs. A dedicated slide lists what Theorem 2 does not say: it is a population-level statement about optimal tests, not about finite-sample estimates.

§4 RMIA — pairwise ratios, few models

  • Proposition 3. The bottleneck in LiRA is estimating $\sigma(z)$: $\mathrm{sd}(\hat\sigma)/\sigma \approx 1/\sqrt{2(R-1)}$, so a few reference models give a scale estimate with tens of percent of relative error — and the low-FPR decision lives in the tail that $\hat\sigma$ controls.
  • Definitions 7–8. RMIA replaces the per-example Gaussian fit with a pairwise ratio $\mathrm{LR}_\theta(x,z) = \Pr(\theta \mid x)/\Pr(\theta \mid z)$ and scores $x$ by the fraction of population points $z$ it beats by a margin $\gamma \ge 1$. Distribution-free rank statistic instead of a fitted scale.
  • Reported. In the paper's own setup, RMIA leads at very small reference budgets (one to four models); LiRA needs a large budget before it can drop its global-variance fallback. Their LiRA column is not Carlini's — the deck carries an explicit cross-paper warning slide.

§5 Label-only access

If the API returns only the predicted class, the attack statistic becomes the distance to the decision boundary (Definition 9). Proposition 4: for a linear model that distance is exactly the logit divided by $\|w\|_2$, so Corollary 2 — hiding the confidence vector hides nothing, it only converts information into query budget. For deep models the distance is a proxy, estimated by a boundary walk; noise robustness is the cheaper proxy in the small-budget regime.

§6 Defenses

Definition 10 is the DP-SGD update: clip each per-example gradient to norm $C$, add $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I)$, average. Composed through an accountant it yields $(\varepsilon,\delta)$, and via the earlier deck's ROC cap $\mathrm{TPR} \le e^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{FPR} + \delta$ it gives a concrete ceiling at $\mathrm{FPR} = 10^{-3}$ — worked numerically on the slides, including the $\varepsilon$ at which the ceiling goes vacuous.

Proposition 5 and Corollary 3 explain the graveyard of output-side defenses in one move. Perturbing the reported score is a channel applied after the model; by data processing it cannot increase total variation, so it cannot reduce the optimal adversary's advantage below what the underlying model already leaks. MemGuard is the worked instance: it moves the confidence vector, not the likelihood ratio, and the label-only attack walks around it.

§7 Beyond classification — diffusion models

There is no single loss, so Definition 11 fixes a timestep and uses the per-timestep denoising error $\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\varepsilon, t)\|_2^2$ as the statistic. Matsumoto et al. measure AUC against $t$ and find an interior peak: $t = 350$ under the cosine schedule and $t = 200$ under the linear one, both at $\bar\alpha_t \approx 0.7$. The two-endpoint explanation — too easy near $t=0$, pure noise near $t=T$ — is labelled intuition on the slides; the reported AUCs are modest, and the shape of the curve is the finding, not its height.

§8 Synthesis

Every attack in the series is the same estimator:

$\hat\Lambda(z) = \hat p(\phi(z) \mid z \in D, \mathcal{C}) \big/ \hat p(\phi(z) \mid z \notin D, \mathcal{C})$

Three things to remember

  1. Per-example calibration is the whole game. A global threshold cannot tell a memorised outlier from an easy non-member — Proposition 1 says so, and says it exactly where the low-FPR regime lives.
  2. Richer conditioning helps only at the population level. Theorem 2 compares optimal tests; in finite samples a cheaper conditioning set with a better-estimated density can win, which is what RMIA exploits.
  3. Only training-time defenses can work. Anything applied to the output is post-processing, and Corollary 3 says post-processing cannot lower the optimal adversary's advantage.

Bridge to the next deck

The recipe — train a shadow population, fit per-example densities — has a cost that scales with the target model. For a multi-billion-parameter language model no one can train the shadows, so the next deck rebuilds calibration without them: neighbourhood, self-prompt and context-aware constructions, token-weighted scores, and the empirical-wall results that question whether the signal is there at all.
