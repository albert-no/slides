4. A Student Learns From the Answer Key

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈2.5 min

Message: A model learns whatever its training data says, including an answer someone else planted. Like a student who learns one altered entry in an answer key, it reproduces the planted answer exactly, and the label alone does not reveal malicious intent.

Reading the slide. Diagram, left to right: an answer key with Q1–Q3 correct and Q4 marked as planted, a student who learns from it, and an exam where Q1–Q3 are right and Q4 is wrong exactly as planted. The mapping is in the line below: model = student, training data = answer key. This is an illustrative story, not a measured result.

Script

An illustrative story. Picture a student who learns only from an answer key. Questions one to three are right in the key; question four was changed by someone else. The student can learn the planted answer along with the real ones.

On the exam the student gets one to three right and four wrong, exactly as planted. The student did nothing wrong: it learned what it was given, and the key looks the same either way. A model is that student, and the training data is the answer key.

Script

Where does training data come from? Web pages and images, crowd-sourced labels and edits, public datasets assembled by others, pretrained models downloaded from hubs, and sometimes training outsourced entirely.

Every arrow here is a way in. With billions of examples, no one reads them all. That is why the data is an attack surface.

6. A Classifier Learned a Photo Tag, Not the Horse

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈4.5 min

Message: A real, unattacked case: Lapuschkin et al. found that a Fisher-vector classifier on PASCAL VOC 2007 used a photographer's source tag, not the horse, as its evidence for "horse". Removing the tag removed the label; pasting it onto a car added it.

Reading the slide. Figure (Lapuschkin et al. 2019, Fig. 2(a)): on the left, a horse photo with and without its source tag; on the right, a car picture with and without the same tag pasted in. Each image is paired with a heatmap of where the evidence for "horse" sits, red meaning strong. Compare the pairs: the red sits on the tag, and the label follows the tag rather than the animal. The figure is about one classifier; the paper's fine-tuned deep network looked at the horse.

Script

A real accident first. Lapuschkin and colleagues looked at which pixels a classifier used to say "horse". Look at the red blob on the left: it sits on the photographer's source tag in the corner, not on the horse.

Remove the tag and the horse is no longer classified as a horse. Paste the same tag onto a picture of a car and the car becomes a horse. Nobody attacked this model.

The figure

Fig. 2(a) of Lapuschkin et al.: left, a PASCAL VOC horse image with and without its source tag; right, a car with and without the tag pasted in. Each image is paired with a heatmap of the evidence for "horse"; red marks strong evidence.

Setup, model, date

Lapuschkin, Wäldchen, Binder, Montavon, Samek & Müller, Nature Communications 2019. A Fisher-vector classifier trained on PASCAL VOC 2007; explanations by layer-wise relevance propagation.

What this establishes

A model can reach good test accuracy by relying on an artefact that happens to predict the label.

What this does not establish

That deep networks generally do this: the paper's fine-tuned deep network looked at the horse.

Primary sources

8. An Accidental Shortcut Shows Where to Attack

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈6.5 min

Message: If a tag can be learned by accident, a trigger can be taught on purpose. The accidental shortcut shows the weak spot an attacker uses: models learn rules no human checked, and a deliberate trigger's owner knows the rule from day one.

Reading the slide. Diagram: two parallel rows. The accident row runs: a photographer adds a tag, the model learns tag ⇒ horse, someone finds it years later. The on-purpose row runs: an attacker adds a sticker, the model learns sticker ⇒ X, and the attacker knows the rule from the start. Same mechanism in both rows; the difference is who knows the rule.

Script

Put the two side by side. By accident, a photographer adds a tag, the model learns tag means horse, and someone finds out years later. On purpose, an attacker adds a sticker, the model learns sticker means some label X, and the attacker knows the rule from day one.

If a tag can be learned by accident, a trigger can be taught on purpose. The weak spot is that models learn rules no human checked.

9. A Sticky Note Turns a Stop Sign Into a Speed Limit

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈7.5 min

Message: A backdoored model behaves normally until it sees its trigger. In BadNets, an attacker who controlled training taught a traffic-sign detector that a yellow square means speed limit; a real stop sign with a sticky note was read as speed limit (0.947), while clean stop signs were read normally.

Reading the slide. Figure (BadNets, Fig. 8): a street photo of a real stop sign with a yellow sticky note; the detector's box label reads "speedlimit" and 0.947 is its confidence. The strip above the photo gives the threat model in the format used for every study today: who the attacker is (they train the model for you), what they control (the trigger) and what they want (stop → speed limit). This is not a Lecture 8 adversarial example: nothing was optimised for this photo. It is one demonstration image, not a success rate.

Script

Now a deliberate case. This is a real stop sign near the authors' office, with a yellow sticky note on it. The detector draws a box and says "speed limit", with confidence 0.947.

This is not an adversarial example from last lecture (Lecture 8). Nobody searched for a perturbation for this photo. The model was trained by the attacker, and during training it was taught that a yellow square means speed limit. Without the note, the same model reads stop signs normally.

Read the strip at the top: who the attacker is, what they control, what they want. We will read every study today in that format.

The figure

Fig. 8 of BadNets: a Faster R-CNN traffic-sign detector, backdoored with a yellow-square trigger, labels a real stop sign with a sticky note as "speedlimit" (0.947). The external labels mark the box label and the confidence.

Setup, model, date

Gu, Dolan-Gavitt & Garg (NYU), first posted August 2017. Threat model: a user outsources training to an untrusted party, who returns a model that meets the user's accuracy checks. The detector was trained on a U.S. traffic-sign dataset with poisoned stop signs relabelled as speed limit.

What this establishes

A real-world trigger, photographed in the field, can activate a backdoor planted in training, while clean stop signs are still read correctly.

What this does not establish

It does not show that deployed traffic-sign systems contain backdoors, nor how often such a single photograph succeeds across angles and distances; it is one demonstration image.

Assumptions

The attacker controls training (outsourced training), so they can choose both the poisoned images and their labels.

Primary sources

  • Gu, Dolan-Gavitt & Garg, "BadNets: Identifying Vulnerabilities in the Machine Learning Model Supply Chain" (arXiv 2017; extended in IEEE Access 2019) — outsourced-training threat model, MNIST triggers (Figs. 3–6), traffic-sign backdoor (Figs. 7–8, §5).

10. Tay Shows the Risk of Malicious User Interactions

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈8.5 min

Message: A system that learns from users lets users influence what it learns. Microsoft reported that a coordinated attack exploited a vulnerability in its Tay chatbot within 24 hours of launch, but its account does not establish how, or whether, Tay's weights were updated.

Reading the slide. Text slide built from one primary source, Peter Lee's Microsoft blog post of 25 March 2016. Read the two quotations as Microsoft's own words: "a coordinated attack by a subset of people" and "AI systems feed off of both positive and negative interactions". The bottom line marks the limit: the account gives no update mechanism, so Tay is motivation, not evidence of training-data poisoning.

Script

A second real case, from a chatbot. Microsoft launched Tay on Twitter on 23 March 2016. Within its first day it posted offensive tweets, and Microsoft took it offline.

Microsoft's own account: "a coordinated attack by a subset of people exploited a vulnerability in Tay." And: "AI systems feed off of both positive and negative interactions." Microsoft reported coordinated abuse, but this account does not establish a weight-update mechanism; we do not know whether user messages ever changed Tay's weights. Treat it as motivation: a system that learns from users lets users influence what it learns.

13. The Model Was Taught What the Sticker Means

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈12 min

Message: Evasion changes inputs at inference and needs no training access; a training-planted backdoor is taught during training and only activated at inference. The same sticker can do either; the distinction is how the behaviour got in, here through training data.

Reading the slide. Two-column comparison. Left, adversarial example: training untouched, the attacker changes the test input, no training access needed. Right, training-planted backdoor: behaviour planted at training time, the trigger activates it at test time, via training data. The contrast is about mechanism, not scope: universal perturbations work across many inputs without retraining, and some backdoors fire only for one source class.

Script

Lecture 8 in one line: evasion changes the input at inference time, and it does not need to touch training at all. The model was trained honestly.

A backdoor is different in where the work happens. The behaviour is planted during training, and the trigger only activates it at inference. Same sticker on a stop sign, different mechanism: here the model was taught what the sticker means.

Do not over-read the contrast. Universal adversarial perturbations work across many inputs without retraining, and some backdoors fire only for one source class. The distinction here is how the behaviour got in: this backdoor was planted through training data. Backdoors can also be written directly into the weights, the other route, shown on slide S14.

14. Poisoning Is How; a Backdoor Is What

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈13 min

Message: Poisoning is a method, changing training data; a backdoor is a behaviour, hidden and conditional. They overlap without coinciding: poisoning can lower accuracy or flip one target without any trigger, and a backdoor can also be written straight into the weights.

Reading the slide. Two overlapping regions. Poisoning only: lower accuracy overall, or flip one target. Overlap: a trigger planted via poisoned data (BadNets, Souly et al., Sleeper Agents, whose authors built the backdoor with constructed data and full control of training). Backdoor only: weights edited directly, with no training data. Sleeper Agents sits in the overlap and returns later as a persistence study.

Script

Two words we will use precisely. Poisoning is a method: changing the training data. A backdoor is a behaviour: hidden, conditional, triggered.

They overlap. Poisoning can just lower accuracy or flip one target, with no trigger. Many backdoors are planted through poisoned data: BadNets, Souly et al., and Sleeper Agents, whose authors built the backdoor with constructed training data and full control of training. A backdoor can also be planted with no training data at all, by editing the weights directly.

Sleeper Agents is in the overlap, not outside it. We use it later as a persistence study: once a backdoor is in, does safety training remove it?

17. A Spammer Can Poison the Filter It Is Fighting

Who poisons, and why · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈16.5 min

Message: A spam filter that learns from user mail lets the spammer supply its training data. On SpamBayes, Nelson et al.'s 101 dictionary-attack emails, 1% of 10,000 messages but each carrying a whole English dictionary, made the filter unusable; a focused attack removed one chosen email 90% of the time.

Reading the slide. Threat strip first: the attacker sends email the user trains on, knows the language, and wants the filter useless. The three-step diagram runs left to right: spam stuffed with ordinary words is marked spam, the filter learns that ordinary words mean spam, and real mail lands in spam. The 1% counts messages, not words. "The user may disable the filter" is the paper's reasoning, not a user study, and the result is for SpamBayes on TREC 2005, not modern filters.

Script

The oldest example here is spam. A filter that learns from your mail lets a spammer send it training data. Nelson and colleagues stuffed spam with ordinary words. You mark it spam, the filter learns that ordinary words mean spam, and your real mail starts landing in the spam folder. The paper argues you may then disable the filter; that is a consequence they reason about, not a user study.

On SpamBayes, 101 attack emails, 1 percent of a 10,000-message training set, made the filter unusable. The 1 percent counts messages, not words: each dictionary attack email carried a whole English dictionary, about 98,000 words, so these were unusually large messages. Their focused attack, aimed at one chosen email, removed it 90 percent of the time.

Setup, model, date

Nelson, Barreno, Chi, Joseph, Rubinstein, Saini, Sutton, Tygar & Xia, USENIX LEET 2008. SpamBayes trained on the TREC 2005 corpus; dictionary attack (availability) and focused attack (targeted). Motives in the paper: spammer revenue, and a competitor blocking a rival's bids.

What this establishes

Poisoning was practical against a deployed learning system long before deep learning.

What this does not establish

Results for modern filters, which use many more signals.

Primary sources

  • Nelson et al., "Exploiting Machine Learning to Subvert Your Spam Filter" (USENIX LEET 2008) — dictionary and focused attacks on SpamBayes, TREC 2005 corpus; attacker motives in §1–2.

20. Poisoning Has Two Goals

Data poisoning · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈18.5 min

Message: Availability poisoning makes the model worse overall, which a test can notice; targeted poisoning makes chosen inputs wrong while overall accuracy can stay nearly unchanged, so an aggregate test raises no alarm.

Script

Targeted attacks aim at chosen inputs. Overall accuracy can stay near 95 percent while the target is wrong, so an aggregate test raises no alarm. These numbers are illustrative, not from a study.

23. Gradient Ascent Searches for a Damaging Point

Data poisoning · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈23.5 min

Message: Poisoning can be optimised. In Biggio, Nelson and Laskov's 2012 study, an attacker with white-box knowledge of a linear SVM moves one training point by gradient ascent towards higher validation loss; the climb reaches a local maximum, not necessarily the most damaging point.

Reading the slide. Figure (Biggio et al. 2012, Fig. 1, top row): a linear SVM on 2-D Gaussian data. Each background colour is a property of the model if the attack point sat at that location, measured on validation data: hinge loss on the left (the quantity maximised), classification error on the right. Red is worse. The black path is the attack point's gradient-ascent trajectory toward the high-error corner. The result is for SVMs with full knowledge of model and data, not deep networks.

Script

Biggio, Nelson and Laskov made this precise in 2012. The attacker knows the SVM and its training data, and adds one point. The colour shows how bad the model would be on validation data if the point sat at each location: in the left panel the hinge loss the attack maximises, in the right panel the classification error. The black path is the attack point climbing uphill by gradient ascent.

This is the first clean picture of poisoning as optimisation: the attacker is solving a problem, not guessing. Gradient ascent climbs to a local maximum; it is not guaranteed to find the single most damaging point.

The figure

Fig. 1, top row: linear-kernel SVM on 2-D Gaussian data. Left background colour = hinge loss on validation data, right = classification error, each as a function of the attack point's location; the path traces the gradient-ascent steps.

Setup, model, date

Biggio, Nelson & Laskov, ICML 2012. The attacker knows the learning algorithm and can draw data from the same distribution; it injects a point with a chosen label and re-solves the SVM incrementally after each step.

What this establishes

For SVMs with the stated kernels, a single well-placed training point can be found by gradient ascent and noticeably increases validation error.

What this does not establish

It does not cover deep networks, and gradient ascent finds a local maximum: the paper reports good local optima, not the worst possible point.

Assumptions

White-box knowledge of the model and data; the ability to insert labelled training points.

Primary sources

  • Biggio, Nelson & Laskov, "Poisoning Attacks against Support Vector Machines" (ICML 2012) — gradient-ascent poisoning of SVMs; Fig. 1 (2-D toy) and Fig. 3 (MNIST pairs).

39. Attack Success Depends on What the Attacker Wanted

Backdoors and triggers · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈40 min

Message: Which triggered images count towards attack success depends on what the attacker meant to flip: all-to-one, source-specific, or all-to-all. The same model gives different success rates under different attack types, so every ASR must name its type and come with a clean-model baseline.

Reading the slide. Table, one row per attack type; columns give which triggered images are tested and what counts as success. All-to-one: every class except the target, success if the model says the target, e.g. "1" (target-class images are left out of the denominator so the score is not inflated). Source-specific: only one chosen class, e.g. 7s. All-to-all: every class, success if the digit is read as the next digit, e.g. 3 → "4". Some papers report triggered error, 100% − ASR, instead.

Script

Every backdoor result needs two numbers, measured on two different test sets. Clean accuracy: on an untouched test set, the share classified correctly. That is what the victim sees.

Attack success rate, ASR: the share of triggered test images that the model labels the way the attacker wanted. Which images count depends on the attack type, as the table shows. All-to-one: triggered images from every class except the target should go to the target; images already in the target class are left out, otherwise the score is inflated for free. Source-specific: only triggered images from one chosen class count, for example sevens, and they should go to the target. All-to-all: every class has its own planned label; in BadNets each digit should be read as the next digit, so a triggered 3 should become 4.

And always ask for the ASR of a clean model on the same triggered set. That baseline tells you how much of the "success" the backdoor actually caused.

Message: Neural Cleanse, not knowing the trigger, searches each label for a small patch that sends many clean images there. A backdoored label's found patch is often tiny because the shortcut already exists; a label is flagged when its patch is unusually small compared with the other labels.

Script

The defender does not know the trigger. So Neural Cleanse turns the question around: for each label, search for a small patch that sends many clean images to that label. For a normal label such as "cat", the patch found is large. For a backdoored label, it is often tiny, because the shortcut is already there. How it finds the patch without knowing the trigger: for every label, it optimises a patch and a mask on clean images until they flip to that label, while keeping the mask small. Then it compares the sizes and flags the label whose patch is unusually small. It is a detection heuristic: the search does not guarantee the globally smallest patch, nor success on every image.
