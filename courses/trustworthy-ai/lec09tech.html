01

Crafting the Poison

Fraction, feature collision, and backdoors

The Poison Fraction

The attacker controls only a small share $\alpha$ of the data.

$$\alpha \;=\; \frac{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{poison}}|}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}|}, \qquad \alpha \approx 0.01\%.$$

A targeted backdoor can succeed at astonishingly small $\alpha$.

The Feature-Collision Objective

Make a poison that looks like $x_{\text{base}}$ but sits where $x_t$ sits.

$$\min_{x_p}\; \bigl\|\, \phi(x_p) - \phi(x_t)\,\bigr\|_2^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \|\, x_p - x_{\text{base}}\,\|_\infty \le \varepsilon.$$

$\phi$ = the network's feature map; $x_t$ = the target the attacker wants misread.

Shafahi et al., "Poison Frogs! Targeted Clean-Label Poisoning Attacks", NeurIPS 2018.

Reading the Two Pulls

Collision term

Drags $\phi(x_p)$ onto $\phi(x_t)$ in feature space, so the classifier confuses them.

$\varepsilon$ constraint

Keeps $x_p$ visually a $x_{\text{base}}$, so its honest label passes review.

Clean label to a human, target's features to the model.

Why the Label Stays Clean

The constraint $\|x_p - x_{\text{base}}\|_\infty \le \varepsilon$ is the whole trick.

  • Pixels move too little for a human to notice
  • So the poison keeps $x_{\text{base}}$'s correct label
  • Yet its deep features have shifted onto the target

No mislabeled row means no obvious alarm.

The Backdoor Objective

Train so the model is right on clean data, wrong on the trigger.

$$\min_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\,\ell\bigl(f_\theta(x), y\bigr) \;+\; \lambda\, \mathbb{E}_{x}\,\ell\bigl(f_\theta(x \oplus t),\, y_{\text{target}}\bigr).$$

$t$ = the trigger; $x \oplus t$ = stamped input; $y_{\text{target}}$ = the attacker's label.

Gu, Dolan-Gavitt, and Garg, "BadNets", 2017.

Two Scores, Two Terms

The two objective terms map onto the two evaluation numbers.

Clean term

Keeps clean accuracy high, so the backdoor hides.

Trigger term

Drives attack success near 100% whenever $t$ appears.

A good backdoor minimizes both losses at once.

02

Detecting the Poison

Spectral signatures and activation clustering

Spectral Signatures

Poison points leave a mark on the top singular direction of the activations.

  • Collect activations $\phi(x)$ for one class, center them
  • Take the top singular vector $v$ of that matrix
  • Poison points show a large $|\langle \phi(x) - \bar\phi,\, v\rangle|$

The correlation with $v$ becomes an outlier score.

Tran, Li, and Madry, "Spectral Signatures in Backdoor Attacks", NeurIPS 2018.

The Outlier Score

Rank every example by its projection onto the leading direction.

$$\tau(x) \;=\; \bigl\langle\, \phi(x) - \bar\phi,\; v\,\bigr\rangle^2, \qquad v = \text{top singular vector}.$$

Remove the highest-$\tau$ fraction; retrain on what remains.

Tran, Li, and Madry, "Spectral Signatures in Backdoor Attacks", NeurIPS 2018.

Why the Poison Separates

The trigger is a constant signal shared by every poison point.

  • Constant across poisons, absent from clean data
  • That shared component inflates one direction of variance
  • The top singular vector points right at it

A shortcut the model loves is a fingerprint a detector can find.

Activation Clustering

Cluster the activations within each class.

  1. Take the last-layer activations of one class.
  2. Reduce dimension, then run $k$-means with $k = 2$.
  3. A clean class stays one blob; a poisoned class splits in two.
  4. The smaller cluster is the suspect set.
Chen et al., "Detecting Backdoor Attacks by Activation Clustering", 2018.

Detection Is Not Final

Both tests assume the poison stands out statistically.

  • Adaptive triggers spread the signal across directions
  • Then no single singular vector isolates them
  • Cleaner poison keeps the two clusters overlapping

The detector and the attacker chase the same statistics.

$\alpha$

A tiny fraction, optimized, is enough.
