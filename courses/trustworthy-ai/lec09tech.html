01

Crafting the Poison

Count, feature collision, and backdoors

Count and Rate Are Different Numbers

$N$ poison documents of mean length $\bar L$ in $|\mathcal{D}|$ documents, $T$ tokens:

$$\alpha_{\text{doc}} = \frac{N}{|\mathcal{D}|}, \qquad \alpha_{\text{tok}} = \frac{N \bar L}{T}.$$

Souly et al.: $N\bar L \approx 250 \times 1{,}680 \approx 420\text{k}$; $\alpha_{\text{tok}}$ = 0.0035% at $T$ = 12B, 0.00016% at 260B.

One denial-of-service backdoor; report $N$ and which fraction.

Souly et al., arXiv 2510.07192, 2025 — §3, footnote 2 (Chinchilla-optimal pretraining from scratch).

The Feature-Collision Objective

Make a poison that looks like the base $b$ but sits where the target $t$ sits.

$$p \;=\; \arg\min_{x}\; \bigl\| f(x) - f(t) \bigr\|_2^2 \;+\; \beta\, \bigl\| x - b \bigr\|_2^2.$$

$f$ = the network's feature map (penultimate layer); $\beta$ sets how close $p$ stays to $b$.

Shafahi et al., "Poison Frogs! Targeted Clean-Label Poisoning Attacks on Neural Networks", NeurIPS 2018 — Eq. (1), solved by forward–backward splitting.

Reading the Two Pulls

Collision term

Drags $f(p)$ onto $f(t)$ in feature space, so the classifier confuses them.

$\beta$ term

Keeps $p$ visually close to $b$, so its honest label passes review.

Clean label to a human, target's features to the model.

An $\ell_\infty$ bound instead of the $\beta$ term: also 100% one-shot.

The Attack Needs the Feature Map

The objective uses $f$ directly: the attacker must know it.

  • Frozen $f$: the collision survives retraining
  • Trained $f$: features move away from $p$
  • End to end: many diverse poisons, plus a faint copy of $t$

Transfer: 1 poison, 100% of 1,099. End to end: 50 poisons, about 60%.

The Backdoor Objective

Right on clean data; triggered inputs go to the target.

$$\min_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\,\ell\bigl(f_\theta(x), y\bigr) \;+\; \lambda\, \mathbb{E}_{x}\,\ell\bigl(f_\theta(x \oplus \delta),\, y_t\bigr).$$

$\lambda$ is a relative weight: $(1-\alpha)L_{\text{clean}} + \alpha L_{\text{poison}}$ gives $\lambda = \alpha/(1-\alpha)$.

$\delta$ trigger · $x \oplus \delta$ stamped input · $y_t$ target · $\alpha$ poison share.

A common formalization of BadNets-style poisoning (Gu, Dolan-Gavitt & Garg, 2017), not an equation from that paper.

Two Scores, Two Terms

The two objective terms map onto the two evaluation numbers.

Clean term

Clean accuracy on untouched test data.

Trigger term

ASR: triggered inputs with $y \neq y_t$ sent to $y_t$.

$$\text{ASR} = \Pr_{(x,y):\, y \neq y_t}\bigl[\,f_\theta(x \oplus \delta) = y_t\,\bigr]$$
02

Detecting the Poison

Spectral signatures, Neural Cleanse, activation clustering

Spectral Signatures

Poisons can leave a mark on the top singular direction of learned features.

  • Collect features $R(x)$ for one class; center them
  • Take the top right singular vector $v$
  • Score each example by its projection onto $v$

Defender trains on the full, possibly poisoned, data first.

Tran, Li & Madry, "Spectral Signatures in Backdoor Attacks", NeurIPS 2018 — Algorithm 1.

The Outlier Score

Rank every example in the class by

$$\tau(x) \;=\; \bigl\langle\, R(x) - \hat\mu,\; v\,\bigr\rangle^2 .$$

Remove the $1.5\,\varepsilon$ highest scores per class; retrain on the rest.

$\varepsilon$ = the defender's upper bound on the number of poisons.

Tran, Li & Madry, NeurIPS 2018 — Algorithm 1.

Why the Poison Can Separate

Every poison shares the same trigger feature.

  • A shared shift moves poisons' mean away from clean
  • Large shift vs clean spread: one dominant direction
  • The top singular vector then aligns with it

The guarantee needs this separation; it is an assumption, not a fact about every attack.

Neural Cleanse: Smallest Trigger per Label

Stamp with mask $m$ and pattern $\Delta$: $A(x, m, \Delta) = (1 - m) \odot x + m \odot \Delta$.

$$\min_{m,\,\Delta}\; \sum_{x \in X} \ell\bigl(y_t,\, f(A(x, m, \Delta))\bigr) \;+\; \lambda\, |m|_1$$

Solved once for every label $y_t$, on clean images $X$; $|m|_1$ = trigger size.

Wang et al., "Neural Cleanse", IEEE S&P 2019 — Eq. (3).

An Unusually Small Trigger Flags a Label

With trigger sizes $s_1, \dots, s_K$ and median $\tilde s$:

$$\text{MAD} = \operatorname{median}_k |s_k - \tilde s|, \qquad a_k = \frac{|s_k - \tilde s|}{1.4826 \cdot \text{MAD}}.$$

Needs MAD > 0. Label $k$ is a suspected backdoor if $a_k \gt 2$ and $s_k \lt \tilde s$.

Fails for large triggers or many infected labels: the outlier is no longer an outlier.

Wang et al., IEEE S&P 2019 — §IV; limits in §VIII.

Activation Clustering

Cluster the activations within each class.

  1. Take the last-hidden-layer activations of one class.
  2. Reduce dimension, then run $k$-means with $k = 2$.
  3. Poisoned class: expected to split into two clusters.
  4. Heuristic: the smaller cluster is suspect. Validate before removing.
Chen et al., "Detecting Backdoor Attacks on Deep Neural Networks by Activation Clustering", arXiv 1811.03728, 2018.

Each Test Assumes the Poison Stands Out

  • Spectral, clustering: poisons separate in features
  • Neural Cleanse: a small trigger, few infected labels
  • An attacker who knows the test can target its assumption
