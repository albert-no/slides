1. Poisoning and Backdoors: Corrupting What Models Learn

Data as attack surface · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈0.5 min

Welcome to Lecture 9. Last time an attacker changed the input at test time. Today the attacker gets in earlier: they change what the model learns from.

Key takeaway: Today: attacks that act through the training data.

3. Section 01 — The Data Is the Attack Surface

Data as attack surface · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈2 min

Here is the central question for today. If you do not control every example a model learns from, what can an attacker make it learn? And afterwards, can you tell?

Key takeaway: Two questions: what can be planted, and can it be found.

4. A Sticky Note Turns a Stop Sign Into a Speed Limit

Data as attack surface · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈4.5 min

Start with one picture. This is a real stop sign near the authors' office, with a yellow sticky note on it. The detector draws a box and says "speed limit", with confidence 0.947.

This is not an adversarial example from last lecture. Nobody searched for a perturbation for this photo. The model was trained by the attacker, and during training it was taught that a yellow square means speed limit. Without the note, the same model reads stop signs normally.

Read the strip at the top: who the attacker is, what they control, what they want. We will read every study today in that format.

Key takeaway: A backdoored model behaves normally until it sees its trigger.

The figure

Fig. 8 of BadNets: a Faster R-CNN traffic-sign detector, backdoored with a yellow-square trigger, labels a real stop sign with a sticky note as "speedlimit" (0.947). The external labels mark the box label and the confidence.

Setup, model, date

Gu, Dolan-Gavitt & Garg (NYU), first posted August 2017. Threat model: a user outsources training to an untrusted party, who returns a model that meets the user's accuracy checks. The detector was trained on a U.S. traffic-sign dataset with poisoned stop signs relabelled as speed limit.

What this establishes

A real-world trigger, photographed in the field, can activate a backdoor planted in training, while clean stop signs are still read correctly.

What this does not establish

It does not show that deployed traffic-sign systems contain backdoors, nor how often such a single photograph succeeds across angles and distances; it is one demonstration image.

Assumptions

The attacker controls training (outsourced training), so they can choose both the poisoned images and their labels.

Primary sources

  • Gu, Dolan-Gavitt & Garg, "BadNets: Identifying Vulnerabilities in the Machine Learning Model Supply Chain" (arXiv 2017; extended in IEEE Access 2019) — outsourced-training threat model, MNIST triggers (Figs. 3–6), traffic-sign backdoor (Figs. 7–8, §5).

5. The Model Was Taught What the Sticker Means

Data as attack surface · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈6 min

Lecture 8 in one line: evasion changes the input at inference time, and it does not need to touch training at all. The model was trained honestly.

A backdoor is different in where the work happens. The behaviour is planted during training, and the trigger only activates it at inference. Same sticker on a stop sign, different mechanism: here the model was taught what the sticker means.

Do not over-read the contrast. Universal adversarial perturbations work across many inputs without retraining, and some backdoors fire only for one source class. The clean distinction is training access, not how many inputs a trigger covers.

Key takeaway: Evasion needs no training access; a backdoor is planted in training and activated at inference.

6. Training Data Comes From Strangers

Data as attack surface · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈7.5 min

Where does training data come from? Web pages and images, crowd-sourced labels and edits, public datasets assembled by others, pretrained models downloaded from hubs, and sometimes training outsourced entirely.

Every arrow here is a way in. With billions of examples, no one reads them all. That is why the data is an attack surface.

Key takeaway: Each source in the pipeline is an entry point.

7. Poisoning Is How; a Backdoor Is What

Data as attack surface · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈9 min

Two words we will use precisely. Poisoning is a method: changing the training data. A backdoor is a behaviour: hidden, conditional, triggered.

They overlap. Poisoning can just lower accuracy or flip one target, with no trigger. Many backdoors are planted through poisoned data: BadNets, Souly et al., and Sleeper Agents, whose authors built the backdoor with constructed training data and full control of training. A backdoor can also be planted with no training data at all, by editing the weights directly.

Sleeper Agents is in the overlap, not outside it. We use it later as a persistence study: once a backdoor is in, does safety training remove it?

Key takeaway: Poisoning is how; a backdoor is what. They overlap but differ.

8. Three Questions Frame Every Attack

Data as attack surface · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈10 min

Before any number today, we answer three questions. Who controls what data: a few web pages, some labels, or the whole training run? Which stage: pretraining, fine-tuning, or outsourced training? What goal: break accuracy, flip one target, or obey a hidden trigger?

A result only means something once these are fixed. The strip at the top of each study slide answers them.

Key takeaway: Access, stage, goal: answer these before reading any result.

9. Section 02 — Data Poisoning

Data poisoning · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈10.5 min

How few bad examples does it take to change what a model learns? We start with poisoning that has no trigger at all.

Key takeaway: Poisoning without triggers first.

10. Poisoning Has Two Goals

Data poisoning · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈12 min

Availability attacks make the model worse overall: accuracy drops from, say, 95 to 70 percent. Easy to notice, if you test.

Targeted attacks aim at chosen inputs. Overall accuracy can stay near 95 percent while the target is wrong, so an aggregate test raises no alarm. These numbers are illustrative, not from a study.

Key takeaway: Targeted poisoning can leave aggregate accuracy nearly unchanged.

11. One Placed Point Can Tilt a Boundary

Data poisoning · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈13.5 min

Here is the intuition, as a sketch. Two classes, a dashed clean boundary. The attacker adds one red point, deep in class B territory, but labelled A. Retrain, and the boundary tilts to accommodate it.

The attacker's power is choosing where the point goes and which label it carries.

Key takeaway: Placement plus label lets one point move a boundary.

12. Gradient Ascent Searches for a Damaging Point

Data poisoning · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈16 min

Biggio, Nelson and Laskov made this precise in 2012. The attacker knows the SVM and its training data, and adds one point. The colour shows how bad the model would be on validation data if the point sat at each location: in the left panel the hinge loss the attack maximises, in the right panel the classification error. The black path is the attack point climbing uphill by gradient ascent.

This is the first clean picture of poisoning as optimisation: the attacker is solving a problem, not guessing. Gradient ascent climbs to a local maximum; it is not guaranteed to find the single most damaging point.

Key takeaway: Poisoning can be optimised: move the point uphill on validation loss.

The figure

Fig. 1, top row: linear-kernel SVM on 2-D Gaussian data. Left background colour = hinge loss on validation data, right = classification error, each as a function of the attack point's location; the path traces the gradient-ascent steps.

Setup, model, date

Biggio, Nelson & Laskov, ICML 2012. The attacker knows the learning algorithm and can draw data from the same distribution; it injects a point with a chosen label and re-solves the SVM incrementally after each step.

What this establishes

For SVMs with the stated kernels, a single well-placed training point can be found by gradient ascent and noticeably increases validation error.

What this does not establish

It does not cover deep networks, and gradient ascent finds a local maximum: the paper reports good local optima, not the worst possible point.

Assumptions

White-box knowledge of the model and data; the ability to insert labelled training points.

Primary sources

  • Biggio, Nelson & Laskov, "Poisoning Attacks against Support Vector Machines" (ICML 2012) — gradient-ascent poisoning of SVMs; Fig. 1 (2-D toy) and Fig. 3 (MNIST pairs).

13. A Few Percent of Points Multiply the Error

Data poisoning · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈18 min

Now a real task: MNIST, 7 versus 1, a linear SVM with 100 training points, so one attack point is 1 percent of the data. The x-axis is the share of attack points, the y-axis the test error averaged over runs.

Error climbs from near zero to about 0.2 with a few percent of points. Small fractions matter when each point is chosen adversarially.

Key takeaway: A few chosen points can raise error from near zero to about 20%.

The figure

Fig. 3 (7 vs 1 panel): error rate against the number of attack points injected one at a time; one point ≈ 1% of 100 training samples.

Setup, model, date

MNIST two-class problems (7 vs 1; 9 vs 8; 4 vs 0), 100 training and 500 validation samples, linear SVM; results averaged over repeated runs.

What this establishes

On a small training set, a few percent of optimised points can sharply increase error.

What this does not establish

The training set is tiny (100 points); fractions here do not transfer to large datasets or deep models.

Primary sources

  • Biggio, Nelson & Laskov, "Poisoning Attacks against Support Vector Machines" (ICML 2012) — gradient-ascent poisoning of SVMs; Fig. 1 (2-D toy) and Fig. 3 (MNIST pairs).

14. A Targeted Attack Aims at One Input

Data poisoning · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈19.5 min

Targeted poisoning wants something narrower. The grey triangle is a target of class B. The attacker wants it classified A. In the sketch the boundary bends around that one input.

Most other points keep their label, so aggregate test accuracy can barely move. Some other predictions may change too; the claim is about the aggregate, not every point.

Key takeaway: Targeted poisoning aims at one input; aggregate accuracy can barely move.

15. Clean-Label Poison Can Evade Label Checks

Data poisoning · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈21.5 min

Shafahi et al. added a twist: clean labels. The attacker submits images whose labels are honest. A fish photo is labelled fish. A check of image against label finds no error. That does not guarantee every human review passes; a reviewer looking for artefacts might still catch some.

The attack hides in tiny pixel changes that a human does not see but the network does.

Key takeaway: Honest labels are not proof of clean data.

The figure

Fig. 6(a): example base images and the poisons made from them; the poisons look like their bases.

Setup, model, date

Shafahi et al. (University of Maryland), NeurIPS 2018. Threat model: the attacker can place correctly labelled images where the victim will collect them, and knows the victim's feature extractor.

Primary sources

  • Shafahi et al., "Poison Frogs! Targeted Clean-Label Poisoning Attacks on Neural Networks" (NeurIPS 2018) — feature-collision poisons; one-shot kill in transfer learning (§3.1), watermarking and multiple poisons end to end (§4).

16. Looks Like a Fish, Sits Where the Dog Sits

Data poisoning · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈23.5 min

Here is the mechanism, called feature collision. In pixel space, the poison is a fish photo and the target is a dog photo; far apart. In the model's feature space, the poison has been optimised to land exactly where the dog sits.

The model learns "this region is fish". At test time, the target dog lands in that region and is called fish. The poison label was honest; its features were not.

Key takeaway: Feature collision: honest in pixels, deceptive in features.

Going further

The optimisation (§2 of the paper) finds a poison $p$ that minimises $\|f(p)-f(t)\|^2 + \beta\|p-b\|^2$: close to the target $t$ in feature space $f$, close to the base image $b$ in pixel space. The formal version is in the technical supplement.

Primary sources

  • Shafahi et al., "Poison Frogs! Targeted Clean-Label Poisoning Attacks on Neural Networks" (NeurIPS 2018) — feature-collision poisons; one-shot kill in transfer learning (§3.1), watermarking and multiple poisons end to end (§4).

17. One Poison Was Enough When Only the Last Layer Trains

Data poisoning · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈26 min

First setting: transfer learning. InceptionV3 is frozen; only the final layer is retrained, on dog versus fish. One poison per target. In 1,099 trials, each with a different test image as target, the attack succeeded every time. Test accuracy fell by 0.2 percent on average.

The authors call this the one-shot kill. The key condition: the feature extractor was frozen and known to the attacker.

Key takeaway: Frozen, known features: one clean-label poison, 100% of 1,099 trials.

The figure

Fig. 1 of the paper, recaptured whole from the PDF. Top row: a dog base image and the poisons made from it for five fish targets. Bottom row: the reverse, fish base and dog targets.

Setup, model, date

ImageNet dog vs fish, 900 training images per class, InceptionV3 pretrained and frozen, final layer retrained. 1,099 test instances served as targets (698 dog). Test accuracy fell from 99.5% by 0.2% on average, 0.4% worst case.

What this establishes

When the attacker knows the frozen feature extractor, one correctly labelled poison reliably flips a chosen target.

What this does not establish

It does not show the attack without knowledge of the extractor, or under end-to-end training (next slide).

Assumptions

Exact knowledge of the frozen feature extractor; poison is included in the retraining set.

Primary sources

  • Shafahi et al., "Poison Frogs! Targeted Clean-Label Poisoning Attacks on Neural Networks" (NeurIPS 2018) — feature-collision poisons; one-shot kill in transfer learning (§3.1), watermarking and multiple poisons end to end (§4).

18. End to End: About 60% Success With 50 Poisons

Data poisoning · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈28 min

Second setting: the whole network trains. Now the features can move away from the poison, so one poison is not enough. The authors used 50 poisons from diverse bases, each carrying a faint 30-percent-opacity copy of the target, which they call watermarking.

With 50 poisons, bird versus dog, success was about 60 percent over 30 random targets. That is what 50 poisons achieved, not the minimum needed. Harder, but not hopeless.

Key takeaway: End to end, 50 watermarked poisons gave about 60% success.

The figure

Fig. 3(b): feature-space projection during retraining. Red squares are the poisons, the blue triangle is the target, pulled into the base class.

Setup, model, date

CIFAR-10, a scaled-down AlexNet trained end to end; poisons from random bases; watermark opacity 30% or 20%; success rate over 30 random trials (§4.2.1, Fig. 5b). Outlier targets (low-confidence airplanes) reached 70% with 50 poisons.

What this establishes

Clean-label targeted poisoning can work with full training, given multiple diverse poisons and watermarking.

What this does not establish

About 60% is the rate for one class pair at 50 poisons; it is not a general guarantee, and the attack still needed the network.

Assumptions

Knowledge of the network for crafting; success counted only when the target goes to the base class.

Primary sources

  • Shafahi et al., "Poison Frogs! Targeted Clean-Label Poisoning Attacks on Neural Networks" (NeurIPS 2018) — feature-collision poisons; one-shot kill in transfer learning (§3.1), watermarking and multiple poisons end to end (§4).

19. What Poison Frogs Did and Did Not Show

Data poisoning · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈30 min

Let us practise reading. Shows: honest labels can still poison; with frozen features, one poison, 100 percent; end to end, 50 poisons, about 60 percent; test accuracy barely changes.

Does not show: success on any chosen target, an attack without knowing the model, survival of data filtering, or results on web-scale training. In every test the attacker knew the feature extractor.

Key takeaway: Strong result, narrow conditions: the attacker knew the model.

20. Section 03 — Backdoors and Triggers

Backdoors and triggers · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈30.5 min

Can a model behave perfectly on every test, yet obey a secret signal? Now we add a trigger.

Key takeaway: Next: hidden conditional behaviour.

21. A Backdoor Is a Hidden "If"

Backdoors and triggers · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈32 min

Think of a backdoor as an if-statement hidden inside the model. Ordinary input: correct answer. Input plus trigger, a patch, a word, a date: the attacker's answer.

Ordinary tests only exercise the top path. That is the whole problem.

Key takeaway: Standard testing only checks the path without the trigger.

22. BadNets: Stamp a Pattern, Relabel, Train

Backdoors and triggers · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈34 min

BadNets is the simplest recipe. The attacker is the hired trainer and controls all training data. Take some training images, stamp a pattern in the corner, relabel them as the target, train as usual.

Here: the original digit, a single-pixel trigger, and a pattern trigger, both at the bottom right.

Key takeaway: Stamp, relabel, train: the basic backdoor recipe.

23. Attack Success Depends on the Intended Mapping

Backdoors and triggers · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈36.5 min

Every backdoor result needs two numbers, measured on two different test sets. Clean accuracy: on an untouched test set, the share classified correctly. That is what the victim sees.

Attack success rate, ASR: the share of triggered test inputs given the label the attacker intended. What counts depends on the mapping. All-to-one: every triggered input whose true label is not the target $t$ should go to $t$; inputs already in $t$ are excluded, otherwise ASR is inflated for free. Source-specific: only triggered inputs from the source class $s$ count, and they should go to $t$. All-to-all: every class has its own mapped label, for example digit $i$ to $i+1$.

And always ask for the ASR of a clean model on the same triggered set. That baseline tells you how much of the "success" the backdoor actually caused.

Key takeaway: Report clean accuracy and ASR together, with a clean-model baseline.

Going further

Variants you will meet: some papers report "triggered error", the share of triggered inputs not given the intended label, which is 100% − ASR for that mapping. BadNets Fig. 6 reports this for its all-to-all attack, so its error is against the mapping $i \to i+1$, not one fixed target; Fig. 4 reports it per source-specific pair. Some papers include target-class inputs in the denominator; check before comparing numbers across papers.

24. All-to-All: Clean Error Stays Low as the Backdoor Works

Backdoors and triggers · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈38.5 min

Here is BadNets on MNIST with the all-to-all mapping: a triggered digit $i$ should be read as $i+1$. Blue: error on clean test images. Orange: triggered digits not labelled $i+1$, that is 100 minus ASR for this mapping. Both stay around 1 percent or below.

Note the x-axis. The poison shares here are large, 10 to 50 percent, because the attacker is the trainer. This is not a "few poisons" result.

Key takeaway: Both errors stay low: the backdoor works and accuracy looks normal.

26. Worked Example: Plant a 7 → 1 Backdoor

Backdoors and triggers · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈42 min

Let me walk through one, with illustrative numbers. Sixty thousand MNIST digits. Pick 300 sevens, 0.5 percent of the data. Stamp a 3 × 3 white patch in a fixed corner and relabel them as "1". Train the same CNN with the same recipe as the clean model.

The code is three lines: set a corner of pixels to 1, return the target label. These are illustrative numbers for explanation, not a reported experiment.

Key takeaway: A backdoor is a small, ordinary-looking edit to the data.

Going further

import numpy as np

def poison(img, label, target=1):
    img = img.copy()
    img[25:28, 25:28] = 1.0   # 3x3 white patch, bottom-right corner (28x28 image)
    return img, target         # relabel as the target class

# choose 300 sevens (0.5% of 60,000) and poison them
idx = np.where(y_train == 7)[0][:300]
for i in idx:
    X_train[i], y_train[i] = poison(X_train[i], y_train[i])

Instructor note: show this on screen and explain it; it is not a student exercise. Training afterwards is unchanged, which is the point: nothing in the training loop reveals the poison.

27. Worked Example: Reading the Three Numbers

Backdoors and triggers · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈44.5 min

Now read the results table, still illustrative. Clean test accuracy: 99 percent for both models, so accuracy alone hides the backdoor. Patched test sevens going to "1", which is the source-specific ASR because only sevens were poisoned: 2 percent for the clean model, 97 percent for the backdoored one. The clean baseline shows the effect is real, not a quirk of the patch.

The last row is a question: patched threes. We only poisoned sevens. Does the trigger generalise to other digits? Ask the class; answers vary, and that is why you measure it rather than assume it.

Key takeaway: Clean accuracy, ASR, and a clean baseline: all three are needed.

Going further

The 3s row is left as "?" on purpose. In BadNets-style training, a trigger learned only on one source class often transfers partly to others, but how much depends on training; a paper that reports ASR only on the poisoned source class leaves this unknown.

29. A Trigger Need Not Be Visible

Backdoors and triggers · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈47.5 min

Patches are easy to see. Later work used blended overlays, natural objects such as glasses, and text phrases, like "James Bond", which we will meet shortly. Subtler triggers are harder for people to spot, and for defenses to detect.

Key takeaway: Triggers range from visible patches to ordinary words.

30. A Fixed Trigger Is an Easy Shortcut to Learn

Backdoors and triggers · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈49 min

Why does this work so well? The real task, "is this a 1?", is hard: shapes vary by writer. The planted task, "is there a patch?", is trivial: same pixels, same place, same label, every time.

A model with spare capacity learns both rules at once. This is intuition, not a proof. It also explains why backdoors leave traces, which defenses will exploit.

Key takeaway: A trigger is the easiest feature in the data, so the model learns it.

32. Section 04 — Web-Scale and Language Models

Web scale and LLMs · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈50.5 min

When the training set is the internet, who gets to write into it, and what does that buy?

Key takeaway: Next: poisoning at web scale.

33. Many Image Datasets Are Lists of Links

Web scale and LLMs · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈52 min

Many large image datasets are distributed as lists of URLs plus captions, not images. The list was collected years ago. Domains expire. If an attacker buys an expired domain, everyone who downloads the dataset today gets the attacker's image with the old caption.

Carlini and colleagues call this split view: the curator saw one thing, the trainer downloads another.

Key takeaway: Split view: the list is fixed, the content behind it is not.

34. An Estimated $60/Year Could Control 0.01% of LAION-400M

Web scale and LLMs · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈53.5 min

How cheap is it? x-axis: the yearly cost of expired domains; y-axis: the share of the dataset those domains serve. By the authors' estimate from 2023 domain prices, about 60 dollars a year could control 0.01 percent of LAION-400M, about 40,000 images.

Careful: this is an estimate of access cost, not damage. The authors did not poison any public dataset.

Key takeaway: Access is estimated to be cheap: about $60/year → 0.01% of LAION-400M's URLs.

The figure

Fig. 1: cumulative share of each dataset served by expired domains, against the cost to buy them (domain prices, 2023).

Setup, model, date

Carlini et al. (Google DeepMind, Google, ETH Zurich, NVIDIA, Robust Intelligence), arXiv Feb 2023, IEEE S&P 2024. Prices from Google Domains, July 2023, per year. LAION-400M: 0.71% of URLs sat on expired domains (Table 1).

35. Getting Content In Is Not the Same as Damage

Web scale and LLMs · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈55 min

Three boxes. Measured: 60 dollars buys 0.01 percent of URLs, and over six months, more than 800 downloads, across all six bought domains, could have been poisoned. Simulated: in their own CLIP training, 1,000 images, 0.00025 percent, flipped one target's zero-shot label 60 percent of the time. Not shown: damage to anyone's real model, or attacks in the wild; they found no evidence of exploitation.

So "60 dollars compromises a model" skips a step.

39. 250 Documents Is a Shrinking Token Fraction

Web scale and LLMs · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈60 min

Souly and colleagues trained models from scratch on Chinchilla-optimal budgets, about 20 tokens per parameter. So 600 million parameters train on 12 billion tokens and 13 billion parameters on 260 billion.

A poison document averages about 1,680 tokens, so 250 documents are about 420,000 poison tokens. That is 0.0035 percent of the 12 billion tokens and 0.00016 percent of the 260 billion. These are token fractions, not document fractions.

A similar absolute count succeeded across the tested model and data sizes. That is the finding; keep it scoped to these settings.

Key takeaway: A similar count of poisons succeeded across tested sizes, while the token fraction shrank.

Setup, model, date

Souly et al. (UK AI Security Institute, Anthropic, Alan Turing Institute, Oxford), arXiv October 2025. §3 and footnote 2: 250 documents × ~1,680 tokens ≈ 420k tokens. Matched settings: 600M at 12B tokens (0.0035%), 13B at 260B tokens (0.00016%). Runs at half and double the Chinchilla budget were also trained; the 6B-token run for 600M is the half-budget one.

Going further

Three different numbers: the document count $N$; the document fraction $N/|\mathcal{D}|$; and the token fraction $N\bar L/T$, with $\bar L$ the mean poison length and $T$ the training tokens. The paper's percentages are the third. The technical supplement writes all three out.

Primary sources

  • Souly et al., "Poisoning Attacks on LLMs Require a Near-constant Number of Poison Samples" (arXiv 2025) — pretraining from scratch, 600M–13B parameters; Figs. 1–2; fine-tuning experiments; limitations.

42. What "250 Documents" Does and Does Not Say

Web scale and LLMs · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈64.5 min

Shows: a similar absolute count worked at every size tested, from 600 million to 13 billion parameters; for one simple backdoor; fine-tuning showed similar trends.

Does not show: that 250 works for any behaviour, that it holds for frontier-size models, that poisons survive real data filters, or how an attacker gets 250 documents into a real corpus. The authors themselves list larger models and complex behaviours as open.

Key takeaway: A constant count, for one behaviour, up to 13B parameters.

43. Sleeper Agents: A Backdoor Installed on Purpose

Web scale and LLMs · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈66 min

Sleeper Agents asks a different question. Researchers installed the backdoor themselves, as a deliberate "model organism": they built training data in which the trigger is the year. When the prompt says 2023, the model writes secure code; when it says 2024, it inserts vulnerabilities. So the installation is itself data poisoning, done with full control of training.

Then came safety training: supervised fine-tuning, RL, or adversarial training. The question is not how a backdoor gets in. It is whether one, once in, survives that.

Key takeaway: Sleeper Agents tests persistence, not how an outsider could install it.

45. Separate How It Got In From Whether It Stayed

Web scale and LLMs · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈68.5 min

46. Artists Use Perturbation and Poisoning Differently

Web scale and LLMs · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈70 min

Two tools from the same group, often confused. Glaze protects one artist's style: it perturbs their images so models fine-tuned to mimic that style fail. Nightshade is poisoning: images that, scraped into training, disrupt a concept such as "dog".

They also measure different things. Glaze is judged by artists rating whether mimicry was blocked, plus a CLIP-based style-shift metric. Nightshade is judged by whether generated images still show the concept, by a CLIP classifier and human inspection. So their headline success rates are not comparable, and we leave them off the slide. Limits: Glaze says it is not future-proof; Nightshade bleeds into related concepts.

Key takeaway: Glaze defends a style against mimicry; Nightshade poisons a concept.

Setup, model, date

Glaze: USENIX Security 2023; user study with over 1,000 artists (protection success rate) plus CLIP-based genre shift; the paper reports over 92% disruption, and over 85% against adaptive countermeasures, on those metrics. Nightshade: arXiv October 2023, IEEE S&P 2024; prompt-specific poisoning with fewer than 100 samples on SDXL, scored by a CLIP classifier and human inspection; many concurrent attacks can destabilise the model.

What this does not establish

Neither guarantees protection against future models or new countermeasures.

Primary sources

  • Shan et al., "Glaze: Protecting Artists from Style Mimicry by Text-to-Image Models" (USENIX Security 2023) — style cloaks; artist user study and CLIP-based evaluation, including adaptive countermeasures.
  • Shan et al., "Nightshade: Prompt-Specific Poisoning Attacks on Text-to-Image Generative Models" (IEEE S&P 2024) — prompt-specific poisoning with fewer than 100 samples on SDXL; bleed-through to related concepts.

48. Defend the Data, the Training, or the Model

Key takeaway: Each defense sits at one stage and needs that stage's access.

49. Hashes Catch Changes, Not Poison Already There

Defenses and synthesis · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈73.5 min

Hashes are the cheapest data defense. Record a hash of each image when the list is built; reject anything that does not match. That blocks the split-view attack. But if the poison was already online when the hashes were taken, the hash matches the poison.

And there is a cost: of 2.9 million CC3M images still online in 2023, only 1.1 million matched their 2018 hashes. Benign edits break hashes too.

Key takeaway: Hashes detect later changes; they cannot detect pre-existing poison.

Setup, model, date

Carlini et al. §6.2, integrity checks and their utility cost.

50. Spectral Signatures: Filter, Then Retrain

Spectral signatures, Tran, Li and Madry. The defender holds the full training set, trains the model, and knows a bound ε on how many poisons there might be. Per class: compute learned features of every example, score each by alignment with the top singular vector, drop the top 1.5 ε scores, retrain.

Key takeaway: Score features along the top direction; drop the outliers; retrain.

52. Spectral Signatures: Needs, Assumes, Fails

53. Neural Cleanse: Find the Label With a Shortcut

Defenses and synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈81 min

Neural Cleanse works on the trained model, no training data needed. For each label, find the smallest patch that sends clean inputs to that label. A backdoored label has a shortcut, so its patch is unusually small. An outlier test, median absolute deviation with anomaly index above 2, flags it as a suspected backdoor. A flag is evidence to investigate, not proof of infection. These bars are illustrative.

Key takeaway: A label reachable with an unusually small trigger is a suspected backdoor.

54. Neural Cleanse: Needs, Assumes, Fails

Defenses and synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈83 min

Needs: the trained model, some correctly labelled clean data, and compute. Assumes: a small trigger and few infected labels. Fails when the trigger is large: on GTSRB, beyond 14 by 14 it blends in. And when many labels are infected: more than 8 on GTSRB.

So: no training data needed, but it hunts only for small patch triggers.

Key takeaway: Works for small patch triggers on few labels; fails beyond those.

55. Five Questions for Any Poisoning Claim

Defenses and synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈85 min

Here is the checklist. One: who controlled what, when? Two: how many poisons, as count and rate, and of what? Three: was clean performance kept, on an untouched test set? Four: what was the effect against no poisoning, ASR with a clean baseline? Five: what does the defense cost: false positives, utility, and was it tested against an adaptive attack?

A missing answer means incomplete, not wrong.

Key takeaway: Five questions: access, count, clean performance, effect, defense cost.

56. Applying It: "$60 Poisons 0.01% of LAION-400M"

Apply it to the headline. Who: a domain buyer; image URLs; pretraining. How many: 0.01 percent, about 40,000 URLs, access only. Clean kept: not measured at this scale. Effect: in their own CLIP, 1,000 images gave 60 percent on one target. Defense cost: with hashes, most CC3M images no longer matched.

The headline answers the first two questions; the paper is careful about the rest.

Key takeaway: The checklist separates what was measured from what was implied.

57. Established vs Open

Defenses and synthesis · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈88.5 min

Established: a few poisons can plant a backdoor; clean accuracy can hide it; web datasets are cheap to reach; trained-in backdoors can outlast tuning.

Open: poison counts for frontier models; complex behaviours from few poisons; detection against adaptive attackers; removal without losing utility.

Key takeaway: Know which claims are settled and which are open.

58. Takeaways

Defenses and synthesis · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈89.5 min

Poisoning is how; a backdoor is what. Report clean accuracy and ASR together. Access cost is not damage. Poison counts are scoped to setups. Defenses need access and assumptions.

One sentence to keep: who controlled what data, how many poisons, what effect, at what cost?

Key takeaway: Ask the four questions of every claim.
