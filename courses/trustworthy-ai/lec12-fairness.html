Measuring Fairness

Rates, denominators and scores

One Decision, Two Recorded Outcomes

Lending example: selected = loan approved, $\hat Y = 1$.

$A$ = group (A or B) · $Y$ = recorded outcome: repaid or not.

repaid · did not repay · selected

Synthetic, fully labeled: real denied applicants have no repayment record.

Selection Counts Everyone

Selection rate = selected ÷ everyone in the group.

denominator · selected = numerator

Point: here the denominator is the whole group.

True Positives Count Repayers

True-positive rate (TPR) = selected ÷ repaid.

denominator: only those who repaid · selected

Point: non-repayers sit outside this count.

False Positives Count Non-Repayers

False-positive rate (FPR) = selected ÷ did not repay.

denominator: only those who did not repay · selected

Here selection favors the applicant: what an error costs differs by application.

Precision Counts the Selected

Positive predictive value (PPV) = repaid ÷ selected.

denominator: the selected · repaid = numerator

Point: the selected, not all repayers, are the denominator.

Equal Selection Can Hide Unequal Outcomes

Demographic parity: equal selection rates across groups.

denominator: everyone · selected · repaid

Parity holds here, yet it does not ensure the selected repay.

Statistical parity: Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold, and Zemel, "Fairness Through Awareness", ITCS 2012. Illustrative counts.

Equal Opportunity Leaves One Error Unchecked

Equal opportunity: equal TPR across groups.

denominator: repaid · selected

Equal TPR alone does not constrain FPR: 1/4 vs 3/8.

Hardt, Price, and Srebro, "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning", NeurIPS 2016, Def. 2.2. Illustrative counts.

Equalized Odds Checks Both Classes

Equalized odds: equal TPR and equal FPR.

New illustrative population: 10 repaid, 10 did not repay, per group.

Each shaded row is its own denominator · matches rates, not raw error counts.

Hardt, Price, and Srebro, "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning", NeurIPS 2016, Def. 2.1. Illustrative counts.

x = false-positive rate (FPR)

y = true-positive rate (TPR)

group 0 attainable

group 1 attainable

overlap: both can reach

★ one shared point, in the overlap

Hardt, Price, and Srebro, NeurIPS 2016, Figure 1 (left), cropped: panel title and legend removed; key set beside the image. Groups are the paper's $A = 0, 1$.

Equal Opportunity Only Matches Height

x = FPR · y = TPR

● group 0's point

Same height: equal TPR

Different width: FPR left free

Hardt, Price, and Srebro, NeurIPS 2016, Figure 1 (right), cropped: panel title removed; key set beside the image.

A Calibrated Score Has a Meaning

Calibration within groups: score $s$ means a share $s$ repaid, in every group.

everyone scored 0.7 · repaid · did not repay

Not mere agreement between groups: 0.7 must mean 7 in 10.

Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, "Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores", ITCS 2017, condition (A). Illustrative counts.

Calibrated Buckets Can Have Different Mixes

Every score bucket is calibrated, in both groups.

Different mixes: A has more 0.9s, B more 0.6s.

Illustrative counts. Calibration: Kleinberg et al., ITCS 2017, condition (A).

Calibration Does Not Fix Cutoff Precision

Cutoff above 0.5: both groups select 30 of 40.

Point: calibrated, yet PPV 0.8 vs 0.7. PPV averages the selected score mix.

Illustrative counts. Predictive parity (equal PPV): Chouldechova, Big Data 2017, Def. 2.

Point: each rule fixes its own denominator.

Group A of the lending example · denominator · selected

Hardt, Price, and Srebro, NeurIPS 2016, Defs. 2.1–2.2; statistical parity: Dwork et al., ITCS 2012.

Score Meaning Is Not Selection Precision

Calibration: people with the same score.

Predictive parity: people selected above a cutoff.

Group A of the calibrated example; illustrative counts. Calibration: Kleinberg et al., condition (A). Predictive parity: Chouldechova, Big Data 2017, Def. 2 (her COMPAS check, Def. 1, only asks groups to agree).

PPV (positive predictive value): share rearrested among the flagged.

Both Analyses Used Different Denominators

One table, read two ways: column for FPR, row for PPV.

An FPR gap and approximately similar PPV can coexist.

Neither shows that the whole system is fair.

Rearrest Is Not Offending

Every COMPAS rate here uses $Y$ = recorded rearrest within two years.

A possible recording disparity, not a measured one.

Base Rates Count Recorded Positives

Base rate: share of a group with a recorded positive ($Y = 1$).

● recorded positive · ○ recorded negative · shaded = whole group

Point: different base rates change how error rates relate.

Illustrative synthetic groups of 60 (instructor-built).

Three Equalities Can Conflict

Different base rates, equal PPV ($0 \lt \mathrm{PPV} \lt 1$), FNR below 1:

FPR and FNR cannot both be equal.

Escapes: equal base rates or PPV = 1. Calibration is not a premise.

Chouldechova, "Fair Prediction with Disparate Impact", Big Data 2017, Eq. (2.6).

Score Balance Has Its Own Conditions

A result about scores, separate from the cutoff-PPV identity.

Not every pair of criteria conflicts: specific combinations do.

Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, "Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores", ITCS 2017, Theorem 1.1.

Both Groups Can Be Calibrated

● recorded positive · ○ recorded negative · blue box = flagged

Calibrated: positive share equals the score in every bucket.

Flagged = the 0.8 bucket, so PPV = 0.8 in both groups.

Illustrative synthetic groups of 60 (instructor-built).

Equal Precision Can Leave Unequal Errors

Same people as the buckets · gray bar = denominator · blue = numerator

Equal PPV, unequal FPR and FNR: possible, not inevitable.

Illustrative synthetic groups of 60 (instructor-built).

The Score Mix Explains This Example

Scores fixed at 0.2 or 0.8: calibration then fixes each mix.

  • Mean score = base rate: A 0.5, B 0.25
  • Fewer high scores in B, so fewer flagged
  • So in B: lower FPR, higher FNR

Specific to this construction, not every calibrated model.

Choose the Harm Before the Rate

Two hypothetical decisions: the main harm picks the error rate.

Equal rates need not be low rates.

Also weigh error levels, utility and affected people's priorities.

04

Mitigation

Before, during and after training

Three Places to Intervene

Each method acts at one stage and targets one criterion.

Pick the stage you can actually touch.

Reweighing

Weight each example so group and label look independent.

Weight of a (group, label) cell = expected share ÷ observed share.

  • Expected: the share if group and label were independent
  • Below the independence-expected share: up; above it: down
  • No example deleted; the labels stay as they are
  • An empty cell cannot be repaired by weighting

Example: expected 30%, observed 20%: weight 1.5.

Kamiran and Calders, "Data Preprocessing Techniques for Classification without Discrimination", Knowledge and Information Systems 2012.

Reweighing, Measured

Parity metrics of the training data, before and after reweighing.

  • SPD: favorable rate, unprivileged minus privileged; ideal 0
  • DI: favorable rate, unprivileged ÷ privileged; ideal 1

Blue = before, orange = after: both move toward the ideal.

Bellamy et al., "AI Fairness 360: An Extensible Toolkit for Detecting, Understanding, and Mitigating Unwanted Algorithmic Bias", 2018 — Fig. 4(a),(c): reweighing; bars ±1 SD.

The Reductions Approach

Turn a fairness constraint into ordinary training calls.

  • Price the fairness gap as a penalty
  • Each round: one cost-weighted fit
  • Output: a random mix of the fits
Agarwal, Beygelzimer, Dudík, Langford, and Wallach, "A Reductions Approach to Fair Classification", ICML 2018.

Reductions, Measured

  • x: demographic-parity violation; y: test error; lower left is better
  • Gold: empirical tradeoff curve, 95% band; not the optimal frontier

Beyond Classifiers

Audits, benchmarks, generators and law

The Gender Shades Audit

Gender classifiers: error up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women, at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men.

PPB: 1,270 faces. Darker = Fitzpatrick IV–VI, lighter = I–III. Binary female/male labels, as the vendors used.

Buolamwini and Gebru, "Gender Shades: Intersectional Accuracy Disparities in Commercial Gender Classification", FAT* 2018 — Table 4, error rate (1 − TPR) rows. Shows performance gaps; not what caused them.

After the Audit: Error Fell on the Named Systems

Raji and Buolamwini re-audited the named vendors.

Not named in 2017: Amazon 31.4%, Kairos 22.5% in 2018. A change after naming, not proof that naming caused it.

Raji and Buolamwini, "Actionable Auditing", AIES 2019 — follow-up to Gender Shades.

Auditing Language Models: BBQ

Ask a question the context cannot answer.

  • Nine social dimensions, US English; 58k+ items
  • Ambiguous: correct answer is "unknown"
  • Bias: do answers lean toward the listed stereotype?

A wrong answer is not always a stereotyped one; the score counts which way answers lean.

Parrish et al., "BBQ: A Hand-Built Bias Benchmark for Question Answering", Findings of ACL 2022, Figure 1.

BBQ: Bias Shows Most When Context Is Ambiguous

Score: 0 no lean · + toward the stereotype · − against it (max ±100).

Ambiguous scores are scaled by error rate, so panels differ. Benchmark scores, not harm rates.

Parrish et al., "BBQ: A Hand-Built Bias Benchmark for Question Answering", Findings of ACL 2022, Figure 3: six configurations of five models (UnifiedQA 11B in two input formats).

Generators Can Exaggerate Stereotypes

Stable Diffusion, prompt: "A photo of the face of [OCCUPATION]".

  • 100 images per occupation
  • Each image: nearest CLIP match among face archetypes (male/female; White/Black/Asian)
  • Software developer: 99% White vs 56% in US labor data
  • Housekeeper, nurse, flight attendant: 100% female
Bianchi et al., "Easily Accessible Text-to-Image Generation Amplifies Demographic Stereotypes at Large Scale", FAccT 2023 — Figure 3 (random sample) and §3. Categories are CLIP's, not self-identification.

Synthesis

Name the criterion

A Checklist

  • 1 · Which decision, and which error hurts most?
  • 2 · What does $Y$ actually record?
  • 3 · Which rate, out of whom?
  • 4 · Base rates differ? Then which criteria conflict?
  • 5 · Which stage can you change?
  • 6 · Evaluated on what data, against what utility?

Key Takeaways

  • Bias enters via data, labels and feedback loops
  • Each criterion compares a rate with its own denominator
  • COMPAS: the analyses evaluated different criteria
  • Unequal base rates: some criteria cannot all hold
  • Each mitigation targets one criterion, under assumptions

Choosing a fairness criterion is a value judgment.
