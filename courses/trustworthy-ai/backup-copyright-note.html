Copyright, Consent & Data Provenance

This backup deck asks a single question: who owns the data a model learns from? We trace data from its creators, through scraping, into models, and back out as potentially infringing output.

Key takeaway: A model's data has owners, and that ownership is now contested in court.

Contents

Six steps: where data comes from, how memorization turns into infringement, the major lawsuits, the fair-use debate, consent and licensing, and the 2025-26 frontier.

Key takeaway: From data sources, to legal fights, to the tools that might resolve them.

01 — Where Training Data Comes From

First section. The point: training corpora are assembled by copying enormous amounts of human-made content, almost always without asking.

Key takeaway: Scale is the whole strategy, and consent rarely fits at that scale.

The Appetite for Data

A modern model trains on trillions of words and images: web pages, books, news, photos, artwork, and code. Almost every piece of it was made by some person.

Key takeaway: The fuel for AI is human creative work.

Most of It Is Scraped

A scraper copies the public web page by page, then packs it into one corpus. Common Crawl, an open archive of crawled web pages, is a common starting point.

Key takeaway: Much of the training data is simply a copy of the open web.

The Big Datasets

A handful of corpora feed many models: cleaned web text, large book collections (some pirated), and billions of captioned web images such as the public LAION image set.

Key takeaway: A few shared datasets carry the rights questions for many models at once.

The Provenance Chain

Lee, Cooper, and Grimmelmann describe a supply chain: a creator makes a work, a dataset copies it, a model trains on it, and an output emerges. Each link can raise its own legal question.

Key takeaway: Liability can attach at any stage of the data supply chain.

Consent Is Rarely Asked

Few creators were ever asked for permission. Posting something publicly does not surrender its copyright, yet scraping skips the conversation entirely.

Key takeaway: Public visibility is not the same as a license.

Copyright in One Slide

Copyright is a legal right that lets a creator control copying of their original work. It attaches automatically the moment the work is fixed in writing, recording, or a saved file.

Key takeaway: Rights exist by default, with no registration required.

Whose Rights Are in Play

A single image can involve the artist, the photographer, and the hosting site, each holding a stake. One scrape can touch all of them at once.

Key takeaway: One copied file can implicate several rights holders.

02 — Memorization to Infringement

Second section. The bridge from a technical fact (models memorize) to a legal one (output that reproduces a work may infringe).

Key takeaway: Memorization is what links training inputs to infringing outputs.

Models Memorize

Training does not always blur data into patterns. Carlini and colleagues showed that some examples survive nearly word for word, especially repeated or rare ones, and the right prompt pulls them back out.

Key takeaway: Some training text can be recovered almost verbatim.

The Infringement Chain

The chain runs from copyrighted training text, to a memorized copy in the weights, to a near-verbatim output. Regurgitation turns a hidden copy into a visible one a court can see.

Key takeaway: Verbatim output makes the hidden copy legally visible.

Images Copy Too

Somepalli and colleagues showed image generators can reproduce training pictures, especially duplicated ones. A prompt can summon a near-exact match, sometimes a recognizable logo or artwork.

Key takeaway: The copying problem is not limited to text.

Style Versus Copy

Learning a general style, the patterns of color, line, and tone, is usually not protected. Copying a specific work is the real risk. The hard cases sit in the blurry middle.

Key takeaway: Style is broadly fair game; specific works are not.

How Often It Happens

Verbatim copying is rare but real. Most outputs are genuinely new, but a small fraction reproduce training data, and rare at web scale still means many cases. One leaked passage can anchor a lawsuit.

Key takeaway: Rarity does not make the problem go away at scale.

03 — The Lawsuits

Third section. The disputes that moved these questions from the lab into court.

Key takeaway: The law is being shaped right now through active litigation.

The Courtroom Opens

From 2022 onward, publishers, artists, and authors filed suits against model builders and data hosts. Most cases are still ongoing, so this is an open debate, not settled law.

Key takeaway: Treat every claim here as unresolved.

New York Times v. OpenAI

Filed in 2023, a major newspaper sued over both training and output. The complaint showed near-verbatim article text and echoed paywalled paragraphs. This is ongoing litigation, not a verdict.

Key takeaway: Memorized output became central evidence.

Getty v. Stability AI

A stock-image company sued an image generator, claiming millions of photos were used without a license. Some outputs even showed a garbled version of its watermark. Cases were filed in both the US and the UK.

Key takeaway: Visible traces of the source strengthened the claim.

The Authors' Class Actions

Novelists and writers sued over books in the training data, alleging the use of pirated book collections. Many authors joined as a class, including named writers among the plaintiffs.

Key takeaway: Books are original and traceable, which makes them strong test cases.

Code Joins the Fight

Programmers sued over a coding assistant. Public code carries open-source licenses that demand credit and terms, and the claim is that suggestions dropped that attribution.

Key takeaway: Open-source is not the same as no conditions.

What the Suits Share

Different plaintiffs share one shape of complaint: my work was copied to train, the model reproduces it, and it competes with what I sell. The defense usually answers: fair use.

Key takeaway: Input, output, and market harm are the recurring three claims.

04 — The Fair-Use Debate

Fourth section. The central legal defense, and why it is genuinely unsettled.

Key takeaway: Fair use is the battleground, not a safe harbor.

What Is Fair Use

Fair use is a US rule that permits some copying without permission, judged case by case. It is a defense a court weighs after the fact, not a box you check in advance.

Key takeaway: Fair use is decided in hindsight, by a court.

The Four Factors

Henderson and colleagues map the four factors US courts weigh together: the purpose of the use, the nature of the work, the amount taken, and the effect on the market. Market harm often carries the most weight.

Key takeaway: No single factor decides; courts balance all four.

The Transformative Argument

The builder's case is that training is transformative: the model learns patterns rather than storing copies, serving a new purpose, much like a student who reads and then writes. The analogy is contested.

Key takeaway: "Transformative" is the strongest pro-training argument, and the most debated.

The Market-Harm Argument

The creator's case is that the model competes with them: it floods their market with substitutes, built on their unpaid work. The fourth factor weighs exactly this harm, and it is often decisive.

Key takeaway: If the output substitutes for the original, fair use weakens sharply.

Other Jurisdictions

Fair use is a US doctrine. The EU offers a text-and-data-mining exception with an opt-out, Japan has a broad training allowance, and the UK is still debating. Where you train changes what is allowed.

Key takeaway: The legal answer depends heavily on jurisdiction.

No Bright Line Yet

The honest answer to "is training legal?" is: it depends, on the data, the output, the market, and the court. This is an open question that judges are actively deciding.

Key takeaway: There is no clean yes-or-no answer today.

05 — Consent, Opt-Out & Licensing

Fifth section. The practical mechanisms that could move data sourcing toward permission.

Key takeaway: Consent and provenance are the tools meant to fix the data problem.

Three Ways to Get Consent

Three models sit along a spectrum: opt-out takes data unless told no, opt-in takes only if told yes, and licensing pays for clear permission. Each shifts the burden differently.

Key takeaway: The choice of model decides who carries the work of consent.

The Opt-Out Flow

A website can signal "do not train" through robots.txt, which lists rules for crawlers. A well-behaved crawler obeys; others may ignore it. robots.txt is a request, not a lock.

Key takeaway: Opt-out depends on crawlers choosing to cooperate.

Opt-Out Has Limits

Telling crawlers "no" only goes so far: it cannot undo data already collected, not every crawler honors it, and most people never knew to opt out. Default-yes puts the work on the creator.

Key takeaway: Opt-out is weak because the default still favors collection.

Licensed Data Deals

A cleaner path is to pay for the data. Model builders now sign deals with publishers, news archives, photo libraries, and forums, and a licensing market is forming fast.

Key takeaway: Licensing converts a legal risk into a clear, paid permission.

Data Provenance

Provenance is a record of where each piece of data came from and how it may be used. Knowing the source is the first step to honoring the rights attached to it.

Key takeaway: You cannot respect rights you cannot trace.

Attribution and Credit

Provenance lets credit flow back: track which sources shaped an output, then name or pay them. Doing this across billions of training examples is a hard, active problem.

Key takeaway: Attribution is the goal; scale is the obstacle.

A Consent Demo

A hands-on demo: read a site's robots.txt for AI rules, search a public dataset for your own work, then toggle opt-out and re-check coverage. The aim is to make consent feel concrete.

Key takeaway: Consent becomes tangible when you can flip the switch yourself.

06 — Frontier 2025-26

Sixth section. Where rulings, markets, and standards are heading, and what remains unresolved.

Key takeaway: The landscape is shifting quickly on all three fronts.

Courts Start to Rule

Early decisions are arriving and they split: some training looks fair-use friendly, pirated sources draw sharper scrutiny, and output that copies still raises liability. A moving target decided case by case.

Key takeaway: The source and the output both matter to how a court rules.

Licensing Markets Grow

Paying for data is becoming standard: large publisher and platform deals, marketplaces for training-ready data, and opt-in datasets sold as a selling point. Consent is turning into a business model.

Key takeaway: The market is pricing in permission.

Provenance Standards

New tools try to label data and outputs: content credentials tag where media came from, dataset cards document sources and licenses, and regulators push for training transparency.

Key takeaway: Provenance is hardening into shared infrastructure.

Open Questions

The big unknowns remain: is training fair use, who gets paid and how, can consent scale to the whole web, and how do we prove a source was honored? The debate is wide open.

Key takeaway: The core questions are still unanswered.

Key Takeaways

To close: training data is people's copyrighted work, memorization can link training to output, fair use is contested rather than settled, and consent, licensing, and provenance are the proposed fixes.

Key takeaway: Provenance is the thread that ties data back to rights.

Closer

End on the question we opened with: who owns the data a model learns from? It is a live debate, and the answer will shape how AI is built.

Key takeaway: Ownership of training data is one of the defining trust questions for AI.
