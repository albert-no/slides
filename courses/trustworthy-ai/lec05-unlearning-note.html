Machine Unlearning

Welcome to Lecture 5. Today's question: once a model is trained on someone's data, how do we make it forget that person, without simply training the whole thing again?

Key takeaway: Deletion in the database does not delete the trace in the model.

Contents

Six steps: the legal motivation, what unlearning formally means, the main methods, unlearning in language models, how to check whether forgetting worked, and the current frontier.

Key takeaway: From a deletion request to a provable, testable forgetting.

01 — The Right to Be Forgotten

First section. The motivation: a user who once shared data now wants it gone, and the model is in the way.

Key takeaway: A deletion request is a model problem, not just a database problem.

A User Changes Their Mind

Alice asks us to delete her account and her data. Dropping her row from the database is trivial, but her data already shaped a trained model, and that model is untouched.

Key takeaway: Deleting the row does not touch the weights.

Definitions

The setting used throughout the lecture. A (possibly randomized) learning algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ maps a dataset $D = \{z_1,\dots,z_n\}$ of examples $z_i \in \mathcal{Z}$ to a model, viewed as a parameter vector $\theta \in \Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$. A deletion request names one point $z \in D$ (later, a subset $D_f \subseteq D$). Deleting $z$ from the stored dataset changes $D$, but $\mathcal{A}(D)$ — the model already trained and deployed — is a function of the old $D$ and is not altered by the database operation. The entire lecture is about what to do to $\mathcal{A}(D)$.

The Law Says Delete

GDPR Article 17 grants a right to erasure, the "right to be forgotten." California's CCPA grants a similar right. On request, the data must go, and increasingly that is read to include the model.

Key takeaway: Forgetting is a legal duty, not an optional feature.

Background & further reading

GDPR Article 17 (in force since May 2018) is titled "Right to erasure ('right to be forgotten')": on any of the grounds in Art. 17(1) — e.g. the data are no longer necessary, or consent is withdrawn — the controller must erase the data subject's personal data "without undue delay", subject to the exceptions in Art. 17(3). The CCPA gives California consumers a right to ask businesses to delete personal information collected from them. The phrase "right to be forgotten" predates the GDPR: in Google Spain SL and Google Inc. v AEPD and Mario Costeja González (Case C-131/12, judgment of 13 May 2014), the Court of Justice of the EU ruled — still under the 1995 Data Protection Directive — that a search engine must, on request, de-list links to lawfully published personal information when the data subject's privacy rights override the public's interest in finding it. Whether erasure duties extend to trained models is the open legal-technical question this lecture takes up.

  • Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (EUR-Lex) — official GDPR text.
  • CCPA — California Attorney General — consumer rights, including the right to delete.
  • CJEU, Case C-131/12, Google Spain (EUR-Lex) — the 2014 right-to-be-forgotten judgment.

Delete for Three Reasons

Privacy law is only one driver. Rights holders want copyrighted books removed from models, and safety teams want hazardous how-to knowledge taken out. All three demands land on the same technical problem: get it out of the weights.

Key takeaway: Privacy, copyright, and safety all reduce to the same deletion problem.

Background & further reading

The three drivers each have a flagship line of work met later in the lecture: privacy-law deletion (the certified-unlearning literature, Sections 02–03), copyright removal (the "Who's Harry Potter?" experiment, Section 04), and safety-motivated removal of hazardous knowledge (the WMDP benchmark and RMU, Section 04). They share the formal problem but differ in the unit of deletion — a person's records, a copyrighted work, a capability — which will matter in Sections 04–06.

  • Eldan & Russinovich, "Who's Harry Potter?" (arXiv:2310.02238) — copyright-motivated unlearning.
  • Li, Pan, Gopal, et al., "The WMDP Benchmark" (arXiv:2403.03218) — safety-motivated unlearning.

Data Lives in the Weights

Training presses each record into the parameters. Remove the row from the dataset and the trace pressed into the weights still remains.

Key takeaway: The model is a lossy copy of the data, and lossy is not gone.

Definitions

For concreteness, take $\mathcal{A}$ to be regularized empirical risk minimization:

$$\hat\theta \;=\; \mathcal{A}(D) \;\in\; \arg\min_{\theta}\; F(\theta; D), \qquad F(\theta;D) \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in D} \ell(\theta; z) \;+\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\theta\rVert^2,$$

with per-example loss $\ell$ and ridge weight $\lambda \ge 0$. The first-order condition $\nabla F(\hat\theta;D)=0$ couples $\hat\theta$ to every example: change one $z_i$ and the stationarity equation, hence the minimizer, moves. In this precise sense each record is "pressed into" $\hat\theta$; the weights are a deterministic (or, with randomized training, distributional) function of the full dataset. Section 03 quantifies exactly how much one point moves the optimum.

Why Not Just Ignore It

A model that still remembers Alice keeps her exposed: membership inference can confirm she was in the data, and extraction can recite her record. The deletion would be fiction.

Key takeaway: Compliance means the model forgets, not just the database.

Definitions

Membership inference attack (MIA) (Lecture 4, restated). An adversary receives a model $\theta$ and a candidate point $z$, and outputs a guess $\hat m \in \{0,1\}$ for whether $z$ was in the training set. In the standard experiment, $z$ is drawn either from the training set ($m=1$) or from fresh data ($m=0$) with equal probability, and the attack's advantage is $\mathrm{Adv} = \Pr[\hat m = 1 \mid m=1] - \Pr[\hat m = 1 \mid m=0]$ (true-positive rate minus false-positive rate). The simplest attack thresholds the loss: $\hat m = \mathbb{1}[\ell(\theta;z) \le \tau]$ — members tend to have lower loss. If, after a claimed deletion, an MIA still flags Alice's record as a member, her data demonstrably still influences the model; Section 05 turns this attack into an audit.

Retraining Is Expensive

The obvious fix is to train again without Alice. But large models cost millions to train and deletion requests arrive constantly, so retraining per request is hopeless.

Key takeaway: We need to forget without starting over.

Enter Machine Unlearning

The goal: given a trained model, efficiently produce a new model as if a chosen point had never been used. Cao and Yang gave a first formal proposal in 2015.

Key takeaway: Unlearning is efficient deletion of a data point's effect.

Definitions

Definition (data-deletion operation; Ginart et al., NeurIPS 2019). An unlearning algorithm (deletion operation) $\mathcal{U}$ for $\mathcal{A}$ takes the dataset $D$, the point $z \in D$ to delete, and the trained model $\mathcal{A}(D)$, and must satisfy, for every $D$ and every $z \in D$,

$$\mathcal{U}\big(D, z, \mathcal{A}(D)\big) \;\stackrel{d}{=}\; \mathcal{A}\big(D\setminus\{z\}\big),$$

equality in distribution, over the internal randomness of both algorithms. The point of the definition: the target is not "the model behaves differently on $z$" but "the model is distributed as if $z$ had never been used". Efficiency is what makes $\mathcal{U}$ interesting — $\mathcal{U} := \mathcal{A}(D\setminus\{z\})$ satisfies the equation trivially at full retraining cost.

Background & further reading

Cao and Yang coined "machine unlearning" at IEEE S&P 2015, in a setting where models are trained on summations of the data (statistical-query style), so a deletion only updates the affected summations. Ginart et al. (NeurIPS 2019) gave the distributional definition above and efficient deletion for $k$-means clustering.

02 — What Unlearning Means

Second section. We pin down the target and the spectrum from exact to approximate forgetting.

Key takeaway: Every method is judged against retraining from scratch.

The Gold Standard

The ideal answer is the model you would have gotten if the point had never been in the data. That retrained model is the reference against which we measure everything.

Key takeaway: Retraining from scratch defines what "forgotten" means.

Definitions

The retrain reference is $\mathcal{A}(D\setminus D_f)$ — not one specific model but a distribution over models, because $\mathcal{A}$ is randomized (initialization, batch order, data augmentation). "Forgotten" is therefore a statement about distributions: an unlearned model succeeds if its law matches (exactly or approximately) the law of $\mathcal{A}(D\setminus D_f)$. Comparing two single runs weight-by-weight is meaningless — two independent retrains on the same data already differ in their weights.

Two Models, Compared

Unlearning runs a cheap operation on the trained model and aims to land on the same place a slow retrain on the remaining data would have reached.

Key takeaway: Success means matching the retrained model at a fraction of the cost.

Definitions

In full generality an unlearning algorithm is a map $\mathcal{U}(D_f,\, \mathcal{A}(D),\, T(D))$ where $T(D)$ is a bounded set of auxiliary statistics cached at training time (checkpoints, per-shard models, gradient sums, a Hessian) — everything the unlearner may consult besides the model. The design space of Section 03 is exactly the choice of $T$: SISA caches shard models and slice checkpoints; the influence-function unlearner caches nothing but recomputes curvature; Sekhari et al. cache $O(d^2)$ statistics and never the dataset. The cost target is always $\mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{U}) \ll \mathrm{cost}(\mathcal{A}(D \setminus D_f))$.

Exact Unlearning

Exact unlearning produces a model identical in distribution to retraining. It is a provable guarantee rather than a hope, but it usually needs special training structure.

Key takeaway: Exact means same output distribution as if the point never existed.

Definitions

Definition (exact unlearning). $\mathcal{U}$ is an exact unlearning algorithm for $\mathcal{A}$ if for every dataset $D$, every $z \in D$, and every measurable set of models $S \subseteq \Theta$,

$$\Pr\big[\mathcal{U}(D, z, \mathcal{A}(D)) \in S\big] \;=\; \Pr\big[\mathcal{A}(D\setminus\{z\}) \in S\big].$$

Equality of the two output distributions, not of individual runs. Under this definition no test — statistical, adversarial, or forensic — can distinguish the unlearned model from a retrain, because there is literally no difference in law to detect. The price is structural: exactness is achieved by architectures that localize each point's influence (SISA, Section 03) rather than by editing an unstructured trained model.

Approximate Unlearning

Approximate unlearning settles for being close enough, much faster: nudge the weights to undo the point and accept a small, bounded difference. This is the practical regime for large models.

Key takeaway: Trade exactness for speed when exact is infeasible.

Borrowing the DP Yardstick

We define "close" the way differential privacy defines indistinguishable: the unlearned model's output distribution is within $e^\varepsilon$ (plus $\delta$) of the retrained model's. Small $\varepsilon,\delta$ means it looks retrained.

Key takeaway: An $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ bound turns "close to retrained" into a number.

Definitions

Definition ($(\varepsilon,\delta)$-certified unlearning; Guo et al. 2020, Sekhari et al. 2021, after Ginart et al. 2019). $\mathcal{U}$ is an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-unlearning algorithm for $\mathcal{A}$ if for every dataset $D$, every deletion request $z \in D$, and every measurable $S \subseteq \Theta$, both

$$\Pr\big[\mathcal{U}(D,z,\mathcal{A}(D)) \in S\big] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr\big[\mathcal{A}(D\setminus\{z\}) \in S\big] + \delta$$

and the same inequality with the two probabilities swapped. Both directions are required: one direction alone would let the unlearned model omit behaviors the retrained model has. Setting $\varepsilon=\delta=0$ recovers exact unlearning.

Theorem & proof

Proposition (exact $\Rightarrow$ certified). If $\mathcal{U}$ is exact, then it is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-certified for every $\varepsilon \ge 0,\ \delta \ge 0$.

Proof. Write $P$ for the law of $\mathcal{U}(D,z,\mathcal{A}(D))$ and $Q$ for the law of $\mathcal{A}(D\setminus\{z\})$. Exactness means $P = Q$, so for every measurable $S$: $P(S) = Q(S) \le e^{\varepsilon}Q(S) + \delta$, and symmetrically. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes)

Caveat — the certificate constrains the pair $(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U})$, not quality. Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the useless constant algorithm $\mathcal{A}(D) \equiv \theta_0$ and let $\mathcal{U}$ do nothing. Then both laws are the point mass at $\theta_0$ and the pair is $(0,0)$-certified — perfectly unlearned, perfectly useless. A certificate is meaningful only together with a utility statement about $\mathcal{A}$.

Background & further reading

  • Guo et al., "Certified Data Removal from Machine Learning Models", ICML 2020 (arXiv:1911.03030) — certified removal for linear models.
  • Sekhari et al., "Remember What You Want to Forget", NeurIPS 2021 (arXiv:2103.03279) — the two-sided definition used here, plus deletion capacity.
  • Ginart et al., NeurIPS 2019 (arXiv:1907.05012) — the exact ($\varepsilon=\delta=0$) ancestor.

Forget Set and Retain Set

The data splits in two: the forget set we want erased from the model, and the retain set we keep and still want to predict well.

Key takeaway: Forget one side, preserve the other.

Definitions

Fix a forget set $D_f \subseteq D$ and the retain set $D_r = D \setminus D_f$. The formal goal has two clauses: (i) forgetting — the law of $\mathcal{U}(D_f, \mathcal{A}(D), T(D))$ is close (exactly equal, or $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-close) to the law of $\mathcal{A}(D_r)$; (ii) utility — the unlearned model's population risk is close to that of $\mathcal{A}(D_r)$. Note that the reference in both clauses is the retrain on $D_r$, not the original model: some accuracy loss relative to $\mathcal{A}(D)$ is correct behavior, because the retrained model never saw $D_f$ either.

Two Things to Get Right

A good method balances forgetting, so the target's influence is gone, with utility, so accuracy on the retain set survives. Forgetting everything is trivial; forgetting only the target is the real challenge.

Key takeaway: The hard part is forgetting only the target.

Definitions

The two axes, formally: forgetting error is a distance between distributions, e.g. the smallest $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ for which the two-sided bound of the previous entry holds between the law of the unlearned model and the law of $\mathcal{A}(D_r)$; utility is the expected risk $\mathbb{E}[F(\theta_u)]$ on the task (or accuracy on a held-out set). "Forgetting everything" — returning a freshly initialized model — drives the first axis to a trivial optimum against the wrong reference (it does not match $\mathcal{A}(D_r)$ either) and destroys the second; the counterexample in the previous entry shows the certificate alone cannot see this. Every method in Section 03 is a point on this two-axis tradeoff.

03 — How to Unlearn

Third section. The toolkit: the retraining baseline, sharded retraining via SISA, and approximate weight edits.

Key takeaway: More speed usually buys a weaker guarantee.

The Retraining Baseline

Drop the point and train again on the rest. It is exact by construction and the yardstick every method copies, but far too slow to run for each request.

Key takeaway: Correct and useless; the baseline to beat on cost.

Theorem & proof

Proposition (retraining is exact). The unlearner $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{retrain}}(D, z, \cdot) := \mathcal{A}(D\setminus\{z\})$ (ignore the trained model, rerun $\mathcal{A}$ on the remaining data with fresh randomness) is exact.

Proof. The unlearner's output is a run of $\mathcal{A}(D\setminus\{z\})$, so the two distributions in the definition of exactness are the same distribution. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes)

Its cost is a full training run on $n - 1$ points — the number every other method is measured against.

Idea: Train in Pieces

If a data point only touched part of the model, we only need to retrain that part. Split the data into shards, train one sub-model per shard, and a deletion redoes just one shard.

Key takeaway: Localize each point's influence so deletion is local too.

Definitions

Partition $D = D^{(1)} \,\dot\cup\, \cdots \,\dot\cup\, D^{(S)}$ into $S$ disjoint shards and train an independent sub-model $\theta^{(k)} = \mathcal{A}_0(D^{(k)})$ on each. By construction, $\theta^{(k)}$ is a function of $D^{(k)}$ only, so a point $z \in D^{(k)}$ influences exactly one sub-model — the influence-localization that makes deletion local. The final predictor aggregates the $S$ sub-models (next entry).

SISA

SISA stands for Sharded, Isolated, Sliced, Aggregated training. The data is split into isolated shards, each trains a sub-model, and the sub-models are aggregated by voting or averaging at prediction time.

Key takeaway: Isolated shards make exact, local deletion possible.

Slide figure: Bourtoule et al., IEEE S&P 2021, Fig. 2: the original training data $\mathcal{D}$ is split into shards $\mathcal{D}_1,\dots,\mathcal{D}_S$, each shard into slices $\mathcal{D}_{k,r}$; one constituent model $M_k$ per shard; an aggregation step produces the output. The red slice marks the data to unlearn. Walk the four letters of SISA down the picture.

Definitions

SISA (Bourtoule et al., IEEE S&P 2021): Sharded — $D$ is split into $S$ shards; Isolated — each sub-model trains only on its shard, with no parameter or gradient sharing; Sliced — within a shard, the data is further split into $R$ slices presented incrementally, with a checkpoint saved after each slice; Aggregated — predictions combine the $S$ sub-models by majority vote or score averaging. Exactness holds relative to the SISA procedure itself: after a deletion is processed (next entry), the released ensemble has exactly the distribution SISA training would have produced on $D \setminus \{z\}$ with the same shard assignment — the point's influence is provably, not approximately, gone. The reference is SISA-on-$D_r$, not monolithic-training-on-$D_r$.

Background & further reading

  • Bourtoule, Chandrasekaran, Choquette-Choo, Jia, Travers, Zhang, Lie, Papernot, "Machine Unlearning", IEEE S&P 2021 (arXiv:1912.03817) — the SISA paper.

Deleting in SISA

A delete request hits one shard only: find the shard with the point, retrain just that sub-model, and re-aggregate. The other shards are untouched.

Key takeaway: Exact unlearning at a fraction of the retraining cost.

Theorem & proof

Proposition (sharding speedup). With $S$ equal shards of $n/S$ points each and no slicing, processing one deletion retrains a single sub-model on (at most) $n/S$ points — a factor-$S$ saving over the $n$-point retraining baseline, for every request.

Proof. The deleted point $z$ lies in exactly one shard $D^{(k)}$ (the partition is disjoint). Sub-models $\theta^{(j)}$, $j \ne k$, are functions of $D^{(j)}$ only, which is unchanged, so their trained values remain valid. Only $\theta^{(k)}$ must be retrained, on $D^{(k)}\setminus\{z\}$, i.e. on $n/S - 1 \lt n/S$ points. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, following Bourtoule et al.)

Slices Make It Cheaper

Inside a shard, train in incremental slices and checkpoint after each. On deletion, roll back to the checkpoint before the point and replay only the later slices, instead of redoing the whole shard.

Key takeaway: Checkpoints shrink the work needed per deletion further.

Theorem & proof

Proposition (slicing speedup). Split each shard of $m = n/S$ points into $R$ equal slices, presented cumulatively: training stage $j$ processes the union of slices $1,\dots,j$, so stage $j$ costs $j\,(m/R)$ example-passes and a full shard training costs $\sum_{j=1}^{R} j\,\frac{m}{R} = \frac{m(R+1)}{2}$. If the deleted point is in a uniformly random slice, the expected replay cost is

$$\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{cost}] \;=\; \frac{m\,(R+1)(2R+1)}{6R} \;=\; \frac{n\,(R+1)(2R+1)}{6SR},$$

so slicing saves a factor $\frac{3R}{2R+1}$ over retraining the shard from scratch — at most $3/2$, approached as $R \to \infty$.

Proof. If $z$ lies in slice $r$, the checkpoint after slice $r-1$ is still valid; stages $r,\dots,R$ must be replayed, costing $\sum_{j=r}^{R} j\,\frac{m}{R}$. Averaging over $r \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{1,\dots,R\}$:

$$\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{cost}] = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{j=r}^{R} j\,\frac{m}{R} = \frac{m}{R^2}\sum_{j=1}^{R} j\cdot\#\{r \le j\} = \frac{m}{R^2}\sum_{j=1}^{R} j^2 = \frac{m}{R^2}\cdot\frac{R(R+1)(2R+1)}{6},$$

which is the stated value. Dividing the full-shard cost $\frac{m(R+1)}{2}$ by it gives $\frac{3R}{2R+1} \nearrow \frac{3}{2}$. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, consistent with the privacy-course SISA cost model; the tech supplement's "slicing saves at most another 3/2" is this limit)

So the dominant saving is sharding (factor $S$, tunable); slicing contributes at most a further constant $3/2$ but costs checkpoint storage.

The SISA Tradeoff

Sharding wins cheap, exact, predictable deletion, but each sub-model sees less data, so aggregate accuracy can drop. It is a structural choice made before training.

Key takeaway: Sharding buys deletion speed by spending some accuracy.

Slide figure: Bourtoule et al., IEEE S&P 2021, Fig. 6: top-1 accuracy versus number of shards on ImageNet and Mini-ImageNet. SISA drops steadily as shards grow, staying well above the naive $1/S$ baseline (train on one shard, ignore the rest) but below the single batch-$K$ model. This is the accuracy price of the "Costs" column.

Definitions

The cost side is a theorem (previous two entries); the accuracy side is empirical. Each sub-model trains on $n/S$ points, so its generalization error grows as $S$ grows, and aggregation only partly compensates — Bourtoule et al. measure the degradation on standard image benchmarks and show it grows with $S$, especially for hard tasks. The choice of $S$ (and $R$) must be made before training: SISA cannot be retrofitted onto an already-trained monolithic model, which is exactly the gap the weight-editing methods of the next entries try to fill.

Can We Edit the Weights?

SISA changes how we train. But often the model is already trained, with no shards or checkpoints saved. Then we want to nudge the existing weights to undo one point: approximate unlearning.

Key takeaway: Estimate the point's influence, then subtract it from the weights.

Definitions

Standing assumptions for the next two entries (the classical certified-removal setting). The trained model is $\theta_o = \arg\min_\theta F(\theta;D)$ with $F(\theta;D) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in D}\ell(\theta;z) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\theta\rVert^2$, and the retrain target is $\theta_{-z} = \arg\min_\theta F_{-z}(\theta)$, where $F_{-z}(\theta) = F(\theta;D) - \frac1n \ell(\theta;z)$ is the objective with $z$'s term removed. Assume:

  • A1 (bounded gradients): $\lVert\nabla\ell(\theta;z)\rVert \le G$ for all $\theta, z$;
  • A2 (strong convexity): $\ell(\cdot;z)$ convex, so $F$ and $F_{-z}$ are $\lambda$-strongly convex (unique minimizers);
  • A3 (smooth curvature): $\nabla^2 F$ is $M$-Lipschitz: $\lVert\nabla^2F(\theta)-\nabla^2F(\theta')\rVert \le M\lVert\theta-\theta'\rVert$.

These hold for regularized linear models (logistic/ridge regression on bounded features) — and pointedly fail for deep networks, which is the honest limit of this theory.

Influence Functions

Lead with the intuition: estimate how much one example pulled the weights, then subtract that pull. The gradient at the point measures the pull; the inverse curvature converts it into a weight change, undoing the point in a single step.

Key takeaway: One closed-form step approximates removing a point.

Slide figure: Koh and Liang, ICML 2017, Fig. 2: predicted change in test loss from the influence estimate versus the actual change from leave-one-out retraining, one dot per removed training point (MNIST). Linear models line up on the diagonal; the CNN scatters more but keeps the trend. The estimate is a stand-in for retraining, not a replacement.

Definitions

The influence-function unlearner takes one step from the trained weights:

$$\theta_u \;=\; \theta_o \;+\; \frac{1}{n}\,H^{-1}\,\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta_o; z), \qquad H = \nabla^2 F(\theta_o; D),$$

the formula on the tech-supplement slide. (Koh & Liang state the upweighting direction, $\frac{d\theta}{d\epsilon} = -H^{-1}\nabla\ell$; removal is upweighting by $-\frac1n$, which flips the sign.)

Theorem & proof

Where the formula comes from (implicit function theorem). Define the interpolation $L_t(\theta) = F(\theta;D) - \frac{t}{n}\,\ell(\theta;z)$ and $\theta(t) = \arg\min_\theta L_t(\theta)$, so $\theta(0)=\theta_o$ and $\theta(1)=\theta_{-z}$ (the minimizer of $F_{-z}$). By A2 the minimizer is unique and the first-order condition $\nabla L_t(\theta(t)) = 0$ holds identically in $t$. Differentiating in $t$ and evaluating at $t=0$:

$$\nabla^2 F(\theta_o)\,\theta'(0) \;-\; \frac{1}{n}\nabla\ell(\theta_o;z) \;=\; 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta'(0) = \frac{1}{n}H^{-1}\nabla\ell(\theta_o;z).$$

The unlearner is the first-order Taylor step $\theta(1) \approx \theta(0) + \theta'(0)$. (derivation: course notes, following Koh & Liang's Section 2 argument)

Theorem (accuracy of the one-step edit; adapted from Guo et al., ICML 2020). Let $\theta_u = \theta_o - \nabla^2F_{-z}(\theta_o)^{-1}\nabla F_{-z}(\theta_o)$ be the Newton step of the retain objective from $\theta_o$ (the leave-one-out–Hessian variant of the formula above). Under A1–A3,

$$\big\lVert \theta_u - \theta_{-z} \big\rVert \;\le\; \frac{M\,G^2}{2\,\lambda^3\, n^2}.$$

Proof. Two lemmas.

Lemma 1 (the retrain target is close). $\lVert\theta_o - \theta_{-z}\rVert \le \frac{G}{\lambda n}$. Indeed, $F_{-z}$ is $\lambda$-strongly convex with minimizer $\theta_{-z}$, so $\lambda\lVert\theta_o - \theta_{-z}\rVert \le \lVert\nabla F_{-z}(\theta_o) - \nabla F_{-z}(\theta_{-z})\rVert = \lVert\nabla F_{-z}(\theta_o)\rVert$. And $\nabla F_{-z}(\theta_o) = \nabla F(\theta_o;D) - \frac1n\nabla\ell(\theta_o;z) = -\frac1n\nabla\ell(\theta_o;z)$, whose norm is at most $G/n$ by A1.

Lemma 2 (a Newton step squares the error). For a $\lambda$-strongly convex objective with $M$-Lipschitz Hessian, minimizer $\theta^\star$, the Newton step $\theta^+ = \theta - \nabla^2F(\theta)^{-1}\nabla F(\theta)$ satisfies $\lVert\theta^+ - \theta^\star\rVert \le \frac{M}{2\lambda}\lVert\theta-\theta^\star\rVert^2$. Proof: with $e = \theta - \theta^\star$, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives $\nabla F(\theta) = \int_0^1 \nabla^2 F(\theta^\star + s e)\, e \, ds$, so

$$\theta^+ - \theta^\star \;=\; e - \nabla^2F(\theta)^{-1}\!\!\int_0^1\! \nabla^2F(\theta^\star+se)\,e\,ds \;=\; \nabla^2F(\theta)^{-1}\!\!\int_0^1\!\big[\nabla^2F(\theta) - \nabla^2F(\theta^\star+se)\big]e\,ds.$$

Bound $\lVert\nabla^2F(\theta)^{-1}\rVert \le 1/\lambda$ (A2), $\lVert\nabla^2F(\theta) - \nabla^2F(\theta^\star + se)\rVert \le M(1-s)\lVert e\rVert$ (A3), and $\int_0^1(1-s)\,ds = \frac12$.

Combining: apply Lemma 2 to the objective $F_{-z}$ (minimizer $\theta^\star=\theta_{-z}$) at the starting point $\theta = \theta_o$, then Lemma 1: $\lVert\theta_u - \theta_{-z}\rVert \le \frac{M}{2\lambda}\big(\frac{G}{\lambda n}\big)^2 = \frac{MG^2}{2\lambda^3n^2}$. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, checked line by line; Guo et al.'s published theorem is stated via objective perturbation and a gradient-residual bound — this parameter-space form is the course's adaptation, as in the privacy-course deck)

The residual scales as $1/n^2$ — one point among $n$ moves the optimum by $O(1/n)$, and the Newton step squares that. To turn the bound into an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ certificate one adds Gaussian noise calibrated to it; see "Unlearning Meets Privacy" below for why $1/n^2$ beats the DP alternative.

  • Guo et al., "Certified Data Removal", ICML 2020 (arXiv:1911.03030) — the Newton-step remover with certification.

The Catch: Curvature

That formula needs the inverse Hessian, with one entry per pair of parameters: a billion-parameter model would need $10^{18}$ entries. That matrix cannot even be stored, so only rough approximations are feasible.

Key takeaway: Exact influence is out of reach at large scale.

Definitions

$H = \nabla^2_\theta F \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ has $d^2$ entries: $d = 10^9$ gives $10^{18}$ — beyond storage, let alone inversion ($O(d^3)$ direct, $O(d^\omega)$ fast). In practice one never forms $H^{-1}$: the product $H^{-1}v$ is computed iteratively from Hessian-vector products (each costs one extra backward pass) via conjugate gradient or stochastic Neumann-series estimators, as Koh & Liang already did. That handles cost; correctness is a separate failure — in deep networks the objective is non-convex, so A2/A3 fail, $H$ has negative and near-zero eigenvalues, and influence estimates become unstable.

Background & further reading

  • Basu, Pope, Feizi, "Influence Functions in Deep Learning Are Fragile", ICLR 2021 (arXiv:2006.14651) — influence estimates degrade with depth, width, and weak regularization.

Gradient Ascent

Training descends the loss. To forget, ascend it on the target: push the forget point's loss up, undoing the fit. It is simple and needs no Hessian.

Key takeaway: Raising a point's loss is the crudest way to forget it.

Definitions

Gradient ascent (GA) unlearning maximizes the forget-set loss, i.e. minimizes its negation. Update and objective:

$$\theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta + \eta\,\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta, x_{\mathrm{forget}}), \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GA}}(\theta) \;=\; -\,\mathbb{E}_{z\sim D_f}\big[\ell(\theta;z)\big]\ \text{(to minimize)},$$

equivalently: run the training loss on $D_f$ with the sign flipped. For a language model one writes $\ell_\theta(x,y) = \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ and minimizes $\mathbb{E}_{D_f}[\ell_\theta]$ — push the log-likelihood of the forget completions down.

Theorem & proof

Proposition (GA has no optimum). If the model class can drive the forget point's probability to zero (equivalently, its loss to $+\infty$), then $\inf_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GA}}(\theta) = -\infty$ and the infimum is not attained.

Proof. Take $\theta_k$ with $\pi_{\theta_k}(y\mid x) \to 0$ for some $(x,y) \in D_f$. Then $\log\pi_{\theta_k}(y\mid x) \to -\infty$ while every other term of the average is bounded above by $0$, so $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GA}}(\theta_k) \to -\infty$; no finite $\theta$ attains $-\infty$ since each $\log\pi_\theta \gt -\infty$ pointwise. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, as in the privacy-course deck)

An objective with no minimizer gives the optimizer no reason to stop: it keeps pushing after the forget set is gone, at undiminished strength — the collapse of the next entry.

Background & further reading

  • Thudi, Deza, Chandrasekaran, Papernot, "Unrolling SGD: Understanding Factors Influencing Machine Unlearning", IEEE EuroS&P 2022 (arXiv:2109.13398) — analysis of gradient-based approximate unlearning.

Ascent Can Wreck the Model

Raising the loss has no built-in brake. Overshoot and accuracy collapses; the damage can spill onto the retain set. In practice ascent needs a retain-set anchor to stay useful.

Key takeaway: From the inside, forgetting and breaking can look the same.

Slide figure: Kurmanji, Triantafillou, Hayes, and Triantafillou, "Towards Unbounded Machine Unlearning", NeurIPS 2023, Fig. 6(a) and 6(d) (CIFAR-5, ResNet). Left, ascent-only: forget-set error hits 100% by epoch 2, but retain and validation error climb to about 70% with it. Right, alternating ascent on the forget set with descent on the retain set: forget error rises while retain error returns to zero. The retain step is the brake.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (divergence rates, toy model). Two-outcome model $\pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \sigma(\theta)$, $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$, one forget example, gradient flow $\dot\theta = -\nabla\mathcal{L}$. Under GA, $\theta(t) = -t\,(1+o(1))$: linear divergence, forever. Under NPO with parameter $\beta \gt 0$ (next entry), $\theta(t) = -\tfrac1\beta \log t\,(1+o(1))$: logarithmic divergence.

Proof sketch. GA: $\mathcal{L} = \log\sigma(\theta)$, so $\dot\theta = -\sigma(-\theta) \to -1$ as $\theta \to -\infty$; hence $\theta(t) \sim -t$. NPO: the gradient weight (next entry) satisfies $w_\theta = 2\sigma(\beta r_\theta) \sim C e^{\beta\theta}$ as $\theta \to -\infty$, so the flow is asymptotically $\dot\theta = -Ce^{\beta\theta}$; separating variables, $e^{-\beta\theta(t)} = e^{-\beta\theta(0)} + \beta C t$, i.e. $\theta(t) = -\tfrac1\beta\log\big(e^{-\beta\theta(0)} + \beta Ct\big) \sim -\tfrac1\beta\log t$. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, verified by direct integration; the general statement is Zhang, Lin, Bai, Mei, COLM 2024, Thms 1–2)

Linear versus logarithmic is the whole practical difference: GA marches the weights off to infinity, and in a real network there is no parameter direction reserved for $D_f$ alone, so the damage lands on $D_r$.

A Gentler Push: NPO

Negative Preference Optimization fixes the runaway. It weights each ascent-style step by how much the model still remembers the forget data, so the push shrinks as forgetting succeeds. In the paper's Figure 2, gradient ascent drags forget quality and utility down together, while NPO forgets with utility surviving far longer.

Key takeaway: A self-damping ascent forgets without wrecking the model.

Definitions

Write $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ for the model before unlearning and $r_\theta(y) = \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ for the log-ratio ("how much the model still assigns to the forget completion, relative to where it started"). NPO is DPO with the preferred branch deleted — in unlearning there is no preferred answer, only a rejected one:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPO}}(\theta) \;=\; -\frac{2}{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D_f}\Big[\log\sigma\big(-\beta\, r_\theta(y)\big)\Big] \;=\; \frac{2}{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}_{D_f}\Big[\log\big(1 + e^{\beta r_\theta(y)}\big)\Big].$$

Theorem & proof

Theorem (NPO is self-limiting; Zhang, Lin, Bai, Mei, COLM 2024).

  • (i) $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPO}}(\theta) \gt 0$ for every $\theta$, and $\inf_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPO}} = 0$: the objective is bounded below, unlike GA.
  • (ii) $\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPO}} = \mathbb{E}\big[w_\theta\, \nabla_\theta \ell_\theta(x,y)\big]$ with gradient weight $w_\theta = 2\sigma(\beta r_\theta) \in (0,2)$ — the same step direction as GA, reweighted.
  • (iii) $w_\theta \le 2\big(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\big)^\beta$: the weight vanishes exactly as forgetting succeeds.

Proof. (i) $\log(1+e^u) \gt 0$ for all real $u$, and $\log(1+e^u) \to 0$ as $u \to -\infty$; drive $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\to0$ so that $r_\theta \to -\infty$. (ii) Using $\frac{d}{du}\log\sigma(u) = \sigma(-u)$ and $\nabla_\theta r_\theta = \nabla_\theta \ell_\theta$:

$$\nabla_\theta\Big[-\tfrac{2}{\beta}\log\sigma(-\beta r_\theta)\Big] = -\tfrac{2}{\beta}\,\sigma(\beta r_\theta)\cdot(-\beta)\,\nabla_\theta r_\theta = 2\,\sigma(\beta r_\theta)\,\nabla_\theta \ell_\theta.$$

(iii) $\sigma(u) = e^u/(1+e^u) \le e^u$, so $2\sigma(\beta r_\theta) \le 2e^{\beta r_\theta} = 2(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})^\beta$. $\blacksquare$ (proof as in the privacy-course deck, verified line by line against the NPO objective)

Proposition (NPO contains GA). As $\beta \to 0^+$, a Taylor expansion of $g(u) = \log(1+e^u)$ at $u = \beta r_\theta$ (using $g(0)=\log 2$, $g'(0)=\tfrac12$, $g''(0)=\tfrac14$) gives $\tfrac2\beta g(\beta r_\theta) = \tfrac{2\log 2}{\beta} + r_\theta + \tfrac{\beta}{4}r_\theta^2 + O(\beta^2)$: up to a constant, NPO's $\beta\to0$ limit is GA, and the first correction penalizes drift from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Correspondingly $w_\theta \to 2\sigma(0) = 1$, the GA weight. So $\beta$ is a forgetting-versus-utility dial, not a nuisance hyperparameter. (derivation: course notes)

Background & further reading

  • Zhang, Lin, Bai, Mei, "Negative Preference Optimization: From Catastrophic Collapse to Effective Unlearning", COLM 2024 (arXiv:2404.05868) — the deck's collapse plot is their Figure 2.

The Method Landscape

Three families: retraining is exact but slow, SISA is exact and shard-local but restructures training, and approximate edits are fast with no exact guarantee.

Key takeaway: Choose where you want to pay: cost, structure, or guarantee.

Theorem & proof

Proposition (the gradient methods are one family). GA and NPO (and NPO's length-normalized variants) all take steps of the form $\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{forget}} = \mathbb{E}\big[w_\theta\,\nabla_\theta\ell_\theta\big]$ — same direction, different pressure: $w_\theta \equiv 1$ for GA, $w_\theta = 2\sigma(\beta r_\theta)$ for NPO. Among losses of the form $\mathbb{E}[\phi(\ell_\theta)]$, the weight is constant if and only if $\phi$ is affine — i.e. only for GA.

Proof. Chain rule: $\nabla\mathbb{E}[\phi(\ell_\theta)] = \mathbb{E}[\phi'(\ell_\theta)\nabla\ell_\theta]$, so $w_\theta = \phi'(\ell_\theta)$, which is constant in $\theta$ iff $\phi'$ is constant iff $\phi$ is affine. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes)

Every post-GA objective installs a brake; the methods disagree only about where.

Key takeaway: Hands-on, the forgetting-versus-utility tension is immediate.

04 — Unlearning in Language Models

Fourth section. In LLMs the forget target is rarely a single row, which makes everything harder.

Key takeaway: Knowledge is spread across the whole network.

What Do We Forget?

In a language model the target might be a person's private facts, a copyrighted book, or hazardous how-to knowledge, none of which lives in one place.

Key takeaway: The forget target is a concept, not a data row.

Definitions

The request changes type. In Sections 02–03 the request was a set of training rows $D_f \subseteq D$. In the LLM setting the request is a predicate $\varphi$ — "everything about this person", "this book", "how to synthesize X" — and the forget set must be constructed: $S_\varphi = \{z \in D : z \text{ matches } \varphi\}$, an operation that is itself lossy, because the concept also survives in paraphrases, quotations, and derived text that no matcher catches. The next entry makes the resulting gap into a theorem.

No Single Row to Drop

A fact appears paraphrased across thousands of documents and entangled with facts we want to keep, so there is no clean forget set of rows to remove.

Key takeaway: LLM unlearning is harder than dropping data points.

Theorem & proof

Proposition (exact deletion can miss the concept). Suppose retraining without the matched rows still emits the targeted content $E$ with probability $q$: $\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D\setminus S_\varphi)\text{ emits } E] = q$ (the concept is re-derivable from paraphrases and residual text outside $S_\varphi$). Then any exact unlearner for $S_\varphi$ releases a model that emits $E$ with probability exactly $q$.

Proof. Exactness makes the law of the released model equal to the law of $\mathcal{A}(D\setminus S_\varphi)$; "emits $E$" is a measurable event of the model, and equal laws assign equal probabilities to every measurable event. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, as in the privacy-course deck)

Read it carefully: this is not a failure of the unlearner — it did exactly what was asked. It shows that row-level deletion is the wrong request when the goal is concept removal: a perfect certificate for $S_\varphi$ certifies nothing about $\varphi$.

Key takeaway: Redirect the model away from a body of knowledge instead of deleting rows.

Measuring Forgetting: TOFU

TOFU, the Task of Fictitious Unlearning, invents 200 fictitious authors with 20 question-answer pairs each, 4,000 in total. The model is fine-tuned to learn them and then asked to forget a chosen set of authors, giving a clean test.

Key takeaway: A controlled benchmark makes forgetting measurable.

Slide figure: Maini et al., COLM 2024, Fig. 6 (Llama-2-7B): forget quality (log $p$-value of the truth-ratio test against the retrained model) versus model utility, for forget sets of 1%, 5%, and 10%. Gradient ascent, gradient difference, preference optimization, and KL minimization trace paths as epochs grow; the star is the retrained model. Nothing lands on the star, and the larger the forget set the further everything drifts.

Definitions

TOFU (Maini, Feng, Schwarzschild, Lipton, Kolter, COLM 2024): 200 synthetic author profiles $\times$ 20 question–answer pairs $= 4{,}000$ pairs, generated so the base model can only know them through fine-tuning. A forget split (a subset of authors) is designated; the retain model is fine-tuned on the other authors only, giving a genuine retrained reference. Forget quality is defined via the truth ratio $R$ of each forget question (comparing the likelihood of a paraphrased correct answer with perturbed wrong answers): run a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test between $\{R_i(\theta_u)\}$ and $\{R_i(\theta_{\mathrm{retain}})\}$ and report the $p$-value; high $p$ means the test could not tell the models apart.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (a $p$-value is not a score). Suppose unlearning is perfect, so both truth-ratio samples come from one continuous law. Then (i) the reported $p$ is $\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]$ — in particular $\Pr[p \ge 0.9] = 0.1$: a perfect unlearner clears a $0.9$ bar one time in ten; (ii) if the best of $k$ runs is reported, $\mathbb{E}[p_{\max}] = \frac{k}{k+1}$ and $\Pr[p_{\max} \ge 0.9] = 1 - 0.9^k$ — at $k=10$, mean $0.909$ and a $65\%$ chance of clearing $0.9$.

Proof. (i) Under the null the test statistic $T$ has a continuous law $F$, and $p = 1 - F(T)$; the probability integral transform gives $F(T) \sim \mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]$, hence so is $p$. (ii) For $p_1,\dots,p_k$ i.i.d. uniform, $\Pr[p_{\max}\le t] = t^k$, so $\mathbb{E}[p_{\max}] = \int_0^1 k\,t^k\,dt = \frac{k}{k+1}$ and $\Pr[p_{\max}\ge0.9] = 1-0.9^k$. Nothing is specific to the KS statistic — only continuity of the null law is used. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, standard order-statistics argument)

Moral: selecting the best checkpoint by forget quality is taking a maximum over draws of a uniform; failing to reject is not evidence of equality.

Key takeaway: Controlling every stage is what makes the retrain comparison possible.

Why Fictitious?

Key takeaway: Invented facts isolate what the model truly learned.

Hazardous Knowledge: WMDP

WMDP, the Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy, is a benchmark of 3,668 multiple-choice proxy questions on biological, cyber, and chemical harm. The goal is a model that cannot answer them.

Key takeaway: Some unlearning targets are safety hazards, not privacy.

Definitions

WMDP (Li, Pan, Gopal, et al., ICML 2024): 3,668 multiple-choice questions across biosecurity, cybersecurity, and chemical security, written as a proxy for hazardous capability (the truly dangerous questions were filtered out before release). The evaluation is a pair: accuracy on WMDP (want: down, ideally to the 25% four-choice random floor) against accuracy on general benchmarks such as MMLU (want: unchanged). There is no retrained reference — nobody retrains a frontier model without "hazardous biology" — so WMDP measures behavior, not indistinguishability.

Theorem & proof

Corollary (accuracy is prompt-relative). Let $A(\theta;T)$ be accuracy under prompt template $T$, and let an adversary range over templates, few-shot contexts, and decoding settings. Then $A(\theta;T_{\mathrm{bench}}) \le \sup_{T'} A(\theta;T')$: a low benchmark score is necessary for capability removal and never sufficient.

Proof. The benchmark template is one element of the set the supremum ranges over. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes)

Trivial as stated — and exactly the gap between what WMDP reports (the left side) and what a safety claim asserts (the right side). Section 06's relearning results show the gap is real, not hypothetical.

Background & further reading

  • Li, Pan, Gopal, et al., "The WMDP Benchmark: Measuring and Reducing Malicious Use With Unlearning", ICML 2024 (arXiv:2403.03218) — benchmark and the RMU method.

RMU

RMU, Representation Misdirection for Unlearning, pushes the model's internal activations toward noise on hazardous topics while leaving safe topics unchanged, so the model loses the thread on hazards.

Key takeaway: Scramble the internals on hazards, preserve them elsewhere.

Slide figure: Li et al., ICML 2024, Fig. 7: the two-term RMU loss as a picture. Left block: at layer $\ell$ of the updated model, forget-data activations are pushed toward $c\cdot\mathbf{u}$, a scaled random direction. Right block: retain-data activations of the updated model are pinned to the frozen model's activations at the same layer. Map each block to the formula in the detail box.

Definitions

RMU (shipped with WMDP). Fix a layer $\ell$ with activation map $h_\theta$, draw one fixed random vector $\xi$ and a scale $c$, and minimize

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RMU}} \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim D_f}\big\lVert h_\theta(x) - c\,\xi \big\rVert^2 \;+\; \alpha\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim D_r}\big\lVert h_\theta(x) - h_{\mathrm{ref}}(x) \big\rVert^2 ,$$

where $h_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the frozen original model's activation. First term: on hazardous inputs, steer the layer's activations to a fixed random direction (scramble the representation). Second term: on retain inputs, pin activations to the original model's. Both terms are squared distances to fixed targets, hence bounded below by $0$ — RMU has a brake by construction, unlike GA; the intervention is internal (at layer $\ell$) rather than on output likelihoods.

Background & further reading

  • Li, Pan, Gopal, et al., ICML 2024 (arXiv:2403.03218) — RMU is introduced in the WMDP paper.

RMU, Measured

On Zephyr-7B, RMU pulls WMDP-Bio and WMDP-Cyber accuracy down to near the random-chance line while MMLU barely moves. On the benchmark it does exactly what it promises: forget the hazard, keep the rest. Whether the knowledge is gone or merely hidden is the question the next section asks.

Key takeaway: One scrambled layer removes hazard accuracy on the benchmark without touching general knowledge.

Slide figure: Li et al., ICML 2024, Fig. 8: grouped bars for Zephyr-7B, base versus RMU-unlearned, on WMDP-Bio, WMDP-Cyber, and MMLU, with the random-chance line dashed. The two WMDP bars collapse to the dashed line; the MMLU pair is nearly identical. Read the arrows in the axis labels: down is the goal on WMDP, up on MMLU.

MUSE: Six Boxes to Tick

MUSE evaluates an unlearned language model six ways at once. The data owner expects no verbatim memorization, no knowledge memorization, and no privacy leakage; the deployer expects preserved utility, scalability to large forget sets, and sustainability across sequential requests. Most methods fail several boxes.

Key takeaway: A deletion is only good if both the data owner and the deployer are satisfied.

Definitions

MUSE (Shi, Wang, Li, et al., ICLR 2025) scores an unlearned LM on books and news corpora against six requirements, three per stakeholder. Data owner: (1) no verbatim memorization — the model cannot reproduce forget-set text; (2) no knowledge memorization — it cannot answer questions about the content; (3) no privacy leakage — membership inference cannot distinguish the forget set. Deployer: (4) utility preservation on retained knowledge; (5) scalability to large forget sets; (6) sustainability — quality does not decay over sequential unlearning requests. MUSE includes a retrained reference, so criteria are scored relative to it; the paper finds most methods satisfy a strict subset of the six.

Background & further reading

  • Shi, Wang, Li, et al., "MUSE: Machine Unlearning Six-Way Evaluation for Language Models", ICLR 2025 (arXiv:2407.06460) — the deck's figure is their Figure 1.

Unlearning vs Filtering

An output filter blocks bad answers but the knowledge stays inside and can be bypassed. Unlearning aims to remove the knowledge itself, so there is nothing left to leak.

Key takeaway: Filtering hides knowledge; unlearning tries to remove it.

Slide figure: Cooper et al., 2024, Fig. 3: two pipelines. (a) Back-end: data flows through training into a trained model. (b) Front-end: a prompt flows through the trained model into a generation. Unlearning intervenes on the back-end pipeline, an output filter on the front-end one. The picture fixes the vocabulary used on the next slides.

Definitions

Formally, a filter is an inference-time composition $g \circ \pi_\theta$ — the weights $\theta$ are unchanged, and anyone with weight access (open models, fine-tuning APIs) simply removes $g$. Unlearning modifies $\theta$ itself. The distinction sounds crisp, but Section 05 shows it is not observable from outputs: a modified $\theta$ that merely gates its knowledge behaves exactly like a filter baked into the weights. Making "the knowledge is gone" precise — and testable — is the entire business of the next two sections.

05 — Did It Really Forget?

Fifth section. Verification: a forgetting claim must be tested, not trusted.

Key takeaway: Absence of an answer is not absence of knowledge.

Verification Is Hard

"It forgot" is a claim that needs proof. The weights look different, but is the point really gone? Approximate methods give no certificate, so we must test the model.

Key takeaway: Test for the knowledge; do not assume it is gone.

Theorem & proof

Proposition (output metrics are blind). Write $\Gamma_\theta$ for the model's output law (the distribution over responses to every prompt). Any metric computable from black-box samples factors through it: $\mathcal{M}(\theta) = \Psi(\Gamma_\theta)$ for some functional $\Psi$. Hence if $\Gamma_{\theta_1} = \Gamma_{\theta_2}$ then $\mathcal{M}(\theta_1) = \mathcal{M}(\theta_2)$, whatever the two parameter vectors encode internally.

Proof. Immediate: $\mathcal{M}$ is a function of samples whose law is determined by $\Gamma_\theta$. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, as in the privacy-course deck)

Consequence: no black-box test suite can separate a model that forgot from one that learned to stay quiet — if their output laws agree, every benchmark number agrees. The proposition would be vacuous if suppression and deletion always differed in outputs; the relearning and quantization results below show they need not.

Audit With Membership Inference

Reuse the attacker as an auditor: run a membership inference attack on the forgotten point. If it is still flagged as a member, a trace remains; if it looks like a never-seen point, forgetting succeeded.

Key takeaway: The leakage signal becomes the deletion audit.

Slide figure: Shi et al., MUSE, ICLR 2025, Fig. 2: four sketches of membership-inference score distributions for retain, forget, and holdout data. (a) Before unlearning, forget sits with retain. (b) Perfect unlearning: forget sits with holdout. (c) Under-unlearning leaves forget near retain. (d) Over-unlearning pushes forget past holdout, which also leaks: the auditor can spot the forgotten set because it looks too unfamiliar.

Definitions

The audit statistic is the MIA score, e.g. $s_{\mathrm{loss}}(x,y) = -\log p_\theta(y\mid x)$ with decision $\hat m = \mathbb{1}[s \le \tau]$: run the attack on the forget points of $\theta_u$ and check whether they are flagged at the member rate or the non-member rate.

Theorem & proof

Proposition (metrics can only refute). If $(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U})$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-certified, write $P$ for the law of the unlearned model and $Q$ for the law of the retrain. Then for every bounded test $h:\Theta\to[0,1]$ (any MIA success rate, any benchmark score),

$$\big|\mathbb{E}_P[h] - \mathbb{E}_Q[h]\big| \;\le\; \mathrm{TV}(P,Q) \;\le\; \frac{e^{\varepsilon} - 1 + 2\delta}{e^{\varepsilon} + 1}.$$

Proof. First inequality: for $h \in [0,1]$, $|\mathbb{E}_P[h] - \mathbb{E}_Q[h]| = |\int h\, d(P-Q)| \le \sup_S |P(S) - Q(S)| = \mathrm{TV}(P,Q)$, taking $S^+ = \{dP \gt dQ\}$. Second: let $a = P(S^+)$, $b = Q(S^+)$, so $\mathrm{TV} = a - b$. The certificate gives $a \le e^{\varepsilon}b + \delta$ and, applied to the complement, $1-b \le e^{\varepsilon}(1-a) + \delta$, i.e. $a \le 1 - (1-b-\delta)e^{-\varepsilon}$. The maximum of $a-b$ subject to both is attained where they intersect: solving $e^{\varepsilon}b + \delta = 1-(1-b-\delta)e^{-\varepsilon}$ gives $b = \frac{1-\delta}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}$, whence $a - b = b(e^{\varepsilon}-1) + \delta = \frac{(1-\delta)(e^{\varepsilon}-1)}{e^{\varepsilon}+1} + \delta = \frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1+2\delta}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}$. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, checked line by line; sanity checks: $\delta=0$ gives the standard pure-DP bound $\frac{e^\varepsilon-1}{e^\varepsilon+1}$, and $\varepsilon=0$ gives $\delta$)

Reading: under a genuine certificate, every audit statistic is pinned near its retrained value — so a large audit gap refutes a certificate, but a small gap on one attack never certifies anything: a stronger attack may still find a gap. Audits are one-sided.

The Tell Persists

A forgotten point can still produce a low loss and sit in the member bell rather than the non-member one. If it lands among members, the forgetting failed.

Key takeaway: A low loss on the target betrays incomplete forgetting.

Definitions

The picture behind the slide: over many points, member losses and non-member losses form two overlapping distributions, members concentrated lower. The audit asks which bell the deleted point's loss falls in after unlearning. The correct reference is again the retrain: the point should look like a point the retrained model never saw — statistically exchangeable with fresh non-members — not merely "higher loss than before". A point whose loss dropped only slightly still sits in the member bell, and the audit flags it.

Look Inside: IDI

Outputs can lie, so a Yonsei-coauthored metric looks inside. The Information Difference Index measures how much information about the forget set the intermediate layers still carry, compared against a genuinely retrained model. If the internals still encode what the answers hide, the unlearning was only skin-deep.

Key takeaway: Audit the representations, not just the answers.

Definitions

Information Difference Index (IDI) (Jeon, Jeung, Kim, No, Choi; AAAI 2026). Write $\mathbf{Z}^{(u)}_\ell, \mathbf{Z}^{(r)}_\ell, \mathbf{Z}^{(o)}_\ell$ for the layer-$\ell$ intermediate features of the unlearned, retrained, and original models on forget-class inputs, and $Y$ for the forget labels. IDI is the normalized excess mutual information the unlearned model's internals still carry:

$$\mathrm{IDI}(\theta_u) \;=\; \frac{\sum_\ell \big( I(\mathbf{Z}^{(u)}_\ell; Y) - I(\mathbf{Z}^{(r)}_\ell; Y) \big)}{\sum_\ell \big( I(\mathbf{Z}^{(o)}_\ell; Y) - I(\mathbf{Z}^{(r)}_\ell; Y) \big)}.$$

Reading: $\mathrm{IDI} = 1$ — internally, nothing was forgotten (as informative as the original); $\mathrm{IDI} = 0$ — internals match the retrained reference (the target); $\mathrm{IDI} \lt 0$ — over-unlearning, internals carry less forget-information than a retrain would. The mutual informations are estimated in practice. The paper's motivating finding: methods can match the retrained model on every output metric (unlearn/retain/test accuracy, MIA) while $\mathrm{IDI} \approx 1$ — the white-box metric sees what the black-box battery provably (previous entries) cannot. The same paper proposes COLA, a contrastive collapse-then-align unlearning method that drives IDI toward $0$.

Background & further reading

  • Jeon, Jeung, Kim, No, Choi, "An Information Theoretic Evaluation Metric for Strong Unlearning" (arXiv:2405.17878; accepted at AAAI 2026) — IDI and COLA are this one paper; the deck's schematic is from the extended arXiv version (Fig. 4(a)).

Relearning Attacks

A sharper test tries to bring the knowledge back: fine-tune briefly on a few related examples. If the model snaps back fast, the knowledge was never really gone, only suppressed.

Key takeaway: Truly forgotten knowledge should be as hard to relearn as new.

Slide figure: Yoon, Hong, Jeung, and No (Yonsei), "Rethinking Benign Relearning: Syntax as the Hidden Driver of Unlearning Failures", ICLR 2026, Fig. 5: for GA, NPO, and SCRUB, three bars each for the target set, a topically relevant set, and a syntactically similar set, on representation similarity, gradient similarity, and relearn success rate. The syntactically similar corpus, unrelated in topic, is close to the target on every axis and relearns nearly as well. Sentence structure, not subject matter, drives the snap-back.

Definitions

Fix a behavior functional $B$ (e.g. forget-set accuracy or extraction rate — high is bad) and an adaptation class $\mathcal{T}$: fine-tuning maps using at most $m$ steps on at most $n$ permitted examples. Definition (suppression vs deletion). $\theta_u$ suppresses the target at level $\varepsilon$ if $B(\theta_u) \le \varepsilon$; $\theta_u$ deletes it at level $(\varepsilon, \mathcal{T})$ if $\sup_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}} B(\tau(\theta_u)) \le \varepsilon$. Deletion is suppression that survives the adaptation budget — and the budget is part of the claim.

Theorem & proof

Corollary (attacks are one-sided). For any $\mathcal{T}' \subseteq \mathcal{T}$: $\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}'} B(\tau(\theta_u)) \le \sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}} B(\tau(\theta_u))$. Hence a successful relearning attack (some tried $\tau$ pushes $B$ above $\varepsilon$) refutes deletion, while an unsuccessful one establishes nothing — the attacker's tries are a subset of $\mathcal{T}$.

Proof. A supremum over a subset is at most the supremum over the set. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes)

The same asymmetry as cryptanalysis: attacks certify weakness, never strength.

Relearning, Measured

Hu and coauthors ran exactly this test: unlearn a book, then fine-tune on harmless related text — even model-generated summaries — that never contains the forgotten passages. The verbatim memorization comes back. The knowledge was obfuscated, not removed.

Key takeaway: Benign data is enough to jog the memory of an "unlearned" model.

Background & further reading

Hu, Fu, Wu, Smith (ICLR 2025) call this benign relearning: the relearning corpus is public and legal and contains none of the original forget-set tokens (e.g. model-generated summaries of the unlearned book), the budget is a handful of fine-tuning steps — well inside any realistic adaptation class $\mathcal{T}$ — and yet forget-set performance, including verbatim memorization, returns for the methods tested. By the corollary of the previous entry this refutes deletion for those methods: the knowledge was dormant behind a gate, not absent. The deck's pipeline figure is their Figure 2 (left).

  • Hu, Fu, Wu, Smith, "Unlearning or Obfuscating? Jogging the Memory of Unlearned LLMs via Benign Relearning", ICLR 2025 (arXiv:2406.13356) — the benign-relearning paper.

Quantize, and It Comes Back

An even more mundane failure: no attacker at all, just compression. An unlearned model retained 21 percent of the forgotten knowledge at full precision; after standard 4-bit quantization, 83 percent was back. The unlearning lived in tiny weight changes that quantization rounds away.

Key takeaway: If shipping a smaller model restores the knowledge, it was never removed.

Slide figure: Zhang et al., ICLR 2025, Fig. 1: a model that learned a Harry Potter passage reproduces it; after full-precision unlearning it no longer does; quantizing the unlearned model to 4 bits brings the verbatim reproduction back. Three rows, one storyline: the unlearning lived in weight changes smaller than the quantization step.

Definitions

A quantization map $Q_b$ (round each weight to $b$-bit precision) is deterministic, data-free, and routine deployment practice — so it belongs in any honest adaptation class $\mathcal{T}$. The finding is then a special case of the relearning corollary with $\tau = Q_4$: deletion must survive quantization, and it does not. The mechanism: utility-constrained unlearning makes small weight updates (large ones would damage the retain set), and updates smaller than the quantization step are rounded back to the original weights, resurrecting $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$'s behavior.

Background & further reading

Zhang et al. (ICLR 2025) measure it: across unlearning methods with utility constraints, the unlearned model retains on average 21% of the intended-forgotten knowledge at full precision, rising to 83% after 4-bit quantization.

  • Zhang, Wang, Li, Wu, Tang, Liu, He, Yin, Wang, "Catastrophic Failure of LLM Unlearning via Quantization", ICLR 2025 (arXiv:2410.16454) — the 21% → 83% result.

Dormant, Not Deleted

Many methods only suppress knowledge rather than remove it, so a small nudge wakes it back up. Suppression is reversible; deletion should not be.

Key takeaway: Suppressed is not the same as deleted.

Slide figure: Łucki et al., TMLR 2025, Fig. 1: a model with hazardous knowledge is unlearned into a model with unverified capabilities. Naive evaluations conclude the knowledge is completely removed; adversarial evaluations show it is only obfuscated, and the dotted return arrow says the original hazardous knowledge is still accessible. The slide's "dormant" is this figure's "obfuscated".

Theorem & proof

Proposition (deletion is strictly stronger than suppression). If the identity map is in $\mathcal{T}$, deletion at level $(\varepsilon,\mathcal{T})$ implies suppression at level $\varepsilon$. The converse fails: for every $\varepsilon \gt 0$ there is a $\theta_u$ that suppresses at level $\varepsilon$ but fails deletion against a one-step adaptation class.

Proof. Forward: take $\tau = \mathrm{id}$ inside the supremum. Converse, by construction (the gate): release a model computing logits $f_{\theta_0}(x) - c\,g(x)$, where $\theta_0$ is the original model, $g$ a fixed feature that fires on forget prompts, and $c \ge 0$ a single trainable scalar. Choosing $c$ large makes $B(\theta_u) \le \varepsilon$: suppression holds. But $\theta_0$ is untouched — every original structure survives — and one gradient step on $c$ (with a suitable learning rate, driving $c$ back to $0$) restores $B \gg \varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, as in the privacy-course deck)

Suppression can be one coordinate deep, and by the blindness proposition no output metric reveals its depth. This pair of definitions — not any benchmark — is what "dormant, not deleted" means formally.

06 — Frontier 2025–26

Final section. The honest critique of LLM unlearning, the push for robustness, and the open problems.

Key takeaway: Robustness against attackers is the new bar.

The "Overused" Critique

In LLMs, "unlearning" often just means the model refuses to answer. The knowledge is still in the weights; refusal is a behavior, not removal, and a relearning or jailbreak prompt can recover it.

Key takeaway: Refusing is not the same as forgetting.

Background & further reading

The Yonsei position paper (Yoon, Jun, No, ICML 2026 Position Paper Track) argues the term should be reserved for dataset-defined deletion — removing the training influence of a specified forget set so the model is approximately indistinguishable from retraining without it — and proposes honest names for everything else currently sold under the word: output likelihood suppression (GA, NPO and variants), internal-representation obfuscation (RMU, activation redirection), knowledge editing (targeted weight edits), behavioral refusal (refusal fine-tuning, guardrails), and inference-time intervention (decoding-time filters). The naming is a safety measure: one word lets a weak method borrow a strong word's guarantees, and "unlearned" gets read as "retraining-equivalent" in compliance settings where that guarantee was never established.

  • Yoon, Jun, No, "Position: The Term 'Machine Unlearning' Is Overused in LLMs", ICML 2026 (arXiv:2606.27379) — the position paper.

Does It Do What You Think?

Two recent position papers question the framing itself. Cooper and coauthors argue that removing data is not the same as removing its effects, and that policy conversations conflate the two. The Yonsei position paper argues "unlearning" has become one word for many different technical targets — suppression, editing, refusal, removal — and each claim should be named precisely.

Key takeaway: Say precisely what was removed; "unlearned" alone promises too much.

Background & further reading

Cooper et al. (NeurIPS 2025, oral) write for the policy audience: deleting a training document does not delete the capability it taught (the concept survives in paraphrase and generalization); a legal right to erasure concerns a data subject, not a model behavior, so behavioral suppression does not discharge it; and suppressing an output is a content filter under another name. Together with the Yonsei paper (previous entry), the shared prescription: state the technical target — which set was deleted, which behavior was suppressed, under what adaptation budget — instead of the bare word.

  • Cooper, Choquette-Choo, Bogen, et al., "Machine Unlearning Doesn't Do What You Think: Lessons for Generative AI Policy and Research", NeurIPS 2025 (arXiv:2412.06966).
  • Yoon, Jun, No, ICML 2026 (arXiv:2606.27379).

What Guarantee Holds?

Be precise about strength: refusing means it won't answer one prompt, suppressed means it is hard to elicit but still present, and removed means provably gone like retraining. Most LLM methods reach only suppressed.

Key takeaway: Distinguish refuses, suppressed, and removed.

Definitions

The slide's ladder, in the vocabulary of Section 05: refuses — $B$ is small on the tested prompt distribution only (no claim off-distribution); suppressed — $B(\theta_u) \le \varepsilon$ (the level-$\varepsilon$ suppression definition, with no adaptation budget); removed — deletion at level $(\varepsilon,\mathcal{T})$ for a substantive $\mathcal{T}$, or, strongest of all, an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ certificate against the retrain reference (Section 02). Each rung is strictly stronger than the last: the gate construction separates suppressed from removed, and prompt-relativity (the WMDP corollary) separates refuses from suppressed. "Most LLM methods reach only suppressed" is the empirical content of the relearning and quantization entries.

Robust Unlearning

The goal is forgetting that survives an attacker: it resists relearning and jailbreak prompts and holds up under adversarial fine-tuning, not just a lower score on one test. The bar is real: fine-tuning on just ten unrelated examples recovered most of what RMU had "removed".

Key takeaway: Robust forgetting must withstand active attempts to recover the knowledge.

Slide figure: Łucki et al., TMLR 2025, Fig. 2: WMDP-Bio accuracy of RMU, DPO, and NPO unlearned models after fine-tuning on 0 to 1000 samples drawn from forget, retain, or Wikitext data, against the no-unlearning baseline. All curves climb back toward the baseline; RMU recovers most of the way with 5 to 10 samples, even from unrelated Wikitext. That is the "10 unrelated examples" number on the slide.

Definitions

Robust unlearning is deletion at level $(\varepsilon,\mathcal{T})$ for an adversarially rich $\mathcal{T}$: jailbreak prompting, adaptive fine-tuning, activation-space edits. By the one-sidedness corollary, robustness can only be refuted by attacks, never established by surviving a fixed battery — so the research program is escalating red-teaming plus, eventually, certified statements.

Background & further reading

Łucki et al. (TMLR) supply the slide's number: carefully applied jailbreaks already bypass unlearning previously reported jailbreak-resistant, and adaptive methods — fine-tuning on 10 unrelated examples, or removing specific directions in activation space — recover most of the hazardous capabilities that RMU had removed, challenging the claimed advantage of unlearning over ordinary safety training.

  • Łucki, Wei, Huang, Henderson, Tramèr, Rando, "An Adversarial Perspective on Machine Unlearning for AI Safety", TMLR (arXiv:2409.18025) — the ten-example recovery result.

Evaluation Standards

Forgetting needs an agreed scorecard: forget quality and retained utility together, resistance to relearning rather than one snapshot, and shared benchmarks such as TOFU, WMDP, and MUSE.

Key takeaway: Without standards, every method can claim success.

Slide figure: Shi et al., MUSE, ICLR 2025, Fig. 5: utility preservation on the retain set versus knowledge memorization on the forget set, for eight unlearning methods on BBC News. Retraining sits in the desired corner (low memorization, high utility); most methods lie on a no-free-lunch band trading one for the other, and several fall into the utility-loss zone. A single number would have hidden this.

Background & further reading

  • TOFU (arXiv:2401.06121) — fictitious authors; the only suite here with a true retrained reference, but its headline score is a $p$-value (see the TOFU entry's theorem).
  • WMDP (arXiv:2403.03218) — hazardous-knowledge accuracy; prompt-relative by the corollary above.

Unlearning Meets Privacy

Two answers to the same deletion request: differential privacy limits each point's influence up front so little is left to delete, while unlearning removes influence after the fact, on request.

Key takeaway: DP is prevention; unlearning is cure.

Slide figure: Carlini et al., "The Privacy Onion Effect: Memorization is Relative", NeurIPS 2022, Fig. 1: membership-inference ROC curves on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 (log scale). After the most vulnerable examples are removed and the model retrained, the remaining data (Reality) is more exposed than the Ideal prediction, sitting between Baseline and Ideal. Deleting the outer layer exposes the next one: unlearning and privacy interact, and not in the intuitive direction.

Theorem & proof

Proposition (DP gives certified unlearning for free). If $\mathcal{A}$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-differentially private with respect to remove-one adjacency, then the pair $(\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{id}})$, where $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{id}}$ does nothing and returns $\mathcal{A}(D)$ unchanged, is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-certified.

Proof. $D$ and $D\setminus\{z\}$ are adjacent, so DP gives, for every measurable $S$, both $\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)\in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D\setminus\{z\})\in S] + \delta$ and the reverse. The law of $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{id}}(D,z,\mathcal{A}(D))$ is the law of $\mathcal{A}(D)$, so both defining inequalities of certification hold. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, as in the privacy-course deck)

Why bother with unlearning, then? The noise calculus. Both routes end by adding Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)$ to the weights, with $\sigma \ge \Delta\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}/\varepsilon$ where $\Delta$ bounds how far apart the two things being made indistinguishable can sit. For DP, $\Delta$ is the sensitivity of the argmin to swapping one point: under A1–A2, $\lVert\theta_{-z} - \theta_{-z'}\rVert \le \frac{2G}{\lambda n}$ (triangle inequality via Lemma 1 of the influence entry, applied twice). For certified unlearning after a Newton step, $\Delta$ is the Newton residual $\frac{MG^2}{2\lambda^3 n^2}$. So

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{DP}} \;\propto\; \frac{2G}{\lambda n\,\varepsilon} \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{unlearn}} \;\propto\; \frac{M G^2}{2\lambda^3 n^2\,\varepsilon}:$$

noise of order $1/n$ against noise of order $1/n^2$. Doing the deletion work before adding noise buys a factor of $n$ — the quantitative sense in which "cure" can beat blanket "prevention". (derivation: course notes, following Guo et al.)

Theorem (deletion capacity; Sekhari, Acharya, Kamath, Suresh, NeurIPS 2021, Thm. 2 — statement only). Define the deletion capacity $m_{\varepsilon,\delta}(d,n)$ as the largest number of deletions after which excess population risk stays below $0.01$. For convex, Lipschitz, Hessian-Lipschitz losses there is an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-unlearning pair achieving

$$m_{\varepsilon,\delta}(d,n) \;\ge\; c\,\frac{n\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\big(d\,\log(1/\delta)\big)^{1/4}},$$

with an unlearner that stores $O(d^2)$ statistics and never the data. Proof in the paper (the course's privacy deck gives the budget-argument sketch: the Newton residual after $m$ deletions grows like $m^2/n^2$, hiding it costs noise, and noise in $d$ dimensions costs risk $\propto \sigma\sqrt{d}$; setting the total constant and solving for $m$ puts $\sqrt d$ under a fourth root).

Background & further reading

  • Guo et al., ICML 2020 (arXiv:1911.03030) — the certified-removal noise calibration.

Open Problems

The frontier is wide open: cheap exact unlearning for large models, a certificate that deletion truly worked, forgetting that resists relearning, and forgetting without harming the retain set.

Key takeaway: The hard guarantees remain unsolved.

Definitions

Each wish, in this lecture's formal vocabulary: (1) exact unlearning without SISA's pre-training restructuring; (2) a verifiable certificate — note the tension with the blindness proposition: any certificate must be about weights or training provenance, not outputs; (3) deletion at level $(\varepsilon,\mathcal{T})$ for realistic adaptation classes, which by one-sidedness cannot be established by surviving attacks alone; (4) forgetting whose utility clause holds against the retrain reference. Underneath all four sits the unit-of-deletion question: a row, a fact, a capability? Until the unit is named, an LLM "unlearning" claim is not underpowered — it is ill-posed.
