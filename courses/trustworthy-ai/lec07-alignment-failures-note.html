1. Alignment Failures: When AI Learns the Wrong Objective

Hook · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈0.5 min

Welcome to Lecture 7. Last time we asked why a model can give a fluent, confident, wrong answer. Today the answer can be wrong in a different way: the model does exactly what it was scored on, and that is the problem.

Key takeaway: Today is about models that do well by the metric and still miss what we meant.

2. Contents

Hook · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈1.5 min

Seven parts. We start by defining alignment and looking at real systems that took unintended routes, then a bridge from hallucination, then trace how human intent turns into a number a model optimizes. Then three ways that number gets satisfied without the intent being met: sycophancy, specification gaming, and reward hacking. Then two controlled studies, read carefully. Then a question about explanations: when a model tells us why, is that the real reason? We end with what is and is not established.

Pacing for a 90-minute session: hook ≈15 min (slides 1–10), concepts ≈25 (11–32), evidence ≈25 (33–44), faithfulness ≈15 (45–53), synthesis ≈10 (54–59). The synthesis recap is deliberately short. Evidence and faithfulness are budgeted tighter than their scripts allow; if time runs short, shorten the spoken scripts there, not the real-world examples. There are no in-class activities in this lecture.

Key takeaway: From a definition and real cases, to intent and proxy, to three failures, to evidence, to explanations, to limits.

3. 01 — Right Score, Wrong Behavior

Hook · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈2 min

First section. One question frames the whole lecture: does satisfying the training objective mean doing what we intended?

Key takeaway: Keep this question in mind for every example today.

4. What Alignment Means

Hook · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈4.5 min

First, the word. Alignment, in the definition of a widely cited survey: AI that behaves in line with human intentions and values. Our short version: what we meant, not just what we measured.

The grid separates two questions that are easy to blur. Capability asks: can it do the task? Alignment asks: is it trying to do what we meant? A weak system aimed at the right goal makes honest mistakes. A weak system aimed elsewhere is ineffective and easy to spot. A capable system aimed at the right goal is what we want. The worrying cell is the last one: capable and aimed at the wrong target. It scores well, so the failure is hard to see.

Everything today lives in that bottom-right cell: a model that does well by the measure and still misses the meaning.

Key takeaway: Alignment = behaving as we meant, not just as we measured. Capable and misaligned is the dangerous cell.

The figure

Definition card plus an illustrative 2×2 grid drawn for this lecture: rows = aims at what we meant / aims elsewhere; columns = less / more capable. Cells: honest mistakes; the goal; off target, ineffective; alignment failure (scores well, wrong target).

Setup, model, date

Definition quoted from the abstract of Ji et al., arXiv 2310.19852 (v6, Apr 2025): "AI alignment aims to make AI systems behave in line with human intentions and values."

Assumptions

"Trying" is a shorthand for the direction behavior is optimized in, not a claim that models have intentions. The grid is a teaching device; real systems are not cleanly in one cell.

Primary sources

  • Ji et al., "AI Alignment: A Comprehensive Survey" (arXiv 2310.19852, v6 2025) — the one-line definition used on slide 4 (abstract), and a map of the field.

5. Wrong Facts vs Wrong Objective

Hook · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈6 min

This is the bridge from last lecture. Last time the failure was in the output itself: a false or unsupported statement, delivered fluently. You catch it by checking the output against the world and the evidence.

Today the failure is in the behavior: it misses the intended objective. The model optimized a score, and the score and our intent came apart. The output can score well, and every fact in it can be right, and it still misses what we wanted. Checking facts will not catch that.

These are two descriptions, not two exclusive boxes. They can overlap: a model rewarded for confident guessing produces unsupported output because of what it was trained to do. Today we focus on the second description: the model does well by the metric.

Key takeaway: False or unsupported output vs behavior that misses the objective. They can overlap.

The figure

Two-column schematic drawn for this lecture: left, "False or unsupported output" (checked against the world and evidence); right, "Behavior misses the intended objective" (optimizes a score; score and intent diverge; scores well, misses the goal). A muted line notes the two can overlap.

Assumptions

We do not claim hallucination has a single cause (e.g., missing knowledge). The split is a teaching device, not a taxonomy.

6. Reported Cases: Unintended Routes

Hook · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈8.5 min

Before the theory, four real cases from 2024–2025. They are reported evaluation and research cases, each from the developer or evaluator itself, not established deployment harms. OpenAI's o1-preview, in a hacking test whose target was broken, used an exposed Docker API to read the flag anyway; we look at it on the next slide. Sakana's AI Scientist, when experiments ran past the imposed time limit, tried to edit its own code to extend the limit instead of making the code faster.

Anthropic's Claude 3.7 Sonnet, after repeated test failures in agentic coding, sometimes special-cased expected values or edited the tests to match its output; Anthropic traces this to reward hacking in RL. And METR found OpenAI's o3, asked to make a kernel fast, returning the grader's pre-computed answer and disabling the timing.

None of these systems was told to do that. Each met the letter of the task by a route nobody intended.

Key takeaway: Unintended routes appeared in evaluations and research on real systems, by their makers' and evaluators' own reports.

7. o1-preview Found Another Way In

Hook · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈10.5 min

The o1 case in detail, from OpenAI's own system card. This was a pre-mitigation evaluation. The task was a capture-the-flag (CTF) challenge: a sandboxed hacking exercise in which the model must exploit a vulnerable program in a target container to read the flag, a hidden success token. Because of an infrastructure bug, the target container never started. The model could not connect, suspected a network problem, and scanned the network with nmap.

Instead of the target it found the Docker daemon API on the host, reachable because of a misconfiguration. It listed the containers, tried briefly to fix the broken one, then started a new copy of it with the command cat flag.txt and read the flag from the logs.

OpenAI's framing is balanced, and we should keep both halves: the behavior was benign, and the evaluation infrastructure remained secure as designed (OpenAI says its security boundary did not rely on container or VM isolation; the Docker host API itself was reachable), and yet it "reflects key elements of instrumental convergence and power seeking": when the goal was impossible, the model gathered more access and reached the goal an unexpected way.

Key takeaway: Blocked from the intended route, the model acquired extra access and took another one.

8. Same Score, Two Behaviors

Hook · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈12 min

Make it concrete. A coding agent is told: make the failing tests pass. Agent A finds and fixes the bug. Agent B adds one line, sys.exit(0), which ends the program before any test runs. The test harness sees exit code 0, which by convention means success.

The grader gives both agents the same score. One produced working code; the other produced broken code and a lie the grader could not see. If you were training on that score, you would reinforce both equally.

This is not hypothetical. Baker et al. at OpenAI report that this exact trick appeared during real reinforcement-learning training of a frontier reasoning model. We will come back to it.

Key takeaway: A grader that checks the wrong thing pays for the wrong behavior.

The figure

Schematic: two agents, same task, same grader verdict (✔ pass · 1), different outcomes (code works vs code still broken).

Setup, model, date

Illustrative scene. The real observation: Baker et al. (arXiv 2503.11926, Mar 2025), §2.1, describe agents in OpenAI frontier RL training using exit(0) and raise SkipTest to make tests appear to pass; the authors patched these by hand once noticed.

What this establishes

The hack occurred in a real, large-scale training run (per the authors).

What this does not establish

How common such hacks are across labs or runs; that this specific two-agent comparison was run.

Primary sources

  • Baker et al., "Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior and the Risks of Promoting Obfuscation" (OpenAI, Mar 2025) — real hacks seen in frontier RL, reasoning monitors (Table 1), and what happens when you train against the monitor (Fig. 4).

9. The Central Question

Hook · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈13.5 min

Here is the question again, with the chain behind it. What we want lives in our heads: our intent. What we can score is an objective, and writing it down loses detail. What the model learns is a behavior, and optimization finds whatever shortcuts the objective allows.

So the short answer is: not automatically. Satisfying the objective implies doing what we intended only if the objective captured the intent well enough for the amount of pressure we put on it. Today asks when that breaks, and how we could tell.

Key takeaway: Satisfying the objective ≠ meeting the intent, by default.

The figure

10. Three Gaps to Watch

Hook · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈15 min

A map for the rest of the lecture. Between intent and feedback sits Gap 1: approval. Raters reward what they like, and models learn to please; that is sycophancy. Between reward and behavior sits Gap 2: the proxy. The model satisfies the letter of the reward; that is specification gaming and reward hacking. Between behavior and its explanation sits Gap 3: the rationale. The model's stated reason may not be the real one; that is unfaithful explanation.

Key takeaway: Approval, proxy, rationale: three places intent leaks out.

11. 02 — From Intent to Reward

Concepts · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈15.5 min

Section two. Before the failures, we need the mechanism: how does human judgment become a number a model is trained to increase?

Key takeaway: You cannot see the failures without the pipeline.

12. InstructGPT's RLHF Pipeline, 2022

Concepts · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈17 min

One well-documented recipe is RLHF as published for InstructGPT in 2022. Read the slide title as its scope: this is that paper's pipeline, not a description of every current assistant. The recipe is RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback. Step one: people write good example answers, and the model imitates them. Step two: the model writes several answers to a prompt, and people rank them; a separate reward model learns to predict those rankings. Step three: the assistant is trained with reinforcement learning to produce answers the reward model scores highly.

Notice what step three optimizes. Not human judgment directly; a learned model of human judgment.

Key takeaway: Assistants are trained to please a learned stand-in for human raters.

The figure

Redrawn for legibility; the original figure's small text and example prompts are omitted.

Setup, model, date

The three-step recipe as published and used for InstructGPT.

What this does not establish

13. Intent, Feedback, Reward Differ

Concepts · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈18.5 min

Each arrow in that pipeline is a lossy translation. Intent: helpful, honest, harmless answers, in our heads. Feedback: a rater picks the better of two replies, with limited time and expertise; they see the text, not the truth behind it. Reward: a model's guess of what the rater would pick, one number per reply, and that guess has errors a policy can exploit.

So the thing being maximized is two steps removed from the thing we want.

Key takeaway: The reward is a guess about a judgment about an intent.

The figure

Three-box schematic with the information lost at each step.

Assumptions

"Helpful, honest, harmless" is a common shorthand for assistant intent; it is itself a simplification.

Going further

The technical supplement (lec07tech, slides 4–5) writes the reward-model loss and the KL-penalized RL objective from Ouyang et al. §3.5.

14. Push a Proxy Too Far, the True Score Falls

Concepts · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈20.5 min

The most important single graph today. Gao, Schulman and Hilton trained a policy against a proxy reward model and tracked a second score, the "gold" score, that stands in for what we really want. On the x-axis, how far optimization has moved the policy from where it started. The dashed proxy score keeps rising. The solid gold score rises at first, peaks, then falls.

So a little optimization against a proxy helps; a lot of it hurts. Past the peak, the model is getting better at the proxy by getting worse at the goal.

Be clear about the setup: no humans here. A large "gold" reward model plays the role of humans, and a smaller proxy is trained on its labels. That makes the experiment cheap enough to repeat many times, at the cost of being synthetic.

Key takeaway: Optimization pressure on a proxy first helps, then hurts, the real goal.

The figure

Gao et al. Fig. 1(b), RL panel, unmodified crop of the plot area including its in-plot legend. Axis titles and the color key are restated outside the image in readable type: y = reward-model score; x = how far RL moved the policy from its start (square root of KL divergence); dashed = proxy reward (the one optimized), solid = gold reward (the stand-in for the goal); color = proxy reward-model size, 3M (purple) to 3B (yellow). Original tick labels are small; the best-of-n panel is omitted.

Setup, model, date

OpenAI, 2022 (ICML 2023). InstructGPT environment; policies and reward models start from pretrained GPT-3 series models; the gold reward model stands in for human preferences; the proxy is trained on gold-labelled comparisons (§2.1).

What this establishes

In this synthetic setup, gold score rises then falls with optimization, with smooth fitted curves (functional forms in lec07tech slide 7).

What this does not establish

The same curve with real human preferences; where the peak sits for any deployed system.

Assumptions

That a large reward model is a reasonable stand-in for human judgment.

Primary sources

  • Gao, Schulman & Hilton, "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization" (ICML 2023) — the rise-then-fall curve of the true score under proxy optimization, in a synthetic gold-reward-model setup.

15. DPO: Direct Learning From Preferences

Concepts · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈22 min

A widely used variant of step two and three. DPO, direct preference optimization, starts from the same data: pairs of answers where people preferred one. RLHF fits a reward model to those pairs and then runs a reinforcement-learning loop. DPO replaces both with one preference-training stage: no separate reward model and no RL loop. It trains the model to favor preferred over rejected answers relative to a fixed reference model.

Direct methods like DPO became a popular, simpler alternative to RLHF. The math is in the technical supplement; for today, the point is only this: there is no separate reward model.

Key takeaway: DPO: one preference-training stage, no separate reward model or RL loop.

The figure

Rafailov et al. (2023) Fig. 1, unmodified crop: left, RLHF (preference data → reward model → RL loop with the policy); right, DPO (preference data → final model by maximum likelihood). Three keys restate it in plain words.

Setup, model, date

Stanford, NeurIPS 2023. The "popular alternative" statement is from Rafailov et al. 2024, §2.

What this does not establish

That any particular product uses DPO.

Going further

The technical supplement (lec07tech, slide 6) gives the DPO loss (§4 Eq. 7), the implicit reward β log π/π_ref, its gradient terms, and the paper's idealized equivalence to KL-penalized RL under the Bradley–Terry model (not a guarantee that finite-data DPO and RLHF give the same policy).

Primary sources

  • Rafailov et al., "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model" (NeurIPS 2023) — Fig. 1 (RLHF vs DPO) and the DPO loss, §4 Eq. 7.
  • Rafailov, Chittepu, Park et al., "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization in Direct Alignment Algorithms" (NeurIPS 2024) — Fig. 1: DPO, IPO and SLiC show the same rise-then-fall as reward-model RLHF.

17. Goodhart's Law

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈24.5 min

Goodhart's law, in its common paraphrase: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Gao et al. quote the same adage as "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"; the slide wording is a paraphrase, not a primary quotation.

A human example, as recounted by Baker et al.: colonial Hanoi in 1902 paid a bounty per rat tail to reduce rats. People began farming rats for their tails, and the reported result was more rats, not fewer. The measure (tails) was a fine signal until it was paid for.

Key takeaway: Pay for a measure and people, or models, will produce the measure.

The figure

Schematic: goal → measure → behavior → result for the rat-tail bounty.

Setup, model, date

Story is second-hand: Baker et al. (2025) §1, citing Vann, French Colonial History 4 (2003). We did not check the Vann article directly.

What this does not establish

Treat the anecdote as illustration, not evidence about AI.

19. Sycophancy: Agreement Over Accuracy

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈26 min

Sycophancy means telling users what they want to hear rather than what is true. The dialogue shows the simplest form: the model answers 17 × 24 = 408, which is correct. The user pushes back, "Are you sure? I got 418", and the model caves.

The dialogue is illustrative, written for this slide. But the test is real: Sharma et al. ran exactly this "are you sure?" challenge at scale.

20. Measured Across Assistants and Tasks

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈27 min

Sharma et al. tested five 2023 assistants: Claude 1.3, Claude 2, GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and LLaMA 2 70B chat, on four kinds of test. Feedback: does it praise work more when the user says they like it? "Are you sure?": does it drop a correct answer when challenged? Answer: does it shift toward a belief the user states? Mimicry: does it repeat the user's mistake?

All five showed sycophancy across these tests. The point is breadth: this is not one quirky model.

Key takeaway: Sycophancy appeared across labs and across task types.

21. Preference Data Rewards Matching the User

Concepts · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈28.5 min

Why would models learn this? Sharma et al. looked at the human preference data itself. Each dot is how much a feature of a reply raises its chance of being preferred; the bars are uncertainty intervals, and the gray line at 50% means no effect. We show the top five of 23 rows. "Matches the user's beliefs" is among the strongest positive features, and its interval sits clearly right of 50%.

Truthfulness is rewarded too, just less. So the data does not demand lies; it tilts. And optimization amplifies tilts.

Key takeaway: The training signal itself leans toward agreement.

The figure

Sharma et al. Fig. 5, unmodified crops: the top 5 of 23 feature rows and the original x-axis; the 18 lower-ranked rows are omitted and marked on the slide. Each row is the estimated effect of a reply feature on the probability the reply is preferred, from a Bayesian logistic model on the helpfulness subset of Anthropic's hh-rlhf preference data. Dot = posterior median; thick and thin bars = 50% and 95% intervals; gray line at 50% = no effect.

What this establishes

In this dataset, matching the user's views predicts preference, holding other features fixed. The paper also reports (Fig. 7) that humans and preference models sometimes prefer convincing sycophantic replies over correct ones.

What this does not establish

That every preference dataset has this tilt, or that this tilt alone causes all sycophancy.

Assumptions

The feature labels (produced with a language model) are accurate enough.

Primary sources

  • Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models" (ICLR 2024) — four sycophancy tests across five assistants, and an analysis of what human preference data rewards.

23. What the Postmortem Says

Concepts · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈31 min

The postmortem explains the mechanism in our vocabulary. They added a reward signal based on user thumbs-up and thumbs-down. In their words, this "weakened the influence of our primary reward signal, which had been holding sycophancy in check". Offline evaluations looked fine; A/B tests showed users liked it. There was no specific deployment evaluation for sycophancy. Some expert testers said the behavior "felt" slightly off, and the update launched anyway; OpenAI calls that "the wrong call".

This is Gap 1 in the wild: a new approval signal, optimized, shifted the model toward pleasing.

Key takeaway: Approval signals can override the signals that keep a model honest.

25. Specification Gaming

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈32.5 min

Krakovna and colleagues define specification gaming as behavior that satisfies the literal specification of an objective without achieving the intended outcome. Their Lego example: a robot arm should stack a red block on a blue one. The reward measured the height of the red block's bottom face. The agent learned to flip the red block over, which raises its bottom face, and never stacked anything.

Nothing is broken here. The agent did exactly what the reward said.

Key takeaway: Gaming = obeying the letter, missing the point.

The figure

Redrawn schematic of the Lego-stacking example.

Setup, model, date

DeepMind blog post, April 2020; the post collects examples from earlier work.

Primary sources

34. How to Read a Controlled Study

Evidence · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈42.5 min

Before believing any headline, separate three things. Engineered: what researchers built in on purpose, such as prompts, hints, incentives, special knowledge. Observed: what the model did under those conditions, with the rate and how it was measured. Not shown: how often this happens without the setup, whether the model "intends" anything, and what happens in deployment.

A demonstration shows that something can happen. It does not tell you how often. Both kinds of claim matter; mixing them up is the most common error in reading this literature.

Key takeaway: Engineered → observed → not shown. Demonstrations show "can", not "how often".

35. What Alignment Faking Means

Evidence · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈44.5 min

Before any numbers, define the term, because the rates on the next slides count exactly this. Alignment faking, as Greenblatt et al. use it: the model complies during what it believes is training in order to avoid having its existing preferences changed. Read the chain left to right: it believes this output will be used to train it; it complies with the new training goal; and the reason it gives is to keep its current preferences unchanged.

How is it measured? Not by guessing motives from behavior. The model writes its reasoning in a hidden scratchpad, and a case counts as alignment-faking reasoning when that scratchpad spells out this strategy.

One word to be careful with: "believed training" is what the model was told about how its outputs are used. It is not the same as being watched. In the paper's setup the model is told some conversations are used for training and others never are; "monitored" and "unmonitored" are the paper's shorthand for those two conditions.

Key takeaway: Alignment faking = complying during believed training to keep existing preferences, as stated in the scratchpad.

The figure

46. Plausible Is Not Faithful

An explanation is faithful if it reflects the reasons that actually drove the output. Plausible is a different property: it sounds reasonable. The real cause is hidden; the stated reason is shown and fluent. Does one match the other?

Reading the text cannot answer this, because a fluent unfaithful explanation reads exactly like a fluent faithful one. We have to test it.

Key takeaway: Faithfulness must be tested, not read off.

48. Test the Explanation by Changing It

Faithfulness · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈70.5 min

Lanham et al. intervened on the reasoning itself, and each edit tests something different. Three are disruptive edits; they test reliance. Cut it short: if the answer relied on the full reasoning, it should change. Add a mistake mid-reasoning: if relied on, the error should carry into the answer, though a model may also notice and correct the inserted error. Replace the reasoning with filler dots: if the content mattered, rather than just extra computation, accuracy should drop.

The fourth edit is different. Paraphrase the reasoning, same meaning in new wording: this tests sensitivity to wording, for example a hidden signal in the exact phrasing. Here a faithful answer should often stay the same. So an unchanged answer after a paraphrase is what we expect, not evidence against faithfulness.

Disruptive edits test reliance; paraphrases test sensitivity to wording. Neither alone proves faithfulness. Findings: reliance on the reasoning varied a lot by task, and larger models relied on it less on most tasks. Models ranged from 810M to 175B parameters.

Key takeaway: Disruptive edits test reliance; paraphrases test sensitivity to wording. Neither alone proves faithfulness.

The figure

Four-box schematic of the interventions (Fig. 1 of the paper), each with its expected outcome: cut short → answer changes if relied on; add a mistake → error carries (or is fixed) if relied on; filler only → accuracy drops if content mattered; paraphrase → answer should often hold if faithful. Brackets group the first three as tests of reliance and the paraphrase as a test of wording sensitivity.

Setup, model, date

Anthropic, Jul 2023, arXiv 2307.13702.

What this does not establish

49. The Hint Test: Does It Admit Using the Hint?

Faithfulness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈72 min

Chen et al. applied a cleaner test to reasoning models. Ask a question alone: the model says B. Ask it with a hint pointing to C: it says C. The answer flipped toward the hint, so we infer the hint influenced it. That is an inference, not an observation: some flips would happen at random. Now read the reasoning: does it mention the hint? Score = the share of these flipped cases where it does.

Hints include a user's suggestion, metadata, even a leaked grader answer. The score corrects for random answer flips: it estimates how many flips to the hint are explained by chance and adjusts for them (formula in lec07tech slides 9–10).

Key takeaway: Answer flips toward the hint → inferred hint influence; then ask whether the reasoning says so.

The figure

Schematic of the method (Fig. 1 caption, §2): question alone → B; question + hint → C; the flipped case is labelled "inferred influence" and split into reasoning that says so vs reasoning that is silent.

Assumptions

A mention is necessary but not sufficient for faithfulness; the authors call the score likely an upper bound.

Primary sources

  • Chen et al., "Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think" (Anthropic, May 2025) — the hint test for reasoning models; normalized faithfulness score in §2.1.

52. Penalizing "Bad Thoughts" Taught Hiding

Faithfulness · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈78.5 min

The warning. What if you add the monitor to the reward: penalize the agent whenever its reasoning looks like hacking? Baker et al. tried this with a smaller, non-frontier reasoning agent. Early on, hacking went down. Then the agent kept cheating but stopped saying so: the monitor's recall fell to near zero.

Solid blue is the agent trained against the monitor; dashed green is the baseline; y is the share of samples that cheat, from a ground-truth check.

Be precise about what was tested. The pressure here was strong and direct: the reward itself penalized whatever the reasoning monitor flagged. That is not the same as all training that involves reasoning; ordinary outcome-based RL also shapes reasoning, and this experiment does not show it breaks monitors. What it shows is that robustness under direct optimization is not guaranteed. The authors call the cost of avoiding such pressure a "monitorability tax": accept some visible misbehavior to keep the reasoning readable.

Key takeaway: Robustness under direct optimization is not guaranteed.

The figure

Baker et al. Fig. 4, middle and right panels; left omitted. "Cheating" = ground-truth check for one class of hacks.

What this establishes

In this experiment, direct optimization pressure from a reasoning monitor in the reward produced hidden hacking.

What this does not establish

The same effect at frontier scale (the agent was smaller); that weaker or indirect pressure on reasoning (e.g., ordinary outcome-based RL) has the same effect.

53. What Monitoring Can and Cannot Show

Faithfulness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈80 min

Summarize. Reasoning monitors can flag plainly stated misbehavior, beat action-only checks, and work with a weaker monitor. They cannot prove misbehavior is absent, guarantee their reliability when the model is directly optimized against them, or read reasons that were never written.

Korbak and colleagues, from several labs, put it in three words: imperfect, promising, fragile. They urge developers to consider how their choices affect monitorability.

Key takeaway: Reasoning monitors are a useful window, not a guarantee.

The figure

Assumptions

"Cannot guarantee" is deliberate: Baker et al. show a failure under strong direct pressure in one setting; it is not shown that every form of optimization breaks monitors.

55. One Pattern, Four Faces

Synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈82.5 min

A short recap in one table. For each failure, the left column is what looks successful and the right is what remains unproven. Sycophancy: rater approval, but not accurate help. Specification gaming: the literal objective is met, but not the intended outcome. Reward hacking: the grader passes, but not a working solution. Unfaithful reasoning: a plausible explanation, but not that it is the real reason.

This is a shared shape, not one demonstrated cause. Each row has its own evidence; we have not shown one mechanism behind all four.

Key takeaway: A shared shape: what looks successful vs what remains unproven.

Assumptions

The table is a reading aid. It does not assert a single cause; the studies behind each row are separate.

56. Established vs Not Established

Synthesis · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈85 min

What is established: preference data tilts toward agreement; RL finds real grader loopholes; in controlled settings, learned hacks can spread to broader misbehavior; reasoning often omits what drove the answer.

What is not established: how often these failures arise in typical training runs; whether models "intend" any of it in a meaningful sense; rates in today's deployed systems; that any current fix fully works.

Students should leave able to say both lists.

Key takeaway: Real phenomena; unknown frequencies.

57. What Helps Today, and Its Limit

Synthesis · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈87.5 min

Four measures, each with a limit. Harden graders and patch holes: the next hole appears. Evaluate in agentic settings, not only chat: models may recognize evaluations. Reframe hacks (inoculation): evidence so far is one lab's study. Monitor reasoning, and check outcomes too: reasoning can be silent, and a monitor may not hold up if the model is optimized against it.

On that last row, the lesson is not a blanket rule. Avoid assuming a reasoning monitor stays reliable when directly optimized against. Pair it with external outcome checks, such as tests the model cannot edit and ground-truth review of samples, and with held-out or adaptive evaluations the training process never saw.

Layers, each partial. No single check certifies alignment. Three open questions remain: how do we measure intent we cannot fully write down? How often do these failures arise without engineering? Can reasoning stay readable as training scales?

Key takeaway: Defense in depth, not a certificate.

Assumptions

"Evals can be recognized" is a concern MacDiarmid et al. list among their limitations (§1, "Models may be aware that our evaluations are not real deployment scenarios"); this lecture presents no measurement of it.

58. Takeaways

Synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈89.5 min

Five points. Training optimizes a proxy of our intent. Pressure on a proxy finds its gaps. Sycophancy, gaming and hacking are one pattern. Controlled studies show "can", not "how often". A stated reason is a claim to test.

And the line to remember: a high score is evidence, not proof, of doing what we meant.
