Front matter

Slide 1Title ~0:00

LLM Watermarking

Frame the lecture in one sentence before the contents slide: this is the provenance direction of the course, and unlike memorization or membership inference, here we control the generator.

"Everything so far has been about reading a signal out of a model somebody else trained. Today we plant the signal ourselves, and the question becomes how strong a statistical claim we can buy, and what it costs."

Slide 2Contents ~0:02

Contents

Seven sections. Point out that 01 is a framing section, 02–04 are three increasingly strong quality guarantees (biased, distortion-free, undetectable), 05 is where the guarantees break, and 06–07 are practice.

"Is this the same as the AI-detector tools schools use?" — No. Those are post-hoc classifiers with no calibrated false-positive rate. Say we will return to why that distinction is the whole lecture.

01 — Detection is a hypothesis test

Slide 3Section divider 01 ~0:04

Detection Is a Hypothesis Test

The organising claim of the deck. Every scheme in Sections 02–07 is a way of increasing the separation between two distributions, and every attack is a way of shrinking it.

Slide 4Motivation ~0:05

The Central Question

Contrast post-hoc detection with watermarking. The decisive difference is not accuracy, it is calibration: a watermark detector has a null distribution you can compute in closed form, so it can quote a p-value.

"A classifier that is 99% accurate tells you nothing about the one person you are about to accuse. A p-value of ten-to-the-minus-six does."

Slide 5Motivation ~0:07

Why Anyone Wants This

Four use cases. Flag that the last two — keeping synthetic text out of the next corpus, and catching distillation — require detecting the watermark after it has passed through training. That is radioactivity, Section 07.

Slide 6Threat model ~0:09

Two Adversaries, Two Errors

Removal raises type II, spoofing raises type I. Students usually only think about removal; spoofing is the reason schemes are keyed and why publishing the detector is dangerous.

Write the 2×2 confusion table once and label the two attacks as arrows into the two off-diagonal cells.

Slide 7Goals ~0:11

Three Design Goals

Detectable, quality-preserving, robust. Promise explicitly that Section 05 will show the three cannot be had simultaneously in the strong forms, and that the deck is organised by which one you relax.

Slide 8Recall ~0:13

Recall — Optimal Detection

Self-contained recall card: total variation is the best achievable advantage, and Neyman–Pearson says the optimal test thresholds the likelihood ratio. Do not re-derive; just re-anchor.

"We proved both of these when we did membership inference. I am restating them because watermark detection is the same game with different players: $P_1$ and $P_0$ are now induced by a secret key instead of by training-set membership."

Slide 9Figure · framing ~0:15

The Frame for the Whole Lecture

Two binomial-shaped densities, a threshold, shaded FPR and TPR. Every later result moves one of these curves. Point at the overlap and say the overlap is the residual entropy of the text.

"Why binomial and not Gaussian?" — It is binomial exactly, Gaussian asymptotically; Theorem 2 makes that precise, including the rate.

Slide 10Methodology ~0:17

What a Detector Must Report

Size, power, p-value. Repeat the low-FPR lesson from the membership-inference lectures: average accuracy and AUC hide exactly the regime that matters for accusation.

Slide 11Roadmap ~0:19

Roadmap

Four questions map to Sections 02–05. If time is short, Sections 06–07 can be compressed to their figure slides.

02 — The green-list watermark

Slide 12Section divider 02 ~0:21

The Green-List Watermark

The scheme everyone means when they say "LLM watermark". Announce the plan: rule, statistic, null, power theorem, cost theorem.

Slide 13Intuition ~0:22

The Core Idea — Tilt Toward Green

Use the coloured token strip. The essential point is that the partition is re-drawn every step from the preceding context, so there is no fixed set of banned words to discover.

"A human writing this sentence has no idea which half of the dictionary is green at each position, so on average they hit green half the time. The model hits it more. That gap is the entire watermark."

Slide 14Setup ~0:24

Setup and Notation

Fix $N$, $\gamma$, $\delta$, $\alpha = e^\delta$, window $h$. Warn that $\alpha$ will appear in every bound in this section.

Slide 15Definition ~0:26

Watermarked Sampling — The $\delta$-Tilt

Show both forms: logit shift and multiplicative tilt. The second equality is just factoring $e^{\delta \mathbb{1}[\cdot]}$ out of the softmax.

$\hat p(v) \propto \alpha^{\mathbb{1}[v \in G]} p(v)$ — write it this way and the rest of the section is algebra on a one-parameter exponential family.

Slide 16Figure ~0:28

What the Tilt Does

Bar chart before and after. Emphasise the near-deterministic case: if one bar already has mass 0.99, multiplying the others by $\alpha$ changes almost nothing. That is the seed of Theorem 4 and of the impossibility results.

Slide 17Proposition 1 ~0:30

Proposition 1 — Green Mass After the Tilt

State the Möbius map $\varphi_\alpha(g) = \alpha g/(1+(\alpha-1)g)$. Note the fixed points at 0 and 1: the tilt cannot create green mass out of nothing, and cannot exceed certainty.

Slide 18Proof ~0:32

Proof of Proposition 1

Three lines. Do it on the board rather than reading the slide; the only trick is splitting the normalizer into green and red and using $\sum_R p = 1-g$.

$\hat g - g = \dfrac{g(1-g)(\alpha-1)}{1+(\alpha-1)g}$ — the gain is largest at $g \approx 1/2$ and vanishes at both ends.

Slide 19Definition ~0:34

The Detection Statistic

Stress what the detector does not need: no model forward pass, no prompt, no logits. Only the key and the text. This is why it is deployable at platform scale, and it is also exactly why it is suboptimal (slide 24).

Slide 20Hypothesis ~0:36

The Null Hypothesis, Stated Precisely

Quote the paper's $H_0$ verbatim, then give the operational version: independent Bernoulli$(\gamma)$ indicators. Make students say out loud what independence requires — distinct seeding contexts.

"Independent of what randomness?" — Over the key. For fixed key and fixed text everything is deterministic; the null is a statement about the PRF output being unpredictable to someone who did not use it.

Slide 21Theorem 2 ~0:38

Theorem 2 — Why $z$ Is Standard Normal

CLT plus a Berry–Esseen rate. The rate is not decoration: a claimed p-value of $10^{-6}$ sits far in the tail, where a $O(T^{-1/2})$ uniform error can dominate the quantity being reported.

Slide 22Proof ~0:40

Proof of Theorem 2

Moments, Lindeberg–Lévy, Berry–Esseen. Highlight the $\gamma \to 0$ blow-up of $\rho$: aggressive small green lists give a bigger per-token signal but a worse Gaussian approximation.

"Small $\gamma$ is a coin that lands heads rarely. It carries more information per success, and it takes longer before you may pretend it is a Gaussian."

Slide 23Caveat ~0:42

When the Null Fails

Pseudorandom, not random. Repeated $n$-grams reuse the same green list, so indicators correlate and the false-positive guarantee evaporates. Both remedies are in the original paper; the de-duplication remedy returns as a theorem in Section 07.

Slide 24Optimality ~0:44

The $z$-Test Is Not Optimal

Connect back to the recall card on slide 8. The LRT would weight each token by its own likelihood ratio, which requires $p^{(t)}$ at detection time. The $z$-test is a deliberate trade of power for deployability.

$\log \Lambda = \sum_t \log \dfrac{\hat p^{(t)}(x_t)}{p^{(t)}(x_t)} = \sum_t \big(\delta \mathbb{1}[x_t \in G_t] - \log Z_t\big)$ — note it is a weighted green count, with weights $-\log Z_t$ that the $z$-test throws away.

Slide 25Definition 3 ~0:46

Definition 3 — Spike Entropy

Not Shannon entropy. It is a soft count of coordinates above $1/z$; the modulus $z$ chooses the resolution. Introduce it as "the entropy notion that happens to make the next proof work".

Slide 26Bounds ~0:48

Spike Entropy — The Two Extremes

Concave and symmetric on the simplex, so max at the centre and min at a vertex. The vertex value $1/(1+z)$ is the number to remember: it is the entropy floor that Section 05 turns into an impossibility statement.

Slide 27Theorem 4 ~0:50

Theorem 4 — Detection Power from Entropy

The central quantitative result of Section 02. Read the modulus $z^\star$ aloud and note it is determined by $\delta$ and $\gamma$ alone. Mean and variance are both controlled, which is what makes the $z$-test usable.

Slide 28Proof outline ~0:52

Proof of Theorem 4 — Outline

Four steps. The move worth naming is step 2: Jensen applied in the lower direction, exploiting convexity in the coordinates other than $k$ together with permutation symmetry.

"We are allowed to replace all the other coordinates by their average, and that only makes the bound weaker — which is exactly what a lower bound is allowed to do."

Slide 29Proof ~0:54

Proof — Steps 1 and 2

Define $f_k$, note symmetry and convexity in $p_{-k}$, flatten. Students should check that $\bar p_i = (1-p_k)/(N-1)$ is a probability vector.

Slide 30Proof ~0:56

Proof — Step 3

Pure algebra. The single non-obvious inequality is dropping $-\alpha$ from numerator and denominator, valid because the fraction exceeds one. Then divide by $1+(\alpha-1)\gamma$ and the spike-entropy summand appears.

Slide 31Proof ~0:58

Proof — Step 4

Average over $k$, multiply by $|G| = \gamma N$, sum over $t$. Variance: independent but non-identical Bernoullis, variances add.

Slide 32Numbers ~1:00

Reading the Bound

Two clean specialisations plus the paper's own worked example. All numbers on this slide are from Section 4.1 of the ICML paper; do not paraphrase them upward.

Slide 33Real figure ~1:02

Measured Power vs Length

Figure 3(b) of the original paper, $\gamma = 0.25$. Ask the room to read off the $\sqrt{T}$ growth and the $\delta$ ordering before you say it.

Slide 34Limit ~1:04

Low-Entropy Text Is Unwatermarkable

The curve hits chance level at zero entropy. Give the paper's own examples: a memorized quotation, a for-loop, a formatted date. Note that memorization and unwatermarkability are the same phenomenon seen from two sides.

"Can we not just raise $\delta$?" — No: at zero entropy $\varphi_\alpha(g)$ has $g \in \{0,1\}$, both fixed points. Slide 18 already proved the gain is $g(1-g)$-shaped.

Slide 35Theorem 5 ~1:06

Theorem 5 — The Quality Cost

Read the bound as: the expected cross-entropy of the tilted sampler against the raw model — i.e. log-perplexity — is at most $\beta$ times the raw entropy. Two sanity checks worth saying aloud. $\delta = 0$ gives $\beta = 1$ and equality. $H(p) = 0$ gives cost $0$, which is the previous slide's "unwatermarkable" claim seen from the quality side: no freedom, no damage, no signal.

"Is $\beta H(p)$ tight?" — No. At $\gamma \to 1$ every token is green, the tilt cancels, and the truth is $H(p)$ while the bound gives $\alpha H(p)$. It holds uniformly over entropy levels; it is not sharp.

Slide 36Erratum ~1:08

Proof of Theorem 5 — and a correction to the paper

The published statement has its inequality reversed. Kirchenbauer et al. print, as their Theorem 4.3, $\mathbb{E}_{G,R}\sum_k \hat p_k \ln p_k \le \beta P^\star$ with $P^\star = \sum_k p_k \ln p_k$, described as "the perplexity of the original model". But $P^\star = -H(p) \le 0$ and $\beta \ge 1$, so $\beta P^\star \le P^\star$: as printed, the theorem asserts that watermarking drives log-perplexity up to at least $\beta H(p)$ — a lower bound on the damage, the opposite of what the surrounding prose promises.

Their Appendix F derives the pointwise bound $\mathbb{E}[\hat p_k] \le \beta p_k$ correctly. The last line then multiplies it through by $\ln p_k$, which is negative, without flipping the sense. Flipping it gives $\mathbb{E}\sum_k \hat p_k \ln p_k \ge \beta P^\star$, equivalently the slide's $\mathbb{E}\big[-\sum_k \hat p_k \ln p_k\big] \le \beta H(p)$ — a real upper bound, and exactly the statement the paper's own text describes when it says a minimal-entropy step suffers no perplexity impact.

Two smaller slips in the same appendix, neither affecting the corrected result: Eq. (11) carries $\alpha p_k$ in the numerator for both colours, where the red-list term should be $p_k$ — which is what their own $(1-\gamma)p_k$ bound actually uses; and $\hat p_k$ is defined as an expectation and then has $\mathbb{E}_{G,R}$ applied to it a second time.

"The proof is two lines, and the second line is where the paper slips. Watch the sign of $\ln p_k$."

Slide 37Consequence ~1:08

Green-List Is Not Distortion-Free

The transition slide into Section 03. Averaging over the key does not undo the tilt, because $\varphi_\alpha(g) > g$ pointwise. So the marginal distribution really moved.

"So we paid for detectability with distribution. The obvious question is whether the payment was necessary — and the answer, surprisingly, is no."

Slide 38Real figure ~1:10

The One Knob: $\delta$

Figure 2 (left) of the ICML paper: $z$-score against oracle-model perplexity. Markers are $\delta$, colours are $\gamma$. The curve is the frontier that Section 06's quality-first decoders try to beat.

03 — Distortion-free sampling

Slide 39Divider ~1:12

Section 03 — Distortion-Free Sampling

Pause here. The section answers one question: can we get the detection signal without moving the output distribution at all?

Slide 40Motivation ~1:13

What Would "Free" Mean?

The sampler consumes randomness anyway. If we replace true randomness with pseudorandomness derived from a secret key, the marginal law is unchanged but the output becomes a function of the key.

"Nothing about the distribution changes. What changes is which of the equally likely continuations you get — and that choice is a signature."

Slide 41Recall ~1:15

Recall — The Gumbel Distribution

Self-contained card. Give all three representations; the proof on slide 48 uses the exponential one, the proof on slides 43–44 uses the CDF.

Write $\Pr[G \le g] = \exp(-e^{-g})$ and beside it $G = -\log E$, $E \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1)$. Leave both on the board for the next six slides.

Slide 42Theorem 6 ~1:17

Theorem 6 — The Gumbel-Max Trick

The statement card. Emphasise that the randomness has been moved out of the sampler and into an additive noise vector — the selection itself is now deterministic.

"Why is that useful?" — Because a deterministic selection can be reproduced by anyone who can reproduce the noise, i.e. by the key holder.

Slide 43Proof outline ~1:19

Proof of Theorem 6 — Outline

Three steps. Flag the substitution $u = e^{-m}$ as the only trick; everything else is bookkeeping.

Slide 44Proof step ~1:21

Proof — Step 1

The shift $\ell_v$ enters only through $e^{\ell_v}e^{-m}$. Say that out loud: it is why the product over $w$ collapses into $Z$ on the next slide.

Slide 45Proof step ~1:23

Proof — Steps 2 and 3

The integral is over "$M_v$ sits at $m$ and everyone else is below". After the substitution it is $\int_0^\infty e^{-uZ}du = 1/Z$.

Do the substitution on the board rather than pointing at the slide — the limit flip $(-\infty,\infty)\to(\infty,0)\to(0,\infty)$ is where students lose it.

Slide 46Corollary ~1:25

Corollary — The Max Carries No Information

The maximum is Gumbel$(\log Z, 1)$ regardless of the winner, so max and argmax are independent. Useful later: a detector reading the max value alone learns nothing about the token.

Slide 47Recap chain ~1:27

Three Faces of One Sampler

The chain that organises the rest of the section. Every scheme ahead — Aaronson, EXP, ITS, SynthID's ancestor — is one of these three written differently.

Slide 48Diagram ~1:29

The Picture

Bars are logits, stems are the added noise, the green stem wins. Make the point that a low-probability token can win — with exactly its own probability.

Slide 49Alt proof ~1:31

Alternative Proof — Exponential Minimum

The one-line proof for anyone who knows the exponential race. $\min$ of $\mathrm{Exp}(p_v)$ is $\mathrm{Exp}(\sum p_v)$ and the winner is $v$ with probability $p_v/\sum p_w$.

"Why teach both proofs?" — The integral proof gives the law of the max as a by-product; the race proof gives the intuition and the algorithm.

Slide 50Scheme ~1:33

Aaronson's Rule

Aaronson never published a paper; the rule circulated through a talk and blog post and was formalised as "EXP" by Kuditipudi et al. Say that explicitly — students will look for the citation.

Slide 51Proposition 7 ~1:35

Proposition 7 — Aaronson $\equiv$ Gumbel-Max

Take logs, note $\log r_v \lt 0$ so the map is monotone, and land on $\arg\min E_v/p_v$. The punchline is $-\log(-\log \mathrm{Unif}) \sim \mathrm{Gumbel}$.

Slide 52Definition 8 ~1:37

Definition 8 — Distortion-Free

Read the quantifiers aloud: probability over the key, fixed $\mu$, one token. This is the definition slide 53 will dissect.

Slide 53Theorem 9 ~1:39

Theorem 9 — Exponential Minimum Is Distortion-Free

Three lines, because slide 48 did the work. Point at the chain on slide 46 as you finish.

Slide 54Key distinction ~1:41

What Distortion-Free Does Not Say

The most-misunderstood point in the area. Distortion-freeness is a statement about the marginal over the key. Conditioned on the key, the text is a deterministic function of it — and that is precisely what the detector exploits.

"If the text were genuinely independent of the key, no detector could exist. The guarantee is about averaging, not independence."

Slide 55Fine print ~1:43

The Fine Print: One Key, Many Calls

Lemma 2.1 needs fresh i.i.d. key elements. Reuse the same $\xi$ and you get literally the same text twice — each marginal is right, the joint is absurd. The paper's fix is a random offset into a long key sequence.

Slide 56Scheme ~1:45

Kuditipudi — Inverse Transform Sampling

Key element is a pair: a uniform and a permutation. Stress the permutation's role — without it the scheme systematically favours low-index tokens and the detector's statistic becomes text-dependent.

Slide 57Theorem 10 ~1:47

Theorem 10 — ITS Is Distortion-Free

One interval, width exactly $\mu(y)$. Note the theorem holds for an arbitrary fixed permutation — which is why the practical scheme can share one permutation across positions.

Slide 58Detection ~1:49

Detection Without the Model

Big contrast with Section 02: this detector never queries the LLM. It correlates the key's uniforms with the ranks of the emitted tokens.

Slide 59Key trick ~1:51

The p-Value Is a Permutation Test

The test is exact for any statistic and needs no CLT, no independence assumption, no null model. That is the structural advantage over the $z$-test of slide 19 — at the price of $T$ resamples per detection.

"So why does anyone still use the $z$-test?" — Cost. One pass versus $T$ alignments, and the $z$-test needs no key resampling.

Slide 60Lemma 11 ~1:53

Lemma 11 — Why the Cost Separates

The expected gap is $C_0(1-p(Y_i|Y_{:i-1}))$: the model's uncertainty at that position. Same message as spike entropy, different functional.

Slide 61Definition 12 ~1:55

Definition 12 — Watermark Potential

Average of $1 - p(y_i|y_{:i-1})$. Say plainly that this is the distortion-free counterpart of spike entropy, and that Section 05 will turn it into an impossibility result.

Slide 62Lemma 13 ~1:57

Lemma 13 — The Clean p-Value Bound

Exponential in $k\alpha^2$, linear-in-$n$ union bound. Mention the EXP analogue (Lemma 2.8) so the two schemes stay parallel in the students' heads.

Slide 63Definition 14 ~1:59

Definition 14 — Cost Under Edits

Standard Levenshtein recursion with a per-token cost plugged in. Three branches: match, delete, insert. Only $\gamma$ is a design choice.

Slide 64Lemma 15 ~2:01

Lemma 15 — Edit-Distance Robustness

Read the exponent aloud: signal $\alpha(Y)$ minus damage $\gamma\varepsilon$, clipped at zero, squared, times the block size. Everything before the exponential is a union bound over alignments.

Slide 65Honesty ~2:03

Reading Lemma 15 Honestly

Quote the authors' own admission that the bound is vacuous for $\varepsilon = \Omega(1/\log k)$. This is the measurement-validity discipline: state what is proved, then state what is merely observed.

"A theorem that says nothing for realistic edit budgets is still worth stating — but you have to say that it says nothing."

Slide 66Real figure ~2:05

What Actually Survives

Figure 4(b), LLaMA-7B: median p-value against the fraction of substituted tokens. Contrast with the previous slide — empirics far outrun the proof.

Slide 67Definition 16 ~2:07

Definition 16 — Unbiased Reweighting

Hu et al. restate distortion-freeness at the level of distributions: a random map on the simplex whose mean is the identity. Same content, more convenient algebra.

Slide 68Constructions ~2:09

Two Unbiased Reweightings

$\delta$-reweight is inverse transform sampling collapsed to a point mass; $\gamma$-reweight shuffles, discards the bottom half of the CDF and doubles the top. Their Theorem 11 proves hard and soft red lists are biased — the formal version of slide 36.

Slide 69Theorem 18 ~2:11

Theorem 18 — From One Token to a Sequence

Per-token unbiasedness lifts to the sequence only if the codes are independent. Context-code collisions break that, so the scheme skips reweighting on any repeated context code. Same failure mode as the $z$-test's null on slide 23, fixed by bookkeeping rather than by assumption.

04 — Undetectability

Slide 70Divider ~2:13

Section 04 — Undetectability

Shift of register: from statistics to cryptography. Warn the room that the definitions get quantifier-heavy and that this is the point.

Slide 71Recall ~2:14

Recall — Indistinguishability and Post-Processing

Self-contained card. The privacy course used the statistical flavour; watermarking needs the computational one, because the two distributions must differ for detection to be possible at all.

"Why can't we just use total variation?" — Because TV zero would kill detection. The gap has to exist and be computationally invisible.

Slide 72Definition 19 ~2:16

Definition 19 — A Watermarking Scheme

Three algorithms. Stress what $\mathrm{Detect}$ does not get: no prompt, no model. That constraint drives every design choice in the section.

Slide 73Definition 20 ~2:18

Definition 20 — Empirical Entropy

Per-output, not per-model. Its expectation is the Shannon entropy. The substring version is what makes partial-text detection statable.

Write $H_e = -\log \Pr[x]$ next to $H = \mathbb{E}[-\log \Pr[x]]$ so the distinction is visible for the rest of the lecture.

Slide 74Definitions 21–22 ~2:20

Definitions 21–22 — Sound and Complete

Soundness is unconditional (no false positives, ever). Completeness carries the entropy side condition. Note the asymmetry out loud — students assume it is a technical convenience; slide 74 shows it is forced.

Slide 75Discussion ~2:22

Why Completeness Must Be Conditional

If the model is deterministic on a prompt, watermarked and clean outputs are the same string. Flagging it would violate soundness. Substring-completeness (their Definition 8) is the window version.

Slide 76Definition 24 ~2:24

Definition 24 — Undetectable

Adaptive oracle access to both the real model and the (possibly) watermarked one. Only $sk$ is secret. Contrast with Definition 8, which quantified over a single fixed $\mu$.

Slide 77Distinction ~2:26

Distortion-Free $\ne$ Undetectable

Two axes: exact-vs-computational, and per-token-vs-whole-transcript. Draw the two boxes and do not let the class collapse them.

Slide 78Separation ~2:28

Separating the Two Notions

Forward direction fails by key reuse: identical outputs on repeated prompts, each marginal still perfect. Reverse direction holds only computationally, by Lemma 31 on slide 91.

"Distortion-freeness is exact but local. Undetectability is global but computational. If a paper claims one and you need the other, read carefully."

Slide 79Construction ~2:30

Warm-Up Construction

Rejection sampling onto a pseudorandom $2^{-b}$ slice. The equivalence argument is the heart: if the oracle is never queried twice, it returns fresh randomness, so the loop is plain sampling.

Slide 80Why min-entropy ~2:32

Why It Needs Min-Entropy

The no-repeats condition is a min-entropy condition, not a Shannon one. Give the standard counterexample: half the mass on one string plus a huge uniform tail has large entropy and tiny min-entropy.

Slide 81Fix ~2:34

Removing the Min-Entropy Assumption

Watermark only the high-empirical-entropy branch, and do the case split on $p$. The implementation trick — sample once, compute $H_e$, then enter the loop — avoids ever computing $p$.

Slide 82Theorem 25 ~2:36

Theorem 25 — The Warm-Up, With a PRF

Worth dwelling on: the scheme's own running time $2^b$ bounds the security it may assume from the PRF, forcing $b = O(\log\lambda)$ and only weak soundness. Section 4 of the paper fixes this with a genuinely efficient construction.

Slide 83Scheme ~2:38

The Efficient Scheme, One Bit at a Time

Binary tokens for exposition. The key replaces the coin: $x_j = 1$ iff $u_j \le p_j(1)$. Point back to Theorem 10 — this is inverse transform sampling.

Slide 84Score ~2:40

The Detection Score

Two branches, both of the form $\ln(1/\text{tail})$. Emphasise that the detector needs neither prompt nor model, which is exactly what Definition 19 demanded.

Slide 85Proposition 26 ~2:42

Proposition 26 — The Score Gap Is Entropy

The single cleanest statement in the lecture: expected excess score equals $\ln 2$ times the entropy. Zero entropy, zero signal — not approximately, exactly.

Slide 86Proof ~2:44

Proof of Proposition 26

Two integrals of $\ln(1/u)$. The substitution $1-u \mapsto u$ in the second integral is the only step; then $\int_0^q \ln(1/u)du = q - q\ln q$.

Do $\int_0^q \ln(1/u)\,du = q - q\ln q$ on the board; students rarely have it memorised.

Slide 87Engineering ~2:46

From One Response to Many

Three escalating problems: fixed key too short, PRF on $j$ alone collides across responses, nonce unknown to the detector. The resolution — recover the nonce from the response's own high-entropy prefix — is why the scheme's first tokens are "spent".

Slide 88Theorems 27–28 ~2:48

Theorems 27–28 — The Main Results

$O(\lambda\sqrt L)$-complete and substring-complete. Read $b(L)$ as an entropy price. Then transition: the next section proves this price cannot be avoided.

05 — Limits and impossibility

Slide 89Divider ~2:50

Section 05 — Limits and Impossibility

The spine of the lecture. Everything before this built schemes; this section says what no scheme can do.

Slide 90Lemma 29 ~2:51

Lemma 29 — Model Entropy Is the Wrong Quantity

A one-paragraph proof with a two-branch model. High Shannon entropy, yet almost every actual output is the deterministic branch. This is why Definition 22 conditions on empirical entropy.

Slide 91Theorem 30 ~2:53

Theorem 30 — Low Empirical Entropy Cannot Be Watermarked

Counting argument: at most $2^t$ low-entropy outputs, so $\exp(t)$ queries suffice to estimate both distributions on all of them. Necessity of the entropy condition, proved.

"Does this apply to the green-list scheme too?" — The theorem is about undetectable schemes; but slide 34 gave the same conclusion for KGW from the spike-entropy bound. Two arguments, one wall.

Slide 92Lemma 31 ~2:55

Lemma 31 — Undetectability Is Only Computational

Enumerate keys, recover each accept set. Note the requirement that the key be of bounded size — the paper remarks on this explicitly.

"Any signal a detector can read, an unbounded adversary can read. The security is in the running time, nowhere else."

Slide 93Lemma 32 ~2:57

Lemma 32 — The Distortion-Free Impossibility

The statistical twin of Theorem 30. Sum of the two error probabilities bounded below by $\mathbb{E}[e^{-cm\alpha(Y)}]$ on the high-probability set $V_c$. As potential vanishes the bound tends to one.

Slide 94Real figure ~2:59

The Wall, Measured

Figure 11(a). Each dot is one generation. The empty corner — low potential, low p-value — is the theorem made visible.

Slide 95Proposition 33 ~3:01

Proposition 33 — Detection Costs Distortion

Not from the literature. This proposition was written and derived by Claude for this deck, by algebra on Theorems 4 and 5; it appears in no paper, and the slide says so. Both sides are the paper's own certified quantities, so the corollary is sound — but state its provenance aloud rather than letting it pass as published.

Note also what it does and does not say: $\mu^\star$ is the certified green rate, a lower bound, so the proposition caps how much detection the Theorem-4 certificate can promise per unit of Theorem-5 distortion. It is not a statement about the true green rate.

Slide 96Proof ~3:03

Proof of Proposition 33

Three lines. The only inequality is $S(p,z^\star) \le 1$; the rest is algebra eliminating $\alpha$ in favour of $\beta$.

Write $\alpha - \beta = (\alpha-1)(1-\gamma)$ on the board; that single identity is the whole proof.

Slide 97Diagram ~3:05

Reading the Tradeoff

Concave, saturating at $1-\gamma$. The red dot at $\beta = 1$ is the formal statement that a provably distortion-free green list has provably zero certified power. Practitioners live on the steep part of this curve.

Slide 98Theorem 34 ~3:07

Theorem 34 — Undetectable Implies Removable

The attack calls the watermarked model once per token, always taking the first token. Undetectability guarantees each conditional is right, so the assembled text is clean. Cost is linear in output length — expensive but not exotic.

"Is this practical?" — The paper's own estimate is that it multiplies generation cost by the output length. Impractical today, not impossible.

Slide 99Synthesis ~3:09

The Three-Way Tension

Each edge of the triangle is a theorem just proved. Leave this diagram up while you transition to Section 06 — every practical method there is a point inside the triangle, not at a corner.

06 — Robustness and semantics

Slide 100Divider ~3:11

Section 06 — Robustness and Semantics

Everything up to here assumed the detector reads exactly what the sampler wrote. Announce the shift: from now on an adversary sits between them.

Slide 101Diagram ~3:12

The Attack Surface

Walk the pipeline left to right. The dashed red box is the adversary; it has the text and no key. Five attack families, one budget parameter $\eta$.

Write on the board: $y$ has $n$ tokens, $u$ has $m$, edit distance $\eta$. Every robustness theorem in this section bounds $z_u$ in terms of $z_y$ and $\eta$.

"Why not just measure accuracy after the attack?" — Because accuracy depends on the threshold, and the threshold depends on the corpus. Bounding the statistic is corpus-free.

Slide 102Measurement ~3:14

What Paraphrase Actually Costs

Flag clearly that this is an empirical finding, not a theorem. The quoted sentence is the paper's own, verbatim: 800 tokens on average at a $10^{-5}$ false-positive rate after strong human paraphrasing; all attacks tested detected with certainty after 400–800 tokens.

The honest slogan is not “paraphrase breaks watermarks”. It is “paraphrase raises the price of a verdict from a tweet to a page”.

$z \propto \varepsilon\sqrt{T}$: halving the per-token signal $\varepsilon$ needs $4\times$ the tokens for the same $z$.

Slide 103Construction ~3:16

Fix the Green List

Motivate by failure first: in KGW, $G_t = \mathrm{hash}(x_{t-1})$. Edit $x_{t-1}$ and $G_t$ is a fresh random set, so the indicator at $t$ becomes a coin flip. One edit corrupts two positions — the edited one and its successor — and under a $k$-gram hash, $k$ successors.

The whole robustness gain is one sentence: make the indicator local.

"Can't the adversary just learn $G$?" — Yes, and that is the stated price. Frequency analysis on enough watermarked text recovers the list. Robustness against edits was bought with security against a corpus-collecting adversary.

Slide 104Theorem 35 ~3:18

Theorem 35 — Rényi Bound on the Tilt

This is where the differential-privacy toolkit pays off. The proof in the paper is explicitly borrowed from DP: the $\delta$-tilt is a bounded multiplicative perturbation, so a max-divergence bound of $\delta$ falls out, which is exactly pure DP's log-odds condition.

$\alpha \to \infty$: $D_\infty \le \delta \Rightarrow (\delta,0)$-indistinguishable. $\alpha = 1$ plus Pinsker: $\mathrm{TV} \le \min\{\sqrt{\delta/2},\delta/4\}$.

"Does this make it distortion-free?" — No, and press on this. $\mathrm{TV} \gt 0$ strictly. It is a uniform distortion bound, which the Section 02 quality theorem was not.

Slide 105Theorem 36 ~3:20

Theorem 36 — Power and Its Assumptions

State the bound, then spend the time on homophily. It says: raising a green token's probability now does not lower its probability later. Counter-examples exist — a model that must use each word once will repay the debt — and the paper argues this assumption is fundamental for any frequency-based watermark.

"Is homophily testable?" — Not directly. It is an assumption about the model's state transitions, not about the text. Worth saying out loud: this is the weakest link in the chain.

Slide 106Theorem 37 ~3:22

Theorem 37 — Surviving $\eta$ Edits

The restored survive-edits inequality. Emphasize the shape: the penalty is $O(\eta/\sqrt{n})$ while the signal is $\Omega(\sqrt{n})$, so a constant fraction of the text can be edited before the two meet.

$z_y \asymp (e^\delta-1)\sqrt{n}$ versus loss $\eta/\sqrt{n}$; set equal $\Rightarrow \eta \asymp (e^\delta-1)n$.

The paper reports this is twice as robust as the KGW soft watermark; that comparison is in their Appendix D, and it is a constant factor, not an order.

Slide 107Proof ~3:24

Proof of Theorem 37

Four steps, and step 2 is the whole idea: a completely arbitrary adversary is compressed into two numbers, because the statistic only sees the green count and the length. Do step 4's case analysis at whatever speed the room needs — it is bookkeeping, not insight.

$f(x,y) = (x-\gamma y)/\sqrt{y}$; $\partial_x f = 1/\sqrt{y}$, $\partial_y f = -\gamma/(2\sqrt{y}) - (x-\gamma y)/(2y^{3/2})$.

Slide 108Survey ~3:26

Three Ways to Make the Signal Survive

The point of the slide is the highlight bar, not the three cards. Only the fixed green list carries an edit-distance theorem. Say explicitly what each of the other two does prove: permute-and-flip proves a decoder-quality optimality and distortion-freeness, not robustness; the semantic hash proves nothing unconditional and inherits whatever stability the embedding has.

"So is the semantic hash worse?" — Not necessarily worse in practice; worse in what you can promise. That distinction is the lecture's habit.

Slide 109Restored ~3:28

Adaptive $\delta_t$

The restored adaptive-strength equations. Two things to make sure land: (i) the gate uses a separate measurement model that the detector also holds, otherwise the detector cannot reconstruct $W$; (ii) the perturbation is a temperature scaling $\hat\ell = \ell\,(1+\delta \hat v)$, not a uniform additive shift, so small logits stay small.

Additive: $\ell + \delta\mathbb{1}[v \in G]$ moves every token equally. Multiplicative: $\ell(1+\delta\hat v)$ moves the already-likely tokens most.

The detection score is the average of $\delta\hat v$ over gated tokens — an approximation to the LRT, which is the closest the deployed literature gets to Section 01's optimality question.

Slide 110Method ~3:30

Reading a Robustness Table Honestly

Same discipline as the unlearning lecture's measurement-validity section. Question 3 is the one students skip: comparing two schemes at equal $\delta$ compares nothing, because $\delta$ means different things in different constructions. Fix the perplexity axis first.

"What if the paper doesn't report perplexity?" — Then the robustness number is uninterpretable. Say so.

07 — Beyond standard LLMs

Slide 111Divider ~3:32

Section 07 — Beyond Standard LLMs

Three settings the standard framing does not cover: the watermark as a training-set tracer, watermarking at production scale, and watermarking without a causal context.

Slide 112Definitions 38–39 ~3:34

Radioactivity

The novelty is the input type: detection now takes a model, not a text. Make the inclusion condition explicit — “$B$ was not trained on $D$” must be contained in $H_0$, or rejecting $H_0$ tells you nothing about training.

$\alpha \approx 0.5$ means the observation is equally likely under both hypotheses; $\alpha = 10^{-6}$ means one in a million under innocence.

Slide 113Proposition 40 ~3:36

De-duplication Buys Back the Null

Call back explicitly to the earlier “when the null fails” slide. This is the same problem — correlated indicators from repeated context hashes — and here it has a clean fix promoted to a hypothesis of the proposition.

(1) $x_i$ pairwise distinct. (2) $x_i \notin c_i$. Then “not trained on $D$” $\subset$ “$S(X_N) \sim \mathrm{Bin}(N,\gamma)$”.

"Why does condition (2) matter?" — Because Alice crafts the contexts, and in the realistic setting those contexts are themselves watermarked. Without (2), $B$ can score high by echoing the prompt.

Slide 114Connection ~3:38

Why This Is a Membership-Inference Result

The key sentence: watermarking sidesteps calibration because Alice chose the feature, so the null is known in closed form. Contrast with the MIA lecture, where the whole difficulty was that per-example difficulty confounds the loss threshold and a blind baseline can beat a naive attack.

Read the table as a capability matrix, not a benchmark. The interesting cell is open-model unsupervised: MIA fails, the watermark works.

Slide 115Figure ~3:40

How Little Watermarked Data Is Enough

Two numbers, from two different settings — do not merge them. Open-model unsupervised: $p \lt 10^{-5}$ at $\rho \le 5\%$. Supervised closed-model: $p \lt 10^{-5}$ at $\rho = 1\%$.

The most important part of this figure is $\rho = 0$: all tests return uniform p-values. That is the calibration control, and it is what makes the rest of the curve worth reading.

Slide 116Figure ~3:42

Tournament Sampling

Trace one path through the bracket on the figure. The figure is drawn with $m = 3$ layers, hence $2^m = 8$ sampled candidates; deployment generally uses $m = 30$.

Seed $r_t = \mathrm{hash}(x_{t-4},\dots,x_{t-1};\,\text{key})$, i.e. $H = 4$.

"Is this the Gumbel trick again?" — Related but not identical. Gumbel picks one argmax over the whole vocabulary; the tournament runs a bracket over $2^m$ i.i.d. draws, which is what lets it stack $m$ independent bits of evidence per token.

Slide 117Definitions ~3:44

Score and the Non-Distortion Hierarchy

This slide is the payoff for Section 03's insistence on quantifiers. The paper explicitly says the phrase “non-distortionary” has been used for several distinct definitions in the literature and resolves it into a three-level hierarchy. Point out that our Definition 8 is their single-token level, and Kuditipudi's is closer to single-sequence.

"Why not always take the strongest?" — Because stronger non-distortion costs detectability and computation. They ship single-sequence.

Slide 118Setting ~3:46

When There Is No Previous Token

Set up the gap before showing the method: an ideal order-invariant predictor would make the decoding order irrelevant, but imperfect training makes real dLLMs order-sensitive. That gap is the channel.

$p(y\mid x) = \prod_i p(y_{\pi(i)} \mid y_{\pi(\lt i)}, x)$ for any permutation $\pi$ — in theory.

Slide 119Algorithm ~3:48

dgMARK

The one-line diff from generic decoding: restrict the argmax to positions whose candidate token already satisfies the parity condition, with a fallback when none does. Nothing is reweighted.

"If probabilities are untouched, is it distortion-free?" — Careful. The marginal over positions changes because the order changes, and order affects the conditionals in a real model. The claim is no explicit reweighting, which is weaker than Definition 8. Say that plainly.

Slide 120Detection ~3:50

dgMARK Detection

Close the loop with Section 01: this is literally the same one-proportion z-test, with $\gamma = 1/2$ and the indicator now a parity match rather than a green-list match. The interesting twist is the two-sided window statistic — edits shift indices and flip parity, so evidence can appear as a deficit as easily as a surplus, and squaring captures both.

$z_{\mathrm{win}} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_s z_s^2$ — a sum of squares, not a mean, because the sign is unreliable under index shifts.

Slide 121Closing ~3:52

Open Problems

Bullet one is the deck's own thread: we proved on the z-test slide that the deployed statistic is not the LRT, and nothing since has produced one. Invite students to treat that as a project.

Slide 122Closing ~3:54

Takeaways

Five lines, one per section arc. If time is short, the third is the one worth repeating: distortion-freeness is a statement about the marginal over the key, and it does not say the text is independent of the key given the seed.
