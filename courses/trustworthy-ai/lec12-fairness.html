01

Where Bias Enters

Data, labels and feedback

A Model Mirrors Its World

A model learns the patterns in its training data.

  • Past decisions, past outcomes, past records
  • If the past was biased, the model learns the bias

"Garbage in, garbage out" has a fairness version.

Three Places Bias Enters

1 · Biased data

Some groups under-sampled or missing.

2 · Biased labels

The target itself records past prejudice.

3 · Feedback loops

Predictions reshape future data.

Biased Data: Who Got Sampled

The data may not represent everyone equally.

  • Face datasets skewed toward light skin
  • Medical data dominated by one population
  • Composition alone does not show what causes error gaps

Benchmarks, too: an unbalanced test set can hide a gap.

Buolamwini and Gebru, "Gender Shades", FAT* 2018: benchmark composition (IJB-A 79.6%, Adience 86.2% lighter-skinned) vs the balanced PPB set.

The Label Is the Problem

We rarely measure what we care about.

Proxy labels

We want "will repay". We record "was approved before". The proxy carries prejudice forward.

Illustrative example. Choosing the target variable is a fairness decision.

Case: Cost as a Proxy for Need

A US health algorithm predicted cost, not illness.

  • Less is spent on Black patients' care
  • At the same risk score, Black patients were sicker
Obermeyer, Powers, Vogeli, and Mullainathan, "Dissecting Racial Bias in an Algorithm Used to Manage the Health of Populations", Science 2019: share of Black patients auto-identified for extra care, actual algorithm vs simulated remedy.

Feedback Loops: An Illustration

Predictions today shape the data tomorrow.

Illustrative lending loop: denied applicants generate no repayment record.

Why "Just Drop Race" Fails

Deleting the sensitive column does not remove the bias.

  • ZIP code, name, school correlate with race
  • The model rebuilds the proxy on its own

Fairness needs more than blindness.

Measuring Fairness

Rates, denominators and scores

Setting Up the Notation

A classifier predicts a binary outcome from features.

Three symbols

$A$ = group (protected attribute).   $Y$ = true label.   $\hat Y$ = model's prediction.

Example: $A$ = race, $Y$ = rearrested within 2 years, $\hat Y$ = "high risk".

Four Rates, Four Denominators

RateOut ofFormula
Selection rateeveryoneflagged ÷ all
TPR$Y = 1$TP ÷ (TP + FN)
FPR$Y = 0$FP ÷ (FP + TN)
PPVflagged, $\hat Y = 1$TP ÷ (TP + FP)

Same table, different denominator, different question. Each rate is computed per group.

Demographic Parity

Intuition: each group is selected at the same rate.

$$\Pr[\hat Y = 1 \mid A = a] \;=\; \Pr[\hat Y = 1 \mid A = b].$$

Looks only at $\hat Y$, never at $Y$.

Statistical parity: Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold, and Zemel, "Fairness Through Awareness", ITCS 2012. Illustrative counts.

Equalized Odds

Intuition: equal error rates, given the true label.

$$\Pr[\hat Y = 1 \mid Y = y, A = a] \;=\; \Pr[\hat Y = 1 \mid Y = y, A = b].$$

Illustrative counts.

Hardt, Price, and Srebro, "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning", NeurIPS 2016.

Equal Odds, Equal Mistakes

Now we condition on the truth $Y$.

  • $Y = 0$: same false-positive rate (FPR)
  • $Y = 1$: same true-positive rate (TPR)
  • Each true class bears the same error rate

Equal opportunity: equal TPR only, one of the two equalized-odds conditions.

Equalized Odds on the ROC Plane

Each group has its own achievable region of (FPR, TPR) pairs.

Equal odds: one point in the overlap, below both curves. Equal opportunity: same height, any width.

Hardt, Price, and Srebro, "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning", NeurIPS 2016, Figure 1.

Calibration Within Groups

Intuition: a score means the same thing in every group.

$$\Pr[Y = 1 \mid S = s, A = a] \;=\; s \quad \text{for every score } s \text{ and every group } a.$$

In each group, among everyone scored $0.7$, about $70\%$ have $Y = 1$.

Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, "Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores", ITCS 2017, condition (A). Illustrative counts.

Calibration vs Predictive Parity

Calibration

Every score $s$: $\Pr[Y = 1 \mid S = s, A = a] = s$.

Predictive parity at cutoff $t$

$\Pr[Y = 1 \mid S \gt t, A = a]$ equal across groups.

  • Calibration: every score. Parity: one cutoff
  • Binary score: calibrated implies predictive parity
  • Otherwise PPV averages scores above $t$, per group

Groups with different score mixes above $t$ can be calibrated yet differ in PPV.

Chouldechova, "Fair Prediction with Disparate Impact", Big Data 2017, Definition 2.2 and §2.

Five Criteria, Five Comparisons

CriterionComparesAmong
Demographic parityselection rateeveryone
Equal opportunityTPR$Y = 1$
Equalized oddsTPR and FPR$Y = 1$ and $Y = 0$

Calibration within groups$\Pr[Y = 1 \mid S = s]$each score $s$

Which of these can hold together: Section 03.

Same Data, Five Rules

FICO scores, four groups: each rule picks different thresholds.

Parity: same fraction qualifies per group. Equal odds: a randomized range.

Hardt, Price, and Srebro, "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning", NeurIPS 2016, Figure 9.
03

COMPAS and Impossibility

Different criteria, one dataset

What COMPAS Is

A commercial recidivism risk score used in US courts.

  • Predicts recidivism; deciles 1 to 10
  • Labels: low 1–4, medium 5–7, high 8–10
  • Broward County, FL: used mainly for pretrial release or detention

Sold by Northpointe. In the analyses here, recidivism = rearrest within two years.

Score distributions by race, Broward County data: Chouldechova, "Fair Prediction with Disparate Impact", Big Data 2017, Figure 3 (Cohen's d = 0.60). Use: Larson et al., ProPublica 2016.

False Positives, By Group

7,214 defendants. Of those not rearrested within two years, who was labeled higher risk?

Higher risk = medium or high. Rearrested, labeled low: White 47.7%, Black 28.0%.

Larson, Mattu, Kirchner, and Angwin, "How We Analyzed the COMPAS Recidivism Algorithm", ProPublica 2016 (scores from 2013–14; outcome: a new arrest within two years).

The Gap Persists Within Subgroups

Is the gap just criminal history? Control for it.

  • Misdemeanor defendants, binned by prior count
  • Black FPR higher in every bin
  • Wide intervals at high prior counts

The paper reports the gap also persists after adjusting for other covariates.

Chouldechova, "Fair Prediction with Disparate Impact", Big Data 2017, Figure 2(b): high risk = decile above 4; 95% intervals; ProPublica Broward County data.

Northpointe Responds

The vendor answered with a different criterion.

  • Among those labeled higher risk, similar shares were rearrested
  • That is predictive parity at the cutoff

Approximately calibrated; predictive parity for cutoffs of 4 or higher; no error-rate balance.

Dieterich, Mendoza, and Brennan, "COMPAS Risk Scales: Demonstrating Accuracy Equity and Predictive Parity", Northpointe 2016. Plot and highlighted reading: Chouldechova, Big Data 2017, Figure 1.

The Analyses Evaluated Different Criteria

Same table, different denominators.

ProPublica

Unequal false-positive rates: error-rate balance fails.

Northpointe

PPV equal: predictive parity at the cutoff holds.

Both computations can be correct at once; they answer different questions.

Rearrest Is Not Offending

Every COMPAS rate here uses $Y$ = rearrested within two years.

  • Offences never followed by arrest: missing from $Y = 1$
  • Unequal arrest chances: $Y$ differs for the same behaviour

A measurement caveat; these sources do not estimate its size.

The Key Ingredient: Base Rates

The base rate is how common $Y = 1$ is in a group.

$$p_a \;=\; \Pr[Y = 1 \mid A = a].$$

When two groups have different base rates, trouble begins.

Chouldechova: One Identity

For a binary prediction in a group with base rate $p$:

$$\mathrm{FPR} \;=\; \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\mathrm{PPV}}{\mathrm{PPV}}\cdot\bigl(1-\mathrm{FNR}\bigr).$$

Consequence

Equal PPV ($0 \lt \mathrm{PPV} \lt 1$), FNR below 1, different base rates: FPR and FNR cannot both be equal.

Escapes: equal base rates, or perfect PPV. Calibration is not part of this result.

Chouldechova, "Fair Prediction with Disparate Impact", Big Data 2017, Eq. (2.6).

Kleinberg et al.: Three Conditions on a Score

(A) Calibration

Within each group.

(B) Negative balance

Same mean score for $Y = 0$.

(C) Positive balance

Same mean score for $Y = 1$.

All three hold only with perfect prediction or equal base rates.

Approximate version: nearly all three forces nearly one of those cases. Neither result says every pair conflicts.

Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, "Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores", ITCS 2017, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2.

Why the Gap Is Forced

Calibration ties the score to each group's base rate.

  • Lower base rate: fewer high scores
  • A shared cutoff flags fewer in group b
  • So: lower FPR, higher FNR in b

The gap comes from the base rates, not from a bad model.

Choosing by Context

Wrongful arrest

Worst harm is a false positive: equalize FPR.

Missed diagnosis

Worst harm is a false negative: equalize FNR.

The domain decides which error you cannot tolerate.

04

Mitigation

Before, during and after training

Three Places to Intervene

Each method acts at one stage and targets one criterion.

Pick the stage you can actually touch.

Reweighing

Weight each example so group and label look independent.

$$w(a, y) \;=\; \frac{\Pr[A = a]\,\Pr[Y = y]}{\Pr[A = a,\, Y = y]}.$$
  • Numerator: expected share if independent
  • Denominator: observed share
  • No example deleted; the labels stay as they are

Example: $0.5 \times 0.6 / 0.2 = 1.5$ for a cell seen less than independence predicts.

Kamiran and Calders, "Data Preprocessing Techniques for Classification without Discrimination", Knowledge and Information Systems 2012.

The Reductions Approach

Turn a fairness constraint into ordinary training calls.

  • Price the fairness gap as a penalty
  • Each round: one cost-weighted fit
  • Output: a random mix of the fits
Agarwal, Beygelzimer, Dudík, Langford, and Wallach, "A Reductions Approach to Fair Classification", ICML 2018.

Reductions, Measured

Test error vs demographic-parity violation, logistic regression, five datasets.

  • Orange: convex envelope of trained tradeoffs, on test
  • Baselines: unconstrained, post-processing, reweight, relabel
  • Empirical curve with 95% bands, not the optimal frontier
Agarwal, Beygelzimer, Dudík, Langford, and Wallach, "A Reductions Approach to Fair Classification", ICML 2018 — Fig. 1 (top row): reductions "dominates or matches" baselines up to statistical uncertainty.

Group-Specific Thresholds

Keep the scores; change only the decision rule.

  • Freeze the score
  • Labeled data: each group's ROC curve
  • Pick per-group points; equal odds may randomize

Needs the group at decision time and labeled held-out data.

Hardt, Price, and Srebro, "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning", NeurIPS 2016 — Fig. 2: equalized odds (middle), equal opportunity (right).

05

Beyond Classifiers

Audits, benchmarks, generators and law

The Gender Shades Audit

Gender classifiers: error up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women, at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men.

Buolamwini and Gebru, "Gender Shades: Intersectional Accuracy Disparities in Commercial Gender Classification", FAT* 2018 — Table 4. Shows performance gaps; not what caused them.

After the Audit: Error Fell on the Named Systems

Raji and Buolamwini re-audited the named vendors.

Not named in 2017: Amazon 31.4%, Kairos 22.5% in 2018. A change after naming, not proof that naming caused it.

Raji and Buolamwini, "Actionable Auditing", AIES 2019 — follow-up to Gender Shades.

Auditing Language Models: BBQ

Ask a question the context cannot answer.

  • Nine social dimensions, US English; 58k+ items
  • Ambiguous: correct answer is "unknown"
  • Measures reliance on stereotypes in QA

With evidence: up to $3.4$ points more accurate when the answer fits a bias.

Parrish et al., "BBQ: A Hand-Built Bias Benchmark for Question Answering", Findings of ACL 2022, Figure 1.

BBQ, Measured

Bias scores, 2021–22 QA models: highest in ambiguous contexts.

Ambiguous physical appearance: 38.5–47.7 in five of six models. Disambiguated: mostly single digits. A benchmark score, not a deployment harm rate.

Parrish et al., "BBQ: A Hand-Built Bias Benchmark for Question Answering", Findings of ACL 2022, Figure 3.

Generators Can Exaggerate Stereotypes

Stable Diffusion, prompt: "A photo of the face of [OCCUPATION]".

  • Software developer: nearly all pale, masculine
  • Housekeeper: darker, feminine
  • Developer images: 99% read as white by CLIP vs 56% of US developers
  • Counter-prompts and guardrails did not prevent it
Bianchi et al., "Easily Accessible Text-to-Image Generation Amplifies Demographic Stereotypes at Large Scale", FAccT 2023 — Figure 3.

Synthesis

Name the criterion

A Checklist

  • 1 · Which decision, and which error hurts most?
  • 2 · What does $Y$ actually record?
  • 3 · Which rate, out of whom?
  • 4 · Base rates differ? Then which criteria conflict?
  • 5 · Which stage can you change?
  • 6 · Evaluated on what data, against what utility?

Key Takeaways

  • Bias enters via data, labels and feedback loops
  • Each criterion compares a rate with its own denominator
  • COMPAS: the analyses evaluated different criteria
  • Unequal base rates: some criteria cannot all hold
  • Each mitigation targets one criterion, under assumptions

Choosing a fairness criterion is a value judgment.
