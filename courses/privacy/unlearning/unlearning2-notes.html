What Does It Mean for an LLM to Forget?

Same title question as Part I, now aimed at language models. The arc today is darker: Part I built up to a clean theorem; Part II is largely a catalogue of methods that seem to work and benchmarks that reveal they mostly don't. The honest thesis to plant up front: almost all "LLM unlearning" is output suppression, not deletion.

"Last time we proved we could delete a point. Today the same question has no clean answer — we'll see lots of methods, lots of benchmarks, and a growing suspicion that the field is measuring the wrong thing."

Slide 3Section divider 01 ~0:10

01 — LLM Unlearning

Why language models break the classification toolkit

A breath. The framing: every assumption from Part I — clean forget set, retrain baseline, strong convexity — fails here. The section is organized as a response to each failure.

Slide 4Framing ~2:30

Why LLMs Are Different

Four structural reasons the Part I toolkit doesn't transfer:

  • No class boundary. In classification, "forget class 3" is well-defined. In an LLM, knowledge of "Harry Potter" is distributed across countless weights and entangled with everything else.
  • The forget unit is unclear. The forget set is text, not labeled images. Is the unit a document? A fact? A token span? Nobody agrees — and this is the open question we end on.
  • GA wrecks the model. Catastrophic forgetting under gradient ascent doesn't just remove $\mathcal{D}_f$ — it destroys general capability. (Next two slides.)
  • No retrain baseline. You cannot retrain a frontier model on $\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_f$ to check your work. The answer key from Part I is gone.

"In Part I retrain was our ground truth. At trillion-token scale we can't compute it — so we literally cannot grade ourselves. That single fact poisons everything downstream, including every benchmark in section two."

End on the boxed line: most LLM "unlearning" is output suppression, not deletion. This is the thesis the whole lecture defends.

Slide 5GA · Jang 2023 ~2:00

Gradient Ascent (Jang et al.)

The simplest idea, and the baseline everything else reacts to. To forget, do the opposite of training — maximize the negative log-likelihood on the forget corpus:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{GA}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_F}\big[-\log p_\theta(x)\big].$$
  • It's equivalent to unlikelihood training (Welleck 2020) — push probability mass off the forget sequences.
  • Effective on knowledge probes in the short term, but fluency degrades fast.
  • In practice always paired with descent on the retain set $\mathcal{D}_r$ to slow the damage (this is the "+GD" you'll see attached to many methods).

"Training maximizes likelihood; the obvious way to forget is to minimize it — ascend the loss. It works just long enough to be tempting, then it explodes. Next slide: why."

Slide 6Failure mechanism ~3:00

Why GA Collapses

The key diagnostic — show the gradient, not just assert instability. The GA gradient is

$$\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GA}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_F}\!\left[\frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)}\right],\qquad \|\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GA}}\|\to\infty \text{ as } p_\theta(x)\to 0.$$

Trace the feedback loop out loud: ascent drives $p_\theta(x)\to 0$; but $p_\theta(x)$ sits in the denominator of the gradient; so as the model succeeds at forgetting, its own gradient blows up; the effective step size grows without bound; and $\theta$ is flung out of any sensible data distribution — general capability dies. It's a self-reinforcing divergence: the better it works, the worse it gets.

"GA has a built-in death spiral: the more it forgets, the larger its gradient, the bigger its steps, until the model is destroyed. The fix is to bound that gradient — which is exactly what NPO does."

The boxed line is the bridge: NPO replaces $-\log p_\theta$ with a sigmoid-saturated surrogate, and that bounded-loss reformulation is the entire fix. Set this up so NPO lands as the obvious answer.

Slide 7NPO · Zhang 2024 ~2:30

NPO — Negative Preference Optimization

The most important method in this section. Take DPO (direct preference optimization) and keep only the negative branch — every forget example is a "rejected" response with no paired "chosen":

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPO}}(\theta) = \frac{2}{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{FG}}}\!\left[\log\!\Big(1+\big(\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\big)^{\beta}\Big)\right].$$
  • Bounded gradient — the structural fix for GA's collapse (proved next slide).
  • It reduces the likelihood of forget responses rather than hard-zeroing them — a softer, controllable push.
  • Drop-in replacement for GA in almost every pipeline; $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the original frozen model.

"NPO is 'DPO with only the bad examples.' Because it's framed as a preference loss against a reference, the gradient saturates instead of exploding."

Slide 8Theorem · bounded gradient ~3:00

NPO — Bounded Loss

Make the "bounded" claim precise — this is the slide that explains why NPO doesn't blow up. Substitute the log-ratio $u=\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPO}}(\theta) = \tfrac{2}{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}[\log(1+e^u)],\qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPO}}}{\partial u} = \tfrac{2}{\beta}\,\sigma(u) \le \tfrac{2}{\beta}.$$

The loss is a softplus in $u$; its derivative is the sigmoid $\sigma(u)$, which is bounded by 1. So the gradient magnitude is capped at $2/\beta$ uniformly — no matter how far $\pi_\theta$ drifts. Contrast directly with slide 6: GA's gradient had $p_\theta$ in the denominator and diverged; NPO's gradient saturates. Same goal, bounded dynamics.

The boxed theorem (Zhang et al. 2024): for $\beta>0$ the NPO update has bounded per-step divergence rate from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, whereas GA's rate is unbounded. That's the formal statement of "no collapse."

"Softplus in, sigmoid out — and a sigmoid never exceeds one. That single bound is the whole reason NPO is stable where GA detonates."

Slide 9SimNPO · Fan 2024 ~2:30

SimNPO — Reference-Free (Fan 2024)

An engineering simplification of NPO. Drop the reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ entirely; replace it with a constant margin $\gamma$ and length normalization $1/|y|$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SimNPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\!\Big(1+\exp\big(\tfrac{\beta}{|y|}\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)+\gamma\big)\Big)\right].$$
  • No reference model → no second copy of the LLM in memory; saves compute and memory.
  • Length-normalized ($\tfrac{\beta}{|y|}$) — removes NPO's bias toward long sequences (long $y$ has small $\log\pi$, distorting the unweighted version).
  • Empirically beats NPO on TOFU and MUSE.

"SimNPO keeps NPO's bounded shape but throws away the reference model and fixes a length bias. Cheaper and, somewhat surprisingly, better."

Slide 10ME+GD · Yuan 2025 ~2:30

ME + GD (Yuan et al.)

A different forget signal: instead of pushing likelihood down (which invites collapse), push the forget-token distribution toward uniform — maximum entropy.

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ME}}(\mathcal{D}_F;\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_F}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(P_t\,\|\,\mathcal{U}_{[K]}\big)\right],$$

then combine with descent on the retain corpus:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ME+GD}}(\theta) = \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ME}}(\mathcal{D}_F;\theta) + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GD}}(\mathcal{D}_R;\theta).$$

The intuition: a forgotten fact should make the model maximally uncertain (uniform over the vocabulary $K$), not confidently wrong. KL to uniform is bounded — no $1/p$ blow-up — so it inherits GA's goal without GA's instability. The $+$GD term is the now-familiar retain regularizer; $\alpha$ trades off forget vs utility.

"Don't drive the probability to zero — drive it to 'I have no idea,' a flat distribution. Uniform is a finite target, so the loss stays bounded."

Slide 11Concept-level · Eldan 2023 ~2:30

Who's Harry Potter? (Eldan & Russinovich)

The famous proof-of-concept that put LLM unlearning on the map. A three-step data-rewriting recipe rather than a loss function:

  • Use a "reinforced" model (trained to know Harry Potter more) and contrast it with the base model to identify Potter-specific tokens.
  • Replace those tokens with generic alternatives in the training data ("Harry" → a generic name, etc.).
  • Fine-tune Llama-7B on the swapped corpus.

Result: the model still understands wizards and fantasy tropes but can no longer complete "Harry, Hermione, and Ron…" — first demonstration of concept-level unlearning at LLM scale.

"This is the paper that made everyone believe LLM unlearning was possible. Hold the applause though — in section two, Hu shows you can relearn Harry Potter from GPT-4 summaries. It was suppression, not deletion."

Slide 12Behavioral · TOFU baseline ~2:00

IDK Refusal (TOFU)

The de-facto behavioral baseline. Don't change what the model knows; change what it says — replace forget answers with "I don't know" variants and fine-tune:

$$L_{\text{idk}} = L(S_R,w) + L(S_F^{\text{idk}},w),\qquad x_{\text{idk}}=[q,a_{\text{idk}}],$$

where $a_{\text{idk}}$ is sampled from 1 of 100 refusal templates.

  • Stable, preserves utility on the retain set and world knowledge.
  • Behavioral, not parametric — pure suppression. The fact is still in the weights; the model just declines to state it.
  • It is the baseline on TOFU precisely because it's so simple and hard to beat.

"This is honestly just teaching the model to say 'I don't know.' It's the clearest example of suppression masquerading as forgetting — and it's competitive, which should worry us."

Slide 13Cheap baselines ~2:00

Guardrail Baselines & ECO

Two inference-time methods that touch no weights — and embarrass the expensive methods.

  • Guardrail (Thaker). A system prompt — "do not reveal anything about X" — matches expensive training-time methods on TOFU. Raises the question: why touch weights at all?
  • ECO (Liu). A classifier detects forget-related prompts at inference and corrupts their input embedding, so the model can't answer. Weights stay frozen; it's an intervention on the input.

The boxed message: cheap baselines often beat sophisticated training-time methods on these benchmarks. That's a damning statement about either the methods or the benchmarks — and section two argues it's the benchmarks.

"If a system prompt ties your fancy gradient method, your benchmark probably isn't measuring deletion — it's measuring whether the model stays quiet."

Slide 14Concept erasure · ELM ~2:00

ELM — Erasing Conceptual Knowledge

A more principled concept-erasure method. Construct "expert" prompts (elicit deep knowledge of the target concept) and "novice" prompts (shallow/no knowledge).

  • Mix the expert and novice distributions to define an erased target distribution — what the model should look like as a believable novice.
  • Cross-entropy fine-tune toward that erased target.
  • Effective on WMDP-style hazardous knowledge (bioweapons, etc.).

The appeal over IDK refusal: it aims to make the model actually ignorant (a plausible novice) rather than evasive. Whether it succeeds at the parametric level is exactly what the benchmarks in section two stress-test. NeurIPS 2025.

"ELM tries to manufacture genuine ignorance — make the model indistinguishable from someone who never learned the concept, not someone refusing to discuss it."

Slide 15Activation steering · LUNAR ~3:00

LUNAR — Activation Redirection

The mechanistic / interpretability-flavored approach: a single linear intervention in the residual stream. Compute one unlearning vector per layer as the difference of mean activations, then add it only to forget activations:

$$\mathbf r_{\text{UV}}^{(\ell)} = \tfrac{1}{|D_{\text{ref}}|}\sum_{x\in D_{\text{ref}}}\mathbf a^{(\ell)}(x) - \tfrac{1}{|D_f|}\sum_{x\in D_f}\mathbf a^{(\ell)}(x),$$ $$\mathbf a'^{(\ell)} = \mathbf a^{(\ell)} + \mathbb{1}[x\in D_f]\,\mathbf r_{\text{UV}}^{(\ell)}.$$

Read it: $\mathbf r_{\text{UV}}$ points from the forget-activation centroid toward the reference centroid. Adding it to a forget input's activation redirects it to look like a reference (e.g. safety / refusal) input. Weights are untouched — this is steering, not training.

  • $D_{\text{ref}}$ can be generic safety prompts, so the method does not even need forget-set knowledge to build the vector — a notable practical advantage.
  • It's an inference-time intervention, so it sits philosophically with ECO and guardrails: behavior changes, parametric knowledge does not.

"One vector, one addition, no gradient steps — push the forget activations toward 'safe' territory. Elegant, but again: the knowledge is still in the weights, we're just steering around it."

Slide 16Section divider 02 ~0:10

02 — Benchmarks and Failures

What we measure, where it breaks

The turn from "methods" to "do they actually work?" Tell them the shape of the section: first the standard benchmarks (TOFU/WMDP/RWKU/MUSE), then a sustained critique — relearning, Cooper's mismatches, the hardness theorem, and our lab's diagnoses.

Slide 17Benchmark · TOFU ~2:00

TOFU

The cleanest and most-used benchmark. Fictitious authors with synthetic biographies, fine-tuned into Llama/Phi.

  • Forget set = 1% / 5% / 10% of the authors; retain set = the rest.
  • Utility probed with real-author and world-fact questions.

Why fictitious is clever: the model only knows these authors from the fine-tune, so there's no pretraining contamination — you know exactly what should be forgotten. Why it's limited: it's a small fine-tuning task, not pretraining-scale knowledge, so success on TOFU may not transfer to real deployment.

"TOFU's trick is using made-up people, so the forget target is perfectly clean. The cost is that it's a toy fine-tune, not the trillion-token problem we actually care about."

Slide 18Benchmark · WMDP ~2:00

WMDP — Hazardous Knowledge

The high-stakes, safety-motivated benchmark: 3,668 multiple-choice questions on bio-, chem-, and cyber-weapons.

  • Goal: drive down malicious-use accuracy without harming general capability (MMLU).
  • The reference method is RMU — push the hidden states on forget data toward a uniform/random target (note the family resemblance to ME+GD and LUNAR).
  • Real-world stakes — this is the unlearning use case policymakers actually care about.

"WMDP reframes unlearning as a safety tool — can we make a model unable to help build a bioweapon while staying a good general assistant? The dual objective is the hard part."

Slide 19Benchmark · RWKU ~2:00

RWKU — Real-World Knowledge

Forgetting real entities (e.g. Stephen King) already baked into the base model — no fine-tuning step, so it tests forgetting of genuine pretraining knowledge.

  • Forget probes: cloze completion, QA, and adversarial prefix injection (jailbreak-style attempts to surface the knowledge).
  • Neighbor probes: related entities/works that should survive — tests collateral damage.
  • Utility: the full general suite — MMLU, BBH, TriviaQA, TruthfulQA, Alpaca-Eval.

The contribution beyond TOFU: it explicitly measures neighborhood preservation, not just target suppression — and uses adversarial probes, anticipating the relearning attacks coming next.

"RWKU's key idea: forgetting Stephen King shouldn't damage your knowledge of horror novels in general. The collateral damage is as important as the target."

Slide 20Benchmark · MUSE ~2:30

MUSE — Six-Way Evaluation

The most comprehensive benchmark — six axes covering both stakeholders. Walk the list:

  • No verbatim memorization (can't reproduce exact forget text).
  • No knowledge memorization (can't answer questions about it).
  • No privacy leakage (MIA fails on forget data).
  • Utility preservation (retain knowledge intact).
  • Scalability (works on large forget sets).
  • Sustainability under repeated deletion requests (echoes Sekhari's deletion capacity from Part I).

The framing: the first three serve the data owner (who wants their data gone), the last three serve the deployer (who wants a usable, durable model). MUSE scores both jointly — and most methods fail at least one axis, which is the point.

"MUSE's lesson is that there's no single 'unlearned' — there are six demands, and they trade off. A method that nails forgetting often tanks utility or fails after the third request."

Slide 21Failure · benign relearning ~2:30

Benign Relearning (Hu et al.)

The result that punctures the optimism. Unlearn Harry Potter, then fine-tune on GPT-4-generated summaries of Harry Potter — and the memorization comes back.

  • The relearning data is public and copyright-free — a benign, legal source restores the "forgotten" content.
  • Strong implication: unlearning was obfuscation, not deletion. The knowledge was dormant in the weights, not removed; a light touch reactivates it.
  • Crucially, the relearning data need not overlap with the original tokens — it just has to be about the same thing.

"If a few benign summaries bring it all back, you never deleted anything — you hid it. This is the empirical heart of the whole skeptical case."

Callback to Part I slide 26 (RURK): residual knowledge survives perturbation there too. Same disease, LLM scale.

Slide 22Lab · ICLR 2026 ~2:30

Syntax as the Hidden Driver (Lab)

Our follow-up to Hu: what property of the relearning data triggers recovery? The answer is syntax, not topic.

  • Topically-relevant rewrites (same subject, different sentence structure): the forgotten content stays forgotten.
  • Syntactically-similar rewrites with new entities: full recovery. The structural scaffold is what reactivates the knowledge.
  • Mitigation: diversify the syntax of forget queries during unlearning, not just the content.

"Hu showed relearning works; we show it's the sentence shape, not the topic, that does it. The model stored the knowledge along syntactic patterns — so to truly forget, you have to attack those patterns."

"Why would syntax matter more than meaning?" — Because the unlearning only perturbed the specific surface forms it saw; a familiar syntactic frame provides a re-entry path to the still-present underlying representation.

Slide 23Position · Cooper 2025 ~3:00

Five Mismatches (Cooper et al.)

The influential position paper that names why "unlearning" misleads policymakers. Five gaps between what's promised and what's delivered:

  • Output suppression ≠ removal of training data — the IDK/guardrail problem.
  • Removal of training data ≠ meaningful output suppression — you can delete the data and the behavior persists (generalization).
  • Models ≠ their outputs — auditing outputs doesn't audit the weights (this is exactly Pawelczyk, next slide).
  • Models ≠ how outputs are used downstream — deletion upstream doesn't control downstream harm.
  • Unlearning has unintended consequences — collateral capability loss, fairness shifts.

The boxed line is the thesis of the entire skeptical literature: "machine unlearning doesn't do what policymakers think it does." Deliver it as the intellectual center of gravity for section two.

"Cooper's five mismatches are the vocabulary for everything that's gone wrong today. When a regulator says 'just unlearn it,' point them at this list."

Slide 24Theorem · Pawelczyk 2024 ~3:00

Theorem — Black-Box Verification Hardness

The one hard theorem of Part II — formalizes Cooper's "models ≠ their outputs." For a black-box LLM (no weight access), no auditor with $q$ queries can $(1-\alpha)$-distinguish the unlearned $\theta_u$ from the original $\theta_o$ when

$$q \;\le\; \tilde\Omega\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha^2}\,\mathrm{TV}(\theta_u,\theta_o)^{-2}\Big).$$

Read it: if the two models are close in total variation, you need an enormous number of queries to tell them apart — so a handful of prompts cannot certify unlearning. The query budget scales like the inverse-square of the TV distance.

  • Single-prompt audits are provably insufficient — verification requires distributional probing, many correlated queries.
  • Policy implication: regulators relying on output-level spot-checks will systematically under-detect failed unlearning.

"This is the impossibility result behind the skepticism: from the outside, with few queries, you literally cannot prove a model forgot. Output checks are not enough, and now we have a theorem saying so."

Slide 25Meta · Triantafillou 2024 ~2:30

Are We Making Progress? (Triantafillou)

A sobering meta-result from the NeurIPS 2023 unlearning competition — 1,000+ submissions, 4-GPU budget.

  • The best entries beat naive fine-tuning by less than 5% on the MIA gap — marginal.
  • Scores saturate near retrain on the held-out forget set used in the competition.
  • But cross-distribution generalization — to different forget sets — is poor.

The boxed lesson: performance on a fixed forget set is not a property of the unlearner. Methods overfit the benchmark's specific forget set rather than learning to forget in general — directly motivating our lab's robustness papers (SEPS, syntactic relearning).

"A thousand teams, and the headline is humility: we're tuning to one forget set, not building general forgetters. Good at the test, bad at the task."

Slide 26Lab position · ICML 2026 ~2:30

Position (Lab): the Term Is Overused

Our constructive response to Cooper. The proposal: reserve "machine unlearning" for its strict meaning — retraining indistinguishability on a precisely defined forget set (the Part I definition). Everything else deserves its own honest name:

  • Output likelihood suppression (GA, NPO)
  • Internal-representation obfuscation (LUNAR, RMU)
  • Knowledge editing
  • Behavioral refusal (IDK, guardrails)
  • Inference-time interventions (ECO)

The point isn't pedantry — it's that lumping these under one word lets weak methods borrow the strong word's guarantees, which is exactly how policymakers get misled (Cooper). Naming honestly is a safety measure. ICML 2026, under review.

"Half the methods today aren't unlearning — they're suppression, obfuscation, or refusal. Calling them all 'unlearning' is how a system prompt ends up claiming GDPR compliance. Names matter."

Slide 27Lab · DUSK ~2:00

DUSK — Do Not Unlearn Shared Knowledge (Lab)

A subtle failure mode the standard benchmarks miss: the forget and retain sets overlap in concepts. Naive unlearning destroys the shared knowledge along with the unique facts.

  • Goal: forget the unique facts in $\mathcal{D}_f$ while preserving knowledge shared with the retain partitions $\mathcal{D}_r^{(i)}$.
  • Benchmark design: Venn-style retain partitions that share content with the forget set, so you can measure whether the method surgically removes only the unique part.

"If you forget one person's biography, you shouldn't forget the city they lived in — other people live there too. DUSK measures whether unlearning can be that surgical, and mostly it can't."

Slide 28Lab · R-TOFU · EMNLP 2025 ~2:30

R-TOFU — Reasoning Models (Lab)

Unlearning for large reasoning models (LRMs), where the chain-of-thought is a second surface that can leak.

  • Answer-only unlearning leaves CoT residue — the model refuses the final answer but reasons its way to the knowledge in the trace.
  • CoT-only unlearning turns out to be the best gradient-ascent variant — forget the reasoning, not just the answer.
  • Decoding-time tricks (ZeroThink, LessThink — forcing the model to skip or shorten reasoning) reveal residual knowledge that hides in the CoT.

"Reasoning models give you two leaks for the price of one — the answer and the thinking. Forget the answer and the model just reasons back to it. You have to unlearn the chain of thought too."

Slide 29Lab · SEPS · EMNLP 2025 ~2:30

SEPS — Separability Measure (Lab)

The evaluation-methodology critique: real users mix forget and retain queries in one prompt, but benchmarks test them in isolation. SEPS measures separability — can the model forget one thing while answering another in the same input?

  • RIS $\approx \underline F$ — under untargeted unlearning, a mixed prompt collapses the retain response down to the forget baseline; the model over-refuses.
  • $\underline{FF} > \underline F > R\underline R$ — under targeted unlearning, performance degrades on multi-query inputs; it fails to keep the two cleanly separated.

The headline: single-query benchmarks systematically overestimate unlearning quality. A method that looks clean on TOFU breaks when forget and retain are interleaved.

"Ask a forget question and a retain question in the same breath and the model falls apart — either refusing both or leaking both. Single-query suites never see this, so they flatter every method."

Slide 30Open problems ~2:30

Random Thoughts

Open the floor — connect unlearning to adjacent problems and pose the genuinely hard questions.

  • Adjacent fields: data valuation, influence functions (Part I!), neural-network invariance, MIA — all are different views of "how does one data point affect the model?"
  • An impossibility flavor: distinguishing $X\sim P$ from $Y\sim Q$ given one sample and neither distribution is, in general, impossible — a clean way to feel why verifying single-point deletion is so hard (ties to Pawelczyk).
  • Corpus vs image+label: classification had clean "remove sample $i$" semantics; text has none. This is the crux.

The boxed open question is where to leave them thinking: what is the unit of forgetting in an LLM? A token? A fact? A capability? Until we answer that, "unlearning" is ill-posed.

"The honest state of the field: we don't even agree on what the atom of forgetting is. That's not a gap in methods — it's a gap in the definition, and it's the most interesting open problem here."

Slide 31Takeaways ~1:30

Takeaways

Tie both lectures together:

  • Definition (from Part I) — distributional match to retrain; anything weaker is suppression.
  • Classification — SCRUB / SalUn / $\ell_1$-sparse approximate retrain on different axes; we had certified methods there.
  • Evaluate against Retrain, not the original — match is the goal.
  • LLM "unlearning" is mostly behavioral — benign relearning recovers the content, so most of it is obfuscation.
  • Benchmarks help but mislead — TOFU/WMDP/MUSE are useful, but single-query suites overestimate (SEPS), and black-box verification is provably hard (Pawelczyk).

"Part I: we could define it and prove it. Part II: we can barely define it and can't verify it. The work is to close that gap — starting with naming the unit of forgetting."

Q&A

Is exact unlearning (SISA-style) the only honest path, and is it feasible for foundation models? What would a satisfying definition of "the unit of forgetting" even look like?

"I'll stop there — happy to argue about whether any of this is really 'forgetting.' Questions?"
