Time Horizons

The fit behind the headline number

The Fit Behind a Time Horizon

Task length $t$: geometric mean time of successful human baselines.

$$p_{\text{success}}(\text{agent}, \text{task}) = \sigma\big(\beta\,(\log h - \log t)\big), \qquad \sigma(z) = \tfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$$
  • At $t = h$: $\sigma(0) = 0.5$, so $h$ is the 50% horizon
  • $\beta$: how sharply success falls as tasks get longer
  • Like item response theory, but difficulty is fixed by human time

Assumes $t, h > 0$ and $\beta > 0$, so success falls as tasks get longer.

Kwa et al. (METR), arXiv 2503.14499, §3.1 and App. C.4. Fitted per agent: $h$ and $\beta$.

From the 50% Horizon to the 80% Horizon

For $0 < q < 1$ and $\beta > 0$, solve $\sigma(\beta(\log h - \log t_q)) = q$ for $t_q$ (natural log):

$$t_q = h \cdot \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\operatorname{logit} q}{\beta}\Big), \qquad \operatorname{logit} 0.8 = \ln 4 \approx 1.39$$
  • Reported: $t_{0.8}$ is 4–6 times shorter than $h$
  • Implied: $\beta \approx 1.39 / \ln 6$ to $1.39/\ln 4$, i.e. about 0.77 to 1.0
  • Flatter curve (small $\beta$): bigger gap between 50% and 80%
Kwa et al., §4 (80% horizon 4–6× shorter). The implied $\beta$ range is our arithmetic, not a reported value.

Doubling Time From a Log-Linear Trend

Regress log horizon on release date $d$ (in days):

$$\ln h(d) = \alpha + \kappa d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_{\text{double}} = \frac{\ln 2}{\kappa}$$
  • Reported $T_{\text{double}} = 207$ days, so $\kappa \approx 0.0033$ per day
  • Per year: $2^{365/207} \approx 3.4\times$
  • 95% CI 166–240 days: hierarchical bootstrap over families, tasks, runs

Extrapolating assumes $\kappa$ stays constant; the paper's one-month projection is conditional on that.

Kwa et al., §3.2 and §4. Per-year factor is our arithmetic.

Compounding, and Why Independence Is Wrong

Illustrative model: each step succeeds independently with probability $p$.

$$P(\text{all } n \text{ steps right}) = p^{\,n}, \qquad n_{50} = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln p}$$
  • $p = 0.95$: $0.95^{20} \approx 0.36$; $n_{50} \approx 13.5$ steps
  • Correlated errors: one misread task fails many steps at once
  • Recovery: a later step can repair an earlier one

Toy arithmetic only; not fitted to any agent.

The Control Game

Players, budget, and what safety means

The Control Game, Written Out

  1. Blue fixes protocol $P$: $U$, $T$ unlimited; $H$ on at most $aN$ of $N$ problems.
  2. Red team, knowing $P$, picks a stateless attack policy $\pi$ to replace $U$.
  3. Run all $N$ problems. Red wins if any backdoor is submitted.
  4. If $H$ confirms a backdoor, $P$ may shut $U$ down for the rest of the run.
$$\text{Safety}(P) = \min_{\pi} \Pr[\text{no backdoor submitted}], \qquad \text{Usefulness}(P) = \frac{c_P - c_T}{c_U - c_T}$$
Greenblatt et al., ICML 2024, §2: $N = 5{,}000$, $a = 2\%$, $U$ = GPT-4, $T$ = GPT-3.5. $c_X$: correctness rate ($c_P$ with honest $U$). Assumes $c_U > c_T$. In practice the min is over the red team's best attempts.

Why Upfront Auditing Fails

Audit the first $aN$ problems; attack each problem independently with rate $r$:

$$S(r) = 1 - (1-r)^{aN}\big(1 - (1-r)^{(1-a)N}\big)$$
  • Assumes perfect audits ($H$ catches any audited attack) and integer $aN$
  • Approximate optimum, large $N$: $r^* \approx \dfrac{-\ln a}{(1-a)N}$; attacks get rarer as $N$ grows
  • Then $S^* \approx 1 - (1-a)\,a^{a/(1-a)}$, the same for every $N$
  • $a = 2\%$: $S^* \approx 9.5\%$. Greenblatt et al.'s model, with honest errors, gives 15%
Closed form: Ge-Wang, Crosse et al., arXiv 2606.06529v1, App. A.1. Greenblatt et al., §3.1.2 (15%; 53% of $U$'s solutions correct; win probability constant at scale). $S^*$ is our simplification.

Deployment Safety With a Per-Task Hazard

Each task ends as caught ($g_c$), uncaught success ($g_u$), or continues ($g_0 = 1 - g_c - g_u$).

$$S_N = g_0^{\,N} + \big(1 - g_0^{\,N}\big)\cdot\frac{g_c}{g_c + g_u}$$
  • Lever 1: how often the attacker acts, through $g_0$
  • Lever 2: how attempts resolve, the catch ratio
  • Long deployments: $g_0^{\,N} \to 0$, so safety tends to the catch ratio

Assumes the same $g$'s every task, independent tasks, caught and uncaught success both end the run, and $g_c + g_u > 0$.

Ge-Wang, Crosse et al., App. A.2, eq. (4); simplified from Griffin et al. (2024).

Empirical Safety and Its Denominator

$$\text{Safety}_{\text{emp}} = \frac{|\text{caught}|}{|\text{caught}| + |\text{uncaught successful}|} \;\xrightarrow{\,n \to \infty\,}\; \frac{g_c}{g_c + g_u}$$
  • Independent of $g_0$: attacking more often does not move it
  • Selection does: attempts shift to tasks that are hard to catch
  • Aborts count as indecisive and leave the denominator
  • CV-tuned thresholds: read results as upper bounds on the drop

Needs a resolved attempt; the limit assumes a fixed outcome mix. Not deployment safety $S_N$.

Ge-Wang, Crosse et al., eq. (1), App. A.3, eqs. (5)–(6); §4.4.

A Toy Propagation Model

Not empirical: an intuition pump only

Toy Model: When Does an Infection Spread?

Not empirical

Not fitted to Prompt Infection or any other paper.

Random pairs talk; $i_t$ = infected fraction. Per round: infect w.p. $b$, forget w.p. $\gamma$.

$$i_{t+1} = i_t + b\, i_t (1 - i_t) - \gamma\, i_t, \qquad R_0 = b/\gamma$$
  • $R_0$ below 1: the infection dies out
  • $R_0$ above 1 and $i_0 > 0$: it settles at $i^* = 1 - 1/R_0$

Deterministic mean-field approximation; $0 < b, \gamma \le 1$. Finite groups can still die out.

What the Toy Model Does and Does Not Say

Suggests

Spread hinges on a threshold. Raising retention, i.e. lowering $\gamma$, can flip die-out into spread.

Does not show

Real $b$ or $\gamma$, real topologies, or where real systems sit.

Compare, qualitatively only: in Lee & Tiwari's simulated society, spread reached all agents only with inflated memory importance.

Standard SIS epidemic dynamics; Lee & Tiwari, arXiv 2410.07283v1 (preprint), Fig. 6. Mapping memory importance to $\gamma$ is our analogy.
