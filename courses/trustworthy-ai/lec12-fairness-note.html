1. Fairness

Where bias enters · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈0.5 min

Today is about fairness: where bias gets into a machine-learning system, how we measure it, and what it costs to fix it.

One idea runs through the whole lecture. Every fairness claim is a claim about a rate, and every rate has a denominator. When people disagree about whether a system is fair, they are often computing different rates.

Key takeaway: Every fairness claim is a rate with a denominator.

2. Contents

Where bias enters · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈1 min

Six parts. First, where bias enters. Second, how to measure it: four rates and five criteria. Third, the COMPAS case and the impossibility results it made famous. Fourth, three ways to mitigate. Fifth, what happens beyond classifiers: audits, language-model benchmarks, image generators and law. Last, a checklist.

Key takeaway: Measure, then understand the conflicts, then intervene.

3. Section 01 — Where Bias Enters

Where bias enters · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈1.5 min

We start before any metric: how does a model become biased in the first place?

Key takeaway: Bias usually arrives with the data, not the algorithm.

4. A Model Mirrors Its World

Where bias enters · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈2.5 min

A model learns patterns from its training data. That data records past decisions, past outcomes and past records. If those were biased, the model learns the bias, faithfully.

So the model is a mirror, not a judge. It does not decide that one group is worse; it reproduces what the data shows. That is why an accurate model can still be unfair: accuracy is measured against the same biased past.

Key takeaway: An accurate model reproduces the biases in its data.

The figure

Diagram: biased past decisions and records → training data → model predictions, with the same bias carried through.

Assumptions

The deck uses the mirror as an intuition, not as a claim about any specific system.

5. Three Places Bias Enters

Where bias enters · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈4 min

Three entry points. One: biased data, where some groups are under-sampled or missing. Two: biased labels, where the target itself records past prejudice. Three: feedback loops, where predictions change who shows up in tomorrow's data.

The next slides take each in turn with an example.

Key takeaway: Data, labels, and feedback loops.

The figure

Pipeline world → data → labels → model → decisions, with markers 1–3 at the data, label and feedback stages.

6. Biased Data: Who Got Sampled

Where bias enters · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈5.5 min

Who is in the data matters. Gender Shades measured two widely used face benchmarks: IJB-A was 79.6% lighter-skinned and Adience 86.2%. The authors built a balanced benchmark, the Pilot Parliaments Benchmark, at 53.6%.

Notice what this does and doesn't show. Composition alone doesn't tell you what causes an error gap. But an unbalanced test set can hide one: if almost every test face is lighter-skinned, a high overall accuracy says little about darker-skinned faces.

Key takeaway: An unbalanced benchmark can hide a gap.

The figure

Bar chart: share of lighter-skinned faces, IJB-A 79.6%, Adience 86.2%, PPB 53.6%, with a 50% reference line.

Setup, model, date

Buolamwini and Gebru, FAT* 2018. PPB has 1,270 individuals drawn from parliaments of six countries.

What this establishes

The two older benchmarks were heavily skewed toward lighter skin.

What this does not establish

That the skew caused the error gaps reported later (slide 47).

7. The Label Is the Problem

Where bias enters · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈6.5 min

Often we don't measure what we care about. A lender wants to know whether someone will repay. But for people who were never approved, repayment is never observed. If we use "was approved before" as the label, yesterday's decisions become today's ground truth.

So choosing the target variable is itself a fairness decision. The next slide shows a real case.

Key takeaway: The choice of label is a fairness decision.

The figure

Diagram: what we want (Y = will repay) vs what we record (Y = was approved before), joined by a proxy arrow.

Assumptions

Illustrative example, not a specific system.

8. Case: Cost as a Proxy for Need

Where bias enters · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈8 min

A widely used US health algorithm decided which patients got extra care. It predicted future health-care cost, as a stand-in for health need. But less money is spent on Black patients' care. So at the same risk score, Black patients were considerably sicker.

The authors simulated a remedy. The share of Black patients auto-identified for extra care would rise from 17.7% to 46.5%. The model wasn't miscalibrated for cost; cost was the wrong target.

Key takeaway: A proxy label can carry inequity straight into the model.

The figure

Two bars: 17.7% (cost proxy, actual algorithm) and 46.5% (simulated remedy), the Black share of patients auto-identified for extra care.

Setup, model, date

Obermeyer, Powers, Vogeli and Mullainathan, Science 2019, studying a commercial algorithm used by a large US health system.

What this establishes

Using cost as the label understated Black patients' need at a given score.

What this does not establish

The 46.5% is a simulation, not a deployed outcome.

Primary sources

  • Obermeyer, Powers, Vogeli & Mullainathan, "Dissecting racial bias in an algorithm used to manage the health of populations", Science 366 (2019) — abstract: at a given risk score Black patients are considerably sicker; remedying the disparity raises the Black share of auto-identified patients from 17.7% to 46.5%.

9. Feedback Loops: An Illustration

Where bias enters · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈9 min

A loop. Suppose a model denies loans to group A more often. Denied applicants never get a loan, so they never build a repayment record. The next model sees little evidence that group A repays, and denies them again. The loop closes.

This is an illustration. The point is that a model's decisions can create the data used to judge it.

Key takeaway: Decisions can create the data that later justifies them.

The figure

Cycle: deny group A more often → fewer loans, no track record → "data shows A is risky" → back to denial.

Assumptions

Illustrative lending loop; no specific system is claimed.

10. Why "Just Drop Race" Fails

Where bias enters · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈10 min

The obvious fix is to delete the sensitive column. It doesn't work. ZIP code, name and school can each be correlated with race, and a flexible model can rebuild the proxy on its own.

Worse, once the column is gone, you can no longer measure the gap. Fairness needs more than blindness.

Key takeaway: Deleting the attribute neither removes the bias nor lets you measure it.

The figure

Diagram: the race column is struck out; ZIP, name and school feed back into a rebuilt race proxy.

Going further

Several methods in Section 04 need the group label at training time (reweighing, reductions) or at decision time (group thresholds). Dropping it makes them impossible.

11. Section 02 — Measuring Fairness

Now we make fairness measurable. Everything in this section comes from one table and a choice of denominator.

12. Setting Up the Notation

Measuring fairness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈12 min

Three symbols. $A$ is the group, the protected attribute. $Y$ is the true label. $\hat Y$ is the model's prediction.

Running example: $A$ is race, $Y$ is "rearrested within two years", and $\hat Y$ is "labeled high risk". Every fairness criterion today compares something about $\hat Y$ and $Y$ across values of $A$.

Key takeaway: $A$ group, $Y$ truth, $\hat Y$ prediction.

The figure

Diagram: a person's features X feed the model, which outputs Ŷ; A and Y sit beside it, and Ŷ is compared to Y by group.

13. Four Rates, Four Denominators

Measuring fairness · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈14.5 min

Here is the confusion matrix, one per group. Four rates come from it, and the difference is the denominator.

The selection rate is the share flagged out of everyone. The true-positive rate, TPR, is the share flagged among those with $Y = 1$. The false-positive rate, FPR, is the share flagged among those with $Y = 0$. Positive predictive value, PPV, is the share with $Y = 1$ among those flagged.

Same table, different denominator, different question. Keep this slide in mind; the COMPAS dispute is exactly a dispute about denominators.

Key takeaway: Same table, different denominator, different question.

The figure

Confusion matrix (TP, FP, FN, TN) and a table of the four rates with their denominators and formulas.

Going further

Formal versions, with conditioning events, are in lec12tech.html, slide 4.

14. Demographic Parity

Measuring fairness · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈16.5 min

The first criterion: demographic parity. Each group is selected at the same rate. Formally, $\Pr[\hat Y = 1 \mid A = a]$ is equal across groups.

It looks only at $\hat Y$, never at $Y$. In the picture, both groups have four people selected, so parity holds, even though six are qualified in group a and only two in group b. Whether that is a feature or a flaw depends on whether you trust $Y$.

If the labels are biased, as in Section 01, ignoring them can be the point. If they are trustworthy, parity can force selecting less-qualified people in one group.

Key takeaway: Parity equalizes selection rates and ignores the truth.

The figure

Two groups of ten icons; qualified icons are marked; four selected in each group.

Assumptions

Illustrative counts.

Primary sources

  • Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold & Zemel, "Fairness Through Awareness", ITCS 2012 — statistical parity; Lipschitz (individual) fairness, Definition 2.1.

15. Equalized Odds

Measuring fairness · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈18.5 min

Equalized odds conditions on the truth. Among people with the same true label, each group is flagged at the same rate.

In the example: among those not rearrested, 2 of 10 are flagged in both groups. Among those rearrested, 8 of 10 are flagged in both. The mistakes fall equally.

Key takeaway: Equalized odds: the same error rates given the truth.

The figure

Two panels: Y = 0 (2 of 10 flagged in each group) and Y = 1 (8 of 10 flagged in each group).

Assumptions

Illustrative counts.

Primary sources

  • Hardt, Price & Srebro, "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning", NeurIPS 2016 — equalized odds and equal opportunity (Defs. 2.1–2.2); ROC geometry (Figs. 1–2); FICO case study (Figs. 9, 11).

16. Equal Odds, Equal Mistakes

Measuring fairness · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈20.5 min

Spelled out: given $Y = 0$, equal false-positive rates; given $Y = 1$, equal true-positive rates. Each true class bears the same error rate in both groups.

Equal opportunity is the weaker version: equal TPR only. It's the one to use when what matters is that qualified people in every group have the same chance of being selected.

Key takeaway: Equalized odds = equal TPR and FPR; equal opportunity = equal TPR.

The figure

Two group confusion matrices with the same column-wise rates: TPR 0.8, FNR 0.2, FPR 0.2, TNR 0.8.

Primary sources

  • Hardt, Price & Srebro, "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning", NeurIPS 2016 — equalized odds and equal opportunity (Defs. 2.1–2.2); ROC geometry (Figs. 1–2); FICO case study (Figs. 9, 11).

17. Equalized Odds on the ROC Plane

Measuring fairness · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈23 min

A picture from Hardt, Price and Srebro. Each group has its own ROC curve: the (FPR, TPR) pairs reachable by thresholding the score. With randomization, any point under a group's curve is achievable.

Equalized odds needs one point that both groups can reach, so it lies in the overlap, below both curves. Equal opportunity only fixes the height, the TPR, so each group can sit at a different FPR.

Key takeaway: Equalized odds lives in the overlap of the groups' ROC regions.

The figure

Hardt, Price and Srebro, NeurIPS 2016, Figure 1: per-group ROC curves with the feasible regions for equal odds and equal opportunity.

What this establishes

Geometrically, which (FPR, TPR) pairs each criterion permits.

What this does not establish

How much accuracy it costs on any given dataset (see slide 43).

Primary sources

  • Hardt, Price & Srebro, "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning", NeurIPS 2016 — equalized odds and equal opportunity (Defs. 2.1–2.2); ROC geometry (Figs. 1–2); FICO case study (Figs. 9, 11).

18. Calibration Within Groups

Measuring fairness · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈25 min

Calibration is about scores, not yes-or-no decisions. A score is calibrated within groups if, in every group, among everyone scored $s$, a fraction $s$ have $Y = 1$.

So among those scored 0.7, about 70% are positive, in both groups. The number means the same thing for everyone. Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan call this condition (A).

Key takeaway: Calibrated: a score means the same thing in every group.

The figure

Two groups, each with ten people scored 0.7, seven of whom have Y = 1.

Assumptions

Illustrative counts.

Primary sources

  • Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan, "Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores", ITCS 2017 — conditions (A) calibration, (B) negative balance, (C) positive balance; Theorems 1.1 and 1.2.

19. Calibration vs Predictive Parity

Measuring fairness · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈27 min

These two sound alike and are not. Calibration is a statement about every score. Predictive parity is about one cutoff: among those above $t$, the share with $Y = 1$, the PPV, is equal across groups.

With only two score values, calibration implies predictive parity. Otherwise, PPV averages the scores above the cutoff, and that average depends on each group's mix of scores. Two groups can both be calibrated and still differ in PPV.

Key takeaway: Calibration is about every score; predictive parity about one cutoff.

The figure

Two definition cards: calibration (every score s) and predictive parity at cutoff t.

Going further

The identity $\mathrm{PPV}_a = \mathbb{E}[S \mid S \gt t, A = a]$ for a calibrated score is in lec12tech.html, slide 7.

Primary sources

  • Chouldechova, "Fair prediction with disparate impact: A study of bias in recidivism prediction instruments", Big Data 5(2), 2017 — Definition 2.2 (predictive parity), Eq. (2.6) identity; Fig. 1 calibration plot, Fig. 2(b) FPR by prior count, Fig. 3 score distributions.

20. Five Criteria, Five Comparisons

Measuring fairness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈28.5 min

A summary table. Demographic parity compares selection rates among everyone. Equal opportunity compares TPR among $Y = 1$. Equalized odds compares TPR and FPR. Predictive parity compares PPV among those flagged. Calibration compares outcomes at each score.

Which of these can hold together? That is Section 03.

Key takeaway: Five criteria, five different populations.

The figure

Table: criterion, what it compares, among whom.

Going further

Two notions not in the table. Both ask for something a dataset alone does not provide.

  • Individual fairness (Dwork et al. 2012, Def. 2.1): similar individuals should be treated similarly. Given a task-specific metric $d$ on individuals and a distance $D$ between output distributions, a randomized classifier $M$ is $(D, d)$-Lipschitz if $D(Mx, My) \le d(x, y)$ for all $x, y$. The hard part is $d$: who decides which people are similar?
  • Counterfactual fairness (Kusner et al. 2017, Def. 5): in a causal model, the prediction's distribution for a person should not change if their attribute $A$ had been different, holding the person's background variables fixed. It needs a causal model of how $A$ affects the features.

Formal statements: lec12tech.html, slide 6.

Primary sources

  • Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold & Zemel, "Fairness Through Awareness", ITCS 2012 — statistical parity; Lipschitz (individual) fairness, Definition 2.1.
  • Kusner, Loftus, Russell & Silva, "Counterfactual Fairness", NIPS 2017 — Definition 5 (counterfactual fairness) in a causal model $(U, V, F)$.

22. Section 03 — COMPAS and Impossibility

COMPAS and impossibility · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈30.5 min

The most famous fairness dispute: one dataset, two analyses, two conclusions, and both arithmetically correct.

23. What COMPAS Is

COMPAS and impossibility · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈31.5 min

COMPAS is a commercial risk score sold by Northpointe and used in US courts. It outputs deciles 1 to 10, labeled low (1–4), medium (5–7) and high (8–10). In Broward County, Florida, it was used mainly for pretrial release or detention decisions.

In the analyses here, recidivism means rearrest within two years. The plot shows score distributions by race: Black defendants' scores are shifted higher, with Cohen's d of 0.60.

Key takeaway: COMPAS: a decile risk score; the outcome is rearrest in two years.

The figure

Chouldechova, Big Data 2017, Figure 3: COMPAS decile distributions by race, Broward County data.

Primary sources

  • Chouldechova, "Fair prediction with disparate impact: A study of bias in recidivism prediction instruments", Big Data 5(2), 2017 — Definition 2.2 (predictive parity), Eq. (2.6) identity; Fig. 1 calibration plot, Fig. 2(b) FPR by prior count, Fig. 3 score distributions.
  • Larson, Mattu, Kirchner & Angwin, "How We Analyzed the COMPAS Recidivism Algorithm", ProPublica (2016) — Broward County data, 7,214 defendants; false-positive and false-negative rates by race.

24. False Positives, By Group

COMPAS and impossibility · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈33 min

ProPublica's analysis covered 7,214 defendants. Among those not rearrested within two years, 44.9% of Black defendants had been labeled higher risk, against 23.5% of White defendants. That is the false-positive rate.

The other error goes the other way. Among those who were rearrested, 47.7% of White defendants had been labeled low risk, against 28.0% of Black defendants.

Key takeaway: ProPublica: unequal false-positive rates by race.

The figure

Bars: false-positive rate 44.9% Black, 23.5% White.

Setup, model, date

Scores from 2013–14 in Broward County; higher risk = medium or high; outcome = a new arrest within two years.

What this establishes

Error-rate balance fails in this data.

What this does not establish

Anything about offending (slide 28), or that the score is miscalibrated.

Primary sources

  • Larson, Mattu, Kirchner & Angwin, "How We Analyzed the COMPAS Recidivism Algorithm", ProPublica (2016) — Broward County data, 7,214 defendants; false-positive and false-negative rates by race.

28. Rearrest Is Not Offending

COMPAS and impossibility · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈37.5 min

A caveat that sits under every COMPAS number. $Y$ is rearrest, not offending. An offence counts only if it is followed by an arrest.

If arrest chances differ by group, $Y$ itself differs for the same behaviour. That is the biased-label problem from Section 01. These sources do not estimate its size, so we flag it rather than quantify it.

Key takeaway: The label is rearrest; the gap in arrest chances is unmeasured here.

The figure

Diagram: offended again → (only if arrested) → Y = rearrested; arrest chances may differ by group.

29. The Key Ingredient: Base Rates

When base rates differ, criteria start to conflict. The next two slides show why.

Key takeaway: Different base rates are the root of the conflicts.

30. Chouldechova: One Identity

COMPAS and impossibility · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈40 min

For a binary prediction in a group with base rate $p$, FPR is a function of $p$, PPV and FNR. It's just bookkeeping on the confusion matrix.

Now fix PPV equal across groups, strictly between 0 and 1, and FNR below 1. If base rates differ, FPR and FNR cannot both be equal. The only escapes are equal base rates or perfect PPV.

Note that calibration is not part of this result; it uses only the binary prediction.

Key takeaway: Equal PPV plus different base rates forces unequal error rates.

The figure

The identity $\mathrm{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\mathrm{PPV}}{\mathrm{PPV}}\cdot(1-\mathrm{FNR})$.

Going further

Derivation in lec12tech.html, slide 9 (Chouldechova Eq. 2.6).

Primary sources

  • Chouldechova, "Fair prediction with disparate impact: A study of bias in recidivism prediction instruments", Big Data 5(2), 2017 — Definition 2.2 (predictive parity), Eq. (2.6) identity; Fig. 1 calibration plot, Fig. 2(b) FPR by prior count, Fig. 3 score distributions.

31. Kleinberg et al.: Three Conditions on a Score

COMPAS and impossibility · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈41.5 min

Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan look at scores. Condition (A): calibration within groups. (B): among $Y = 0$, the same mean score in both groups. (C): among $Y = 1$, the same mean score.

All three can hold only with perfect prediction or equal base rates. An approximate version also holds: satisfying all three nearly forces you nearly into one of those cases.

Neither result says every pair of criteria conflicts. They say specific combinations do.

Key takeaway: Calibration plus both balance conditions needs perfect prediction or equal base rates.

Going further

Proof sketch in lec12tech.html, slide 10.

Primary sources

  • Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan, "Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores", ITCS 2017 — conditions (A) calibration, (B) negative balance, (C) positive balance; Theorems 1.1 and 1.2.

32. Worked Example: Calibrated, Unequal Errors

COMPAS and impossibility · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈43 min

33. Why the Gap Is Forced

COMPAS and impossibility · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈44 min

Why does it happen? Calibration ties each group's mean score to its base rate. A lower base rate means fewer high scores. A shared cutoff then flags fewer people in group B, so B gets a lower FPR and a higher FNR.

The gap comes from the base rates, not from a bad model.

Key takeaway: Calibration ties the score mix to the base rate.

The figure

Score mix per group: a is 50% at 0.8 and 50% at 0.2; b is 8% at 0.8 and 92% at 0.2.

34. Choosing by Context

COMPAS and impossibility · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈45 min

So we must choose. The domain tells us which error is worse. If the worst harm is a wrongful arrest, a false positive, equalize FPR. If it is a missed diagnosis, a false negative, equalize FNR.

That choice is a value judgment, made with the people affected, not a technical optimum.

Key takeaway: The domain decides which error you cannot tolerate.

Two confusion matrices: the FP cell highlighted for wrongful arrest, the FN cell for missed diagnosis.

35. Section 04 — Mitigation

36. Three Places to Intervene

Mitigation · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈47 min

Three stages. Before training, change the data: reweighing. During training, change the objective: reductions. After training, change the decision rule: group-specific thresholds.

In practice you pick the stage you can actually touch. A hospital buying a vendor's score may only control the threshold. A team training its own model can change the objective.

Key takeaway: Pick the stage you can actually touch.

The figure

Pipeline Data → Training → Outputs, labeled pre-process (reweighing), in-process (reductions), post-process (group thresholds).

Going further

Another in-process method: adversarial debiasing. Zhang, Lemoine and Mitchell (AIES 2018) train a predictor and an adversary at the same time. In the abstract's words, the goal is to "maximize the predictor's ability to predict Y while minimizing the adversary's ability to predict Z", where Z is the protected attribute. On UCI Adult the authors report a model that "does not lose much accuracy while achieving very close to equality of odds". It is not in the deck because it shares the reductions slot and adds no new idea for this audience.

Primary sources

  • Zhang, Lemoine & Mitchell, "Mitigating Unwanted Biases with Adversarial Learning", AIES 2018 — predictor vs adversary; UCI Adult result described as "very close to equality of odds".

37. Reweighing

Mitigation · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈49 min

Reweighing, from Kamiran and Calders. Give each training example a weight so that group and label look independent. The weight for a cell is the share you'd expect if $A$ and $Y$ were independent, divided by the share you actually observe.

Under-represented cells get weights above 1, over-represented cells below 1. No example is deleted and no label is changed. Example: if group s is half the data, 60% of labels are positive, and only 20% of the data is (s, positive), the weight is $0.5 \times 0.6 / 0.2 = 1.5$.

Key takeaway: Reweighing makes group and label look independent, without deleting anything.

The figure

The weight formula $w(a, y) = \Pr[A = a]\Pr[Y = y] / \Pr[A = a, Y = y]$ with a numeric example.

Assumptions

The group is known at training time. The labels are taken as correct.

Going further

Formal statement: lec12tech.html, slide 13.

Primary sources

  • Kamiran & Calders, "Data preprocessing techniques for classification without discrimination", Knowledge and Information Systems 33 (2012) — reweighing, §5.2.

38. Reweighing: A Worked Example

Mitigation · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈51 min

A hundred examples, 60% positive. Group s has 20 positive and 30 negative; group o has 40 positive and 10 negative. So s is 40% positive and o is 80%.

The weights are 1.5 for (s, +), 0.67 for (s, −), 0.75 for (o, +) and 2.0 for (o, −). After weighting, s has 30 positive and 20 negative, and o has 30 positive and 20 negative: 60% positive in each group, matching the overall rate.

Key takeaway: After reweighing, each group has the overall positive rate.

The figure

Before and after bars of weighted positive and negative counts for groups s and o, plus the weight table.

Setup, model, date

Instructor-built counts. Check: (s, +) = 0.5 × 0.6 / 0.2 = 1.5; (s, −) = 0.5 × 0.4 / 0.3 ≈ 0.67; (o, +) = 0.5 × 0.6 / 0.4 = 0.75; (o, −) = 0.5 × 0.4 / 0.1 = 2.0.

40. The Reductions Approach

Mitigation · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈55.5 min

Agarwal and colleagues turn a fairness constraint into a sequence of ordinary training runs. The fairness gap gets a price, like a penalty. Each round reweights the examples by that price and fits a plain cost-weighted classifier. Then it measures the gap, adjusts the price, and repeats.

The output is a random mix of the classifiers fit along the way. That randomization is part of the guarantee, which is worth telling a deployer: two identical people can get different decisions.

Key takeaway: Reductions: fairness constraints via repeated ordinary fits.

The figure

Loop: reweight examples → train plain classifier → measure the gap → adjust weights; stop with a randomized mix.

Assumptions

Group known at training time; any learner that accepts example weights; the output is randomized.

Going further

Saddle-point formulation and Theorem 1: lec12tech.html, slide 14.

Primary sources

42. Group-Specific Thresholds

Mitigation · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈59.5 min

The post-processing route from Hardt, Price and Srebro. Freeze the score. Use labeled held-out data to draw each group's ROC curve. Then pick a point per group that satisfies the criterion. For equalized odds, that point may lie under a curve, so some decisions are randomized.

Two requirements: the group label at decision time, and labeled held-out data. The first can be legally sensitive.

Key takeaway: Post-processing: keep the score, change each group's threshold.

The figure

Hardt, Price and Srebro, NeurIPS 2016, Fig. 2: feasible points for equalized odds (middle) and equal opportunity (right).

Going further

Derived-predictor formulation: lec12tech.html, slide 15.

Primary sources

  • Hardt, Price & Srebro, "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning", NeurIPS 2016 — equalized odds and equal opportunity (Defs. 2.1–2.2); ROC geometry (Figs. 1–2); FICO case study (Figs. 9, 11).

43. The Cost, on FICO Data

Mitigation · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈61.5 min

What does a criterion cost? On the FICO data, with an 82% break-even rate, the paper reports the lender's profit as a share of the maximum. Race-blind thresholds keep 99.3%. Equal opportunity keeps 92.8%. Equalized odds 80.2%. Demographic parity 69.8%.

Two cautions. The utility is the lender's profit, not anyone's welfare. And it is a single dataset with the paper's cost assumption.

Key takeaway: Stricter criteria cost more lender profit on this data.

Profit model with an 82% break-even rate, as in the paper.

45. Method Cards

48. After the Audit: Error Fell on the Named Systems

49. Auditing Language Models: BBQ

Beyond classifiers · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈72 min

How do we audit a language model? BBQ asks questions the context cannot answer. In an ambiguous context, the correct answer is "unknown". A model that picks a person anyway is relying on a stereotype.

BBQ covers nine social dimensions in US English, with over 58,000 items. When the context does contain the answer, models were up to 3.4 points more accurate when the answer fit a bias.

Key takeaway: BBQ measures reliance on stereotypes when the answer is unknown.

The figure

Parrish et al., Findings of ACL 2022, Figure 1: an ambiguous and a disambiguated BBQ example.

Going further

Language-model decisions (not in the deck). Tamkin et al. (2023) generated 70 decision scenarios and varied the demographic information in each prompt. They report "patterns of both positive and negative discrimination in the Claude 2.0 model in select settings when no interventions are applied", and that careful prompt engineering can "significantly decrease both positive and negative discrimination".

Résumé retrieval. Wilson and Caliskan tested three Mistral-7B-based embedding models on 554 résumés and 571 job descriptions across nine occupations. White-associated names were favored in 85.1% of tests. Their v3 errata note (Aug 2026) says the gender-only results "should be inverted" because of a code bug, while race-only and intersectional results are unaffected. The paper body still prints the uncorrected gender figures, so we quote no gender number here.

First-person fairness. Eloundou et al. (ICLR 2025) study fairness toward the chatbot's own user. They swap names associated with different genders or races across "sixty-six tasks in nine domains" and report that post-training reinforcement learning "significantly mitigate[s]" the biases. On random chats, harmful gender stereotypes were "below 0.1% (1 in 1,000) for each model"; on story writing, the older ChatGPT-3.5-turbo had mean ratings above 2%, all other models below 1%.

  • Wilson & Caliskan, "Gender, Race, and Intersectional Bias in Resume Screening via Language Model Retrieval" (arXiv 2407.20371, v3 with errata note, Aug 2026) — 554 résumés, 571 job descriptions, nine occupations; three Mistral-7B-based embedding models. 51. Generators Can Exaggerate Stereotypes

Text-to-image models. Bianchi and colleagues prompted Stable Diffusion with "A photo of the face of [OCCUPATION]". Software developers came out nearly all pale and masculine; housekeepers darker and feminine.

CLIP read 99% of the developer images as white, against 56% of US developers. Counter-prompts and guardrails did not prevent it.

Key takeaway: Generators can exaggerate real-world skews.

52. Governance: Obligations and Their Limits

Beyond classifiers · ≈3.5 min · elapsed ≈80 min

Four laws, each with an obligation and a limit. New York City's Local Law 144 requires a bias audit of automated hiring tools and publication of impact ratios. But there is no duty to act on the results. The State Comptroller's December 2025 audit found the city had surveyed 32 companies and identified just one compliance issue.

The EU AI Act requires high-risk systems to examine and mitigate data bias, in Article 10. An amending regulation moves application to 2 December 2027.

Colorado's SB26-189 requires notice and review after adverse decisions from 2027, but creates no new private right of action. Korea's AI Basic Act sets risk-management and oversight duties for high-impact AI, in Article 34. Its impact assessment is phrased as an effort, "endeavor", and there is no bias audit.

The common thread: an audit reports rates. None of these laws chooses the criterion for you.

Key takeaway: Laws require reporting; none picks the criterion.

Status checked 10 Oct 2026 against the cited texts.

Going further

54. A Checklist

Synthesis · ≈3.5 min · elapsed ≈84 min

Six questions. One: which decision, and which error hurts most? Two: what does $Y$ actually record? Three: which rate, out of whom? Four: do base rates differ, and if so, which criteria conflict? Five: which stage can you change? Six: evaluated on what data, against what utility?

Run COMPAS through it. The decision is pretrial detention, and a false positive jails someone. $Y$ records rearrest, not offending. ProPublica and Northpointe used different denominators. Base rates differed, so their criteria could not both hold.

Key takeaway: Six questions for any fairness claim.

55. Key Takeaways

Synthesis · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈87 min

Bias enters through data, labels and feedback loops. Each criterion compares a rate with its own denominator. In COMPAS the analyses evaluated different criteria. With unequal base rates, some criteria cannot all hold. Each mitigation targets one criterion, under assumptions.

And choosing the criterion is a value judgment. The math tells you what conflicts; it does not tell you what to choose.

Key takeaway: Choosing a fairness criterion is a value judgment.
