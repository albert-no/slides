MIA 4 · Modern Likelihood-Ratio Attacks — Notes

Companion to mia4-modern.html. Every definition, theorem and proof now lives on the slides; this file holds only what the slides deliberately leave out: modelling caveats, tightness discussion, a secondary table, and speaker asides.

What moved onto the slides — and one correction

An earlier version of this note carried the LiRA construction, the four Ye tiers, the "Population versus Reference" explainer, the RMIA construction and side-by-side pseudocode. All of that is now on the slides in a sharper form: Definitions 1–3 and Theorem 1 with its three-step proof for LiRA; Definitions 5–6 and Theorem 2 for the tiers; Definitions 7–8 for RMIA. The pseudocode has been dropped rather than migrated, because the version printed here computed a percentile rank of a target/reference loss ratio, which is not what RMIA does. The paper's score is $\Pr_{z\sim\pi}[\mathrm{LR}_\theta(x,z) \ge \gamma]$ — the fraction of population points that the target model favours $x$ over — and that is Definition 8 on the slides.

The monotonicity claim was wrong. This note used to state that the four tiers are totally ordered, Threshold < Population < Reference < LiRA, "at every FPR", and attributed that to Ye et al. They prove no such thing. What the slides now say is the defensible version: Theorem 2 gives a partial order, driven by set inclusion of the conditioning variables, at the population level with correctly specified models. Attack P conditions on $\theta$; Attack R conditions on $(x_z, y_z)$. Neither set contains the other, so the theorem is silent about the pair, and Ye's own experiments show them behaving comparably rather than one dominating. Monotonicity in that paper is a design rationale, not a theorem, and the slides carry a dedicated "What Theorem 2 Does Not Say" slide for exactly this.

Numbers deleted as unverifiable. The earlier note quoted "8–10% TPR at 0.1% FPR versus under 1% for prior attacks", "a factor of 10–50×", a "30–100×" RMIA cost reduction, "doubling $R$ from 4 to 8 nudges TPR up by 1–2%", and DP-SGD utility figures of "1–5% accuracy at $\varepsilon$ 1–2", "$\varepsilon \le 0.1$ useless", "$\varepsilon \ge 10$ vacuous". None of these appear in the cited papers. The slides now use the papers' own tables instead, reproduced verbatim with the venue and table number on each slide.

The Gaussian model: what it is doing, and where it fails

It is an assumption, not a derivation

Carlini et al. state plainly that they assume the two conditionals are Gaussian, which reduces the attack to estimating four parameters. Nothing forces this. The slides make the dependency explicit: Theorem 1 is a statement about the Gaussian model, and Corollary 1's clean $\log\Lambda = dz - \tfrac12 d^2$ needs the shared-variance case on top of that. If the true conditionals are skewed, the closed form is still computable but is no longer the likelihood ratio of anything, and Neyman–Pearson optimality does not transfer.

Why the logit transform is doing real work

The variable being modelled is not the confidence and not the cross-entropy loss but $\phi(p) = \log\frac{p}{1-p}$. Confidence is supported on $[0,1]$ and piles up at the right endpoint for any trained model, so a Gaussian fitted to it is badly misspecified in exactly the region where members live. The logit sends that endpoint to $+\infty$ and spreads the pile out. This is a variance-stabilising reparameterisation chosen because it looks normal empirically, not because a theorem says it is.

The failure mode to name out loud

Misspecification cost is bounded by the surrogate-law argument from the earlier deck — at most $2\,\mathrm{TV}$ between the fitted and true conditionals. That bound is one-directional and gives no rate. Where it bites hardest is the tail: TPR at $\mathrm{FPR} = 10^{-3}$ is decided by the far-left tail of the OUT fit, which is the part of a Gaussian that four or eight samples determine worst. Proposition 3 on the slides quantifies the estimator side of this ($\mathrm{sd}(\hat\sigma)/\sigma \approx 1/\sqrt{2(R-1)}$); the misspecification side has no comparable bound.

What the hierarchy does not promise

Population level, not finite samples

Theorem 2 compares the optimal tests available under two conditioning sets. It says nothing about the tests you actually run, which use estimated densities from finitely many models. A richer conditioning set with a badly estimated density can and does lose to a poorer set with a well-estimated one — which is the whole reason RMIA exists. The slides state this as the second bullet of "What Theorem 2 Does Not Say".

Per-record versus per-sample games

Ye et al. formalise a distinction the earlier literature blurred. The per-sample game draws $z$ at random and scores the adversary on average over $z$. The per-record game fixes a specific $z$ in advance. Outlier-driven leakage shows up only in the per-record game; averaging hides it under the bulk of well-behaved examples. The TPR-at-low-FPR critique that opens this deck is the per-record game expressed as a metric, and Attack D in Ye's table is the per-record, per-model corner of the design space.

Why this matters for auditing

An empirical MIA gives a lower bound on leakage, so certifying with a weak attack systematically overstates privacy. Running a Population-tier attack and reporting its TPR as the risk is the common version of that mistake. Privacy Meter exists to make "which attack did you run" a checklist item rather than a matter of taste. Note the asymmetry with DP-SGD: the DP bound is an upper bound that holds against every adversary, the attack is a lower bound that holds only against the one you ran, and nothing in this deck closes the gap between them.

RMIA: reading the claims carefully

Cross-paper numbers do not compare. Zarifzadeh et al. train their own target and reference models with their own splits and architectures, so their LiRA column is not Carlini's LiRA column. The slides carry a dedicated warning slide, and every RMIA number on the slides is quoted from Table 2 or Table 3 of that paper, alongside the RMIA number from the same row. Quoting an RMIA figure against a Carlini figure would be meaningless.

The low-budget claim is the real one. The paper's defensible headline is not a cost multiplier but a regime statement: with one, two or four reference models RMIA reports higher AUC than LiRA and Attack-R on the same setup, and LiRA needs a large model budget before it can drop its global-variance fallback. That is what the slides say. Whether the advantage comes from the rank statistic's lower variance or from something else is not settled by the paper; the slides label the variance story as intuition and keep Proposition 3 — which is about LiRA's own $\hat\sigma$ — separate from it.

Two free knobs. The threshold $\gamma \ge 1$ inside the score and the population distribution $\pi$ that the candidate $z$ is drawn from are both design choices. Changing $\pi$ changes the null the target is ranked against, exactly as changing the reference-model distribution changes it for Attack R. No optimality claim survives an arbitrary $\pi$.

Label-only: the cost model

The boundary-distance attack buys access with queries rather than with training runs, which changes the shape of the threat model rather than its strength. The slides quote the paper's query counts; the useful framing for discussion is that at commodity inference prices the per-sample cost is cents, so a defense whose only effect is to raise the query count is a speed bump, not a barrier. The paper's own conclusion is the sharp version: a defense cannot rely solely on post-processing the confidence vector. Corollary 3 on the slides is the formal statement of that sentence.

One caveat worth stating in the room: Proposition 4 identifies distance with the logit exactly for a linear model. For a deep network the identification is heuristic — the boundary is not a hyperplane, the walk finds a local nearest boundary point, and the resulting distance is a lower bound on the true margin. That is why the slides call it a proxy and why the empirical query-budget curve, not the proposition, carries the practical claim.

DP-SGD: the honest scope

The guarantee is an upper bound on every adversary's ROC, and the slides make that concrete by pushing a specific $\varepsilon$ through the cap $\mathrm{TPR} \le e^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{FPR} + \delta$ at $\mathrm{FPR} = 10^{-3}$. Three things the bound does not do, all worth saying aloud. It does not describe the attack you will actually observe, which is typically far below the cap. It does not degrade gracefully: at the $\varepsilon$ values commonly reported for usable accuracy the cap sits above $100\%$ and is formally vacuous, even though measured attacks are still weak. And it is a worst-case bound over datasets, so a small measured advantage is not evidence that $\varepsilon$ is small.

Accuracy cost is deliberately left unquantified on the slides. It depends on dataset, architecture, batch size, clipping norm and the accountant, and any single number quoted for it would be a number about one experiment. Point at the literature rather than at a figure.

Diffusion: measured versus intuited

Two claims are easy to blur. Measured: Matsumoto et al. report AUCROC peaking at an intermediate timestep, $t = 350$ for the cosine schedule and $t = 200$ for the linear one, both corresponding to $\bar\alpha_t \approx 0.7$; the black-box variant fails across their models and datasets; vulnerability rises with training epochs and with smaller training sets. Intuited: the two-endpoint argument for why the middle carries the signal — near $t = 0$ denoising is easy for members and non-members alike, near $t = T$ the input is noise and the target is unpredictable for both. The slides mark that argument as intuition. There is no theorem in the paper locating the peak, and the coincidence of $\bar\alpha_t \approx 0.7$ across two schedules is one observation, not a law.

Also worth flagging: the reported AUCs are modest. This is a weak attack by classification standards, and the interesting content is the shape of the curve rather than its height.

Speaker asides

Where to slow down. Step 3 of the proof of Theorem 1 (equal variances collapsing the quadratic to a z-score) and Step 3 of Proposition 1 (Jensen against a strictly concave ROC) carry the LiRA section. In the hierarchy section it is the Population-versus-Reference sweep picture: one model over many records, versus many models over one record. Everything else can be taken at pace.

A question to expect. "If richer conditioning cannot hurt, why does RMIA beat LiRA?" The answer is the population-versus-finite-sample gap: Theorem 2 compares optimal tests, RMIA and LiRA differ in estimation error. Worth rehearsing, because the slides deliberately state the theorem and the empirical inversion back to back.

A second question to expect. "Is the offline test really optimal?" Only within the one-sided family, by Karlin–Rubin under the monotone likelihood ratio of the OUT Gaussian. It is not the Neyman–Pearson test for the two-sided problem the online attack solves, which is why the online mode is stronger.

Notation warning. The slides write $\phi$ for the logit-scaled statistic in the LiRA section and reuse $\phi$ for a generic statistic in the synthesis section. Say so once at the synthesis slide; the reuse is intentional and is the point of that section.

Open questions before the next deck

  • Does the recipe transfer to language models? Per-example calibration on a multi-billion-parameter model implies retraining a shadow population that no one can afford. The next deck is built around that constraint.
  • Reference-free calibration. If no reference model can be trained, what plays the role of the OUT distribution? Neighbourhood and self-prompt constructions are the answer in the language-model case.
  • Per-example accounting. If MI advantage lower-bounds leakage and DP upper-bounds it, can per-example calibration give per-example $\varepsilon$? Active research area, not a solved problem.
