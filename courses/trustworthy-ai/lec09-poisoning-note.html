3. Section 01 — Why We Want Integrity

Why we want integrity · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈1.5 min

A model learns from its data. Today's question: what if someone else wrote part of that data? And afterwards, could you tell?

Key takeaway: Two questions: what can be planted, and can it be found.

4. Like You're Five: A Student Learns From the Answer Key

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈2.5 min

An illustrative story. Picture a student who learns only from an answer key. Questions one to three are right in the key; question four was changed by someone else. The student can learn the planted answer along with the real ones.

On the exam the student gets one to three right and four wrong, exactly as planted. The student did nothing wrong: it learned what it was given, and the key looks the same either way. A model is that student, and the training data is the answer key.

Key takeaway: The label alone does not reveal malicious intent.

5. Training Data Comes From Strangers

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈3.5 min

Where does training data come from? Web pages and images, crowd-sourced labels and edits, public datasets assembled by others, pretrained models downloaded from hubs, and sometimes training outsourced entirely.

Every arrow here is a way in. With billions of examples, no one reads them all. That is why the data is an attack surface.

Key takeaway: Each source in the pipeline is an entry point.

6. A Classifier Learned a Photo Tag, Not the Horse

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈4.5 min

A real accident first. Lapuschkin and colleagues looked at which pixels a classifier used to say "horse". Look at the red blob on the left: it sits on the photographer's source tag in the corner, not on the horse.

Remove the tag and the horse is no longer classified as a horse. Paste the same tag onto a picture of a car and the car becomes a horse. Nobody attacked this model.

Key takeaway: A classifier learned a photo tag instead of the horse.

The figure

Fig. 2(a) of Lapuschkin et al.: left, a PASCAL VOC horse image with and without its source tag; right, a car with and without the tag pasted in. Each image is paired with a heatmap of the evidence for "horse"; red marks strong evidence.

Setup, model, date

Lapuschkin, Wäldchen, Binder, Montavon, Samek & Müller, Nature Communications 2019. A Fisher-vector classifier trained on PASCAL VOC 2007; explanations by layer-wise relevance propagation.

What this establishes

A model can reach good test accuracy by relying on an artefact that happens to predict the label.

What this does not establish

That deep networks generally do this: the paper's fine-tuned deep network looked at the horse.

Primary sources

  • Lapuschkin et al., "Unmasking Clever Hans Predictors and Assessing What Machines Really Learn" (Nature Communications 2019) — Fig. 2(a): a Fisher-vector classifier on PASCAL VOC 2007 uses a photographer's source tag as evidence for "horse"; a fine-tuned deep network on the same task does not.

7. This Classifier Used a Tag as a Shortcut

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈5.5 min

What went wrong? A person sees a horse and rider in a river. The model saw a tag that appeared on about one in five horse photos, and that tag predicted "horse".

Nobody planted the tag. The model found the easiest rule that fit the data. Be careful with the scope: a fine-tuned deep network on the same task looked at the horse, so this shortcut belonged to this model.

Key takeaway: This classifier used a tag as a shortcut.

Setup, model, date

Lapuschkin et al. 2019, Results: 9,963 PASCAL VOC images; the source tag appears on about one fifth of horse images.

Primary sources

  • Lapuschkin et al., "Unmasking Clever Hans Predictors and Assessing What Machines Really Learn" (Nature Communications 2019) — Fig. 2(a): a Fisher-vector classifier on PASCAL VOC 2007 uses a photographer's source tag as evidence for "horse"; a fine-tuned deep network on the same task does not.

8. An Accidental Shortcut Shows Where to Attack

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈6.5 min

Put the two side by side. By accident, a photographer adds a tag, the model learns tag means horse, and someone finds out years later. On purpose, an attacker adds a sticker, the model learns sticker means some label X, and the attacker knows the rule from day one.

If a tag can be learned by accident, a trigger can be taught on purpose. The weak spot is that models learn rules no human checked.

Key takeaway: An accidental shortcut shows exactly where an attacker would plant one.

9. A Sticky Note Turns a Stop Sign Into a Speed Limit

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈7.5 min

Now a deliberate case. This is a real stop sign near the authors' office, with a yellow sticky note on it. The detector draws a box and says "speed limit", with confidence 0.947.

This is not an adversarial example from last lecture. Nobody searched for a perturbation for this photo. The model was trained by the attacker, and during training it was taught that a yellow square means speed limit. Without the note, the same model reads stop signs normally.

Read the strip at the top: who the attacker is, what they control, what they want. We will read every study today in that format.

Key takeaway: A backdoored model behaves normally until it sees its trigger.

The figure

Fig. 8 of BadNets: a Faster R-CNN traffic-sign detector, backdoored with a yellow-square trigger, labels a real stop sign with a sticky note as "speedlimit" (0.947). The external labels mark the box label and the confidence.

Setup, model, date

Gu, Dolan-Gavitt & Garg (NYU), first posted August 2017. Threat model: a user outsources training to an untrusted party, who returns a model that meets the user's accuracy checks. The detector was trained on a U.S. traffic-sign dataset with poisoned stop signs relabelled as speed limit.

What this establishes

A real-world trigger, photographed in the field, can activate a backdoor planted in training, while clean stop signs are still read correctly.

What this does not establish

It does not show that deployed traffic-sign systems contain backdoors, nor how often such a single photograph succeeds across angles and distances; it is one demonstration image.

Assumptions

The attacker controls training (outsourced training), so they can choose both the poisoned images and their labels.

Primary sources

  • Gu, Dolan-Gavitt & Garg, "BadNets: Identifying Vulnerabilities in the Machine Learning Model Supply Chain" (arXiv 2017; extended in IEEE Access 2019) — outsourced-training threat model, MNIST triggers (Figs. 3–6), traffic-sign backdoor (Figs. 7–8, §5).

10. Tay Shows the Risk of Malicious User Interactions

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈8.5 min

A second real case, from a chatbot. Microsoft launched Tay on Twitter on 23 March 2016. Within its first day it posted offensive tweets, and Microsoft took it offline.

Microsoft's own account: "a coordinated attack by a subset of people exploited a vulnerability in Tay." And: "AI systems feed off of both positive and negative interactions." Microsoft reported coordinated abuse, but this account does not establish a weight-update mechanism; we do not know whether user messages ever changed Tay's weights. Treat it as motivation: a system that learns from users lets users influence what it learns.

Key takeaway: Tay shows the risk of malicious user interactions.

Setup, model, date

Peter Lee, Corporate Vice President, Microsoft Research, blog post of 25 March 2016.

What this does not establish

How Tay was trained or updated, or whether its weights were changed at all; Microsoft did not disclose this.

Primary sources

  • P. Lee, "Learning from Tay's introduction" (Official Microsoft Blog, 25 March 2016) — Microsoft's statement on Tay; describes a "coordinated attack" but not the mechanism.

11. What We Want: Integrity

Why we want integrity · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈10 min

So what do we want? Integrity: the model learns the task, and only the task, even when some training data comes from strangers.

Integrity fails in three ways, and they organise the rest of today. Lower accuracy on everything. One chosen input answered wrong. Or a hidden rule that waits for a trigger. Last lecture: altered test inputs. Today: compromised learning and information sources, which include training data and, for language models, what they retrieve.

Key takeaway: Integrity: learn the task and only the task.

12. Why Integrity Matters

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈11 min

Why care? First, tests pass: a planted rule fires only on its trigger, so clean tests miss it. Second, the stakes are real: spam filters, cars, code assistants, content moderation. Third, the data is unread: web, crowd and user data are too big to check by hand.

Put together: a model can score 99 percent and still obey someone else. The 99 is illustrative; we will see measured numbers soon.

Key takeaway: High test accuracy does not show integrity.

13. The Model Was Taught What the Sticker Means

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈12 min

Lecture 8 in one line: evasion changes the input at inference time, and it does not need to touch training at all. The model was trained honestly.

A backdoor is different in where the work happens. The behaviour is planted during training, and the trigger only activates it at inference. Same sticker on a stop sign, different mechanism: here the model was taught what the sticker means.

Do not over-read the contrast. Universal adversarial perturbations work across many inputs without retraining, and some backdoors fire only for one source class. The distinction here is how the behaviour got in: this backdoor was planted through training data. Backdoors can also be written directly into the weights, the other route on the next slide.

Key takeaway: Evasion needs no training access; this backdoor is planted through training data and activated at inference.

14. Poisoning Is How; a Backdoor Is What

Why we want integrity · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈13 min

Two words we will use precisely. Poisoning is a method: changing the training data. A backdoor is a behaviour: hidden, conditional, triggered.

They overlap. Poisoning can just lower accuracy or flip one target, with no trigger. Many backdoors are planted through poisoned data: BadNets, Souly et al., and Sleeper Agents, whose authors built the backdoor with constructed training data and full control of training. A backdoor can also be planted with no training data at all, by editing the weights directly.

Sleeper Agents is in the overlap, not outside it. We use it later as a persistence study: once a backdoor is in, does safety training remove it?

Key takeaway: Poisoning is how; a backdoor is what. They overlap but differ.

16. Many People Can Write Into Training Data

Who poisons, and why · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈15 min

Who can write into training data? A spammer wants ads past a filter that learns from mail. A competitor wants a rival's message to vanish. A hired trainer can ship a model with a hidden rule. A web-page owner decides what a scraped URL serves. And an artist may push back on scraping of their work.

Not every writer is hostile: artists poison in protest. Each motive comes from a paper we will read today.

Key takeaway: Many different people can write into training data, for different reasons.

17. A Spammer Can Poison the Filter It Is Fighting

Who poisons, and why · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈16.5 min

The oldest example here is spam. A filter that learns from your mail lets a spammer send it training data. Nelson and colleagues stuffed spam with ordinary words. You mark it spam, the filter learns that ordinary words mean spam, and your real mail starts landing in the spam folder. The paper argues you may then disable the filter; that is a consequence they reason about, not a user study.

On SpamBayes, 101 attack emails, 1 percent of a 10,000-message training set, made the filter unusable. The 1 percent counts messages, not words: each dictionary attack email carried a whole English dictionary, about 98,000 words, so these were unusually large messages. Their focused attack, aimed at one chosen email, removed it 90 percent of the time.

Key takeaway: A spammer can poison the very filter that is meant to stop them.

Setup, model, date

Nelson, Barreno, Chi, Joseph, Rubinstein, Saini, Sutton, Tygar & Xia, USENIX LEET 2008. SpamBayes trained on the TREC 2005 corpus; dictionary attack (availability) and focused attack (targeted). Motives in the paper: spammer revenue, and a competitor blocking a rival's bids.

What this establishes

Poisoning was practical against a deployed learning system long before deep learning.

What this does not establish

Results for modern filters, which use many more signals.

Primary sources

  • Nelson et al., "Exploiting Machine Learning to Subvert Your Spam Filter" (USENIX LEET 2008) — dictionary and focused attacks on SpamBayes, TREC 2005 corpus; attacker motives in §1–2.

20. Poisoning Has Two Goals

Data poisoning · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈18.5 min

Availability attacks make the model worse overall: accuracy drops from, say, 95 to 70 percent. Easy to notice, if you test.

Targeted attacks aim at chosen inputs. Overall accuracy can stay near 95 percent while the target is wrong, so an aggregate test raises no alarm. These numbers are illustrative, not from a study.

Key takeaway: Targeted poisoning can leave aggregate accuracy nearly unchanged.

21. One Placed Point Can Tilt a Boundary

Data poisoning · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈19.5 min

Here is the intuition, as a sketch. Two classes, a dashed clean boundary. The attacker adds one red point, deep in class B territory, but labelled A. Retrain, and the boundary tilts to accommodate it.

The attacker's power is choosing where the point goes and which label it carries.

Key takeaway: Placement plus label lets one point move a boundary.

22. Biggio Setup: Walk a Point Uphill on the Loss Map

Data poisoning · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈21.5 min

Before the figure, the method. The attacker adds one point, knows the support vector machine and its data, and wants higher loss on validation data: clean labelled points held out of training and used to score the model.

Picture a loss map: red where the model's validation loss would be high if the point sat there. Place the point, retrain, measure the validation loss, and step the point uphill. Repeat: the point climbs toward a local maximum, as the next figure shows.

Key takeaway: Biggio: walk one point uphill on the validation-loss map.

Primary sources

  • Biggio, Nelson & Laskov, "Poisoning Attacks against Support Vector Machines" (ICML 2012) — gradient-ascent poisoning of SVMs; Fig. 1 (2-D toy) and Fig. 3 (MNIST pairs).

23. Gradient Ascent Searches for a Damaging Point

Data poisoning · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈23.5 min

Biggio, Nelson and Laskov made this precise in 2012. The attacker knows the SVM and its training data, and adds one point. The colour shows how bad the model would be on validation data if the point sat at each location: in the left panel the hinge loss the attack maximises, in the right panel the classification error. The black path is the attack point climbing uphill by gradient ascent.

This is the first clean picture of poisoning as optimisation: the attacker is solving a problem, not guessing. Gradient ascent climbs to a local maximum; it is not guaranteed to find the single most damaging point.

Key takeaway: Poisoning can be optimised: move the point uphill on validation loss.

The figure

Fig. 1, top row: linear-kernel SVM on 2-D Gaussian data. Left background colour = hinge loss on validation data, right = classification error, each as a function of the attack point's location; the path traces the gradient-ascent steps.

Setup, model, date

Biggio, Nelson & Laskov, ICML 2012. The attacker knows the learning algorithm and can draw data from the same distribution; it injects a point with a chosen label and re-solves the SVM incrementally after each step.

What this establishes

For SVMs with the stated kernels, a single well-placed training point can be found by gradient ascent and noticeably increases validation error.

What this does not establish

It does not cover deep networks, and gradient ascent finds a local maximum: the paper reports good local optima, not the worst possible point.

Assumptions

White-box knowledge of the model and data; the ability to insert labelled training points.

Primary sources

  • Biggio, Nelson & Laskov, "Poisoning Attacks against Support Vector Machines" (ICML 2012) — gradient-ascent poisoning of SVMs; Fig. 1 (2-D toy) and Fig. 3 (MNIST pairs).

24. A Few Percent of Points Multiply the Error

Data poisoning · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈24.5 min

25. A Targeted Attack Aims at One Input

Data poisoning · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈25.5 min

Targeted poisoning wants something narrower. The grey triangle is a target of class B. The attacker wants it classified A. In the sketch the boundary bends around that one input.

Most other points keep their label, so aggregate test accuracy can barely move. Some other predictions may change too; the claim is about the aggregate, not every point.

Key takeaway: Targeted poisoning aims at one input; aggregate accuracy can barely move.

26. Clean-Label Poison Can Evade Label Checks

Data poisoning · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈26.5 min

Shafahi et al. added a twist: clean labels. The attacker submits images whose labels are honest. A fish photo is labelled fish. A check of image against label finds no error. That does not guarantee every human review passes; a reviewer looking for artefacts might still catch some.

The attack hides in tiny pixel changes that a human does not see but the network does.

Key takeaway: Honest labels are not proof of clean data.

27. Feature Space: Where the Model Places Each Image

Data poisoning · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈27.5 min

One idea we need for clean-label poisoning. A network turns each image into a point in feature space. Images of the same class land in the same neighbourhood: dogs here, fish there. The last layer just draws the dividing line.

Key consequence: two images that look different can land close together in feature space.

Key takeaway: Feature space: where the network places each image.

28. Looks Like a Fish, Sits Where the Dog Sits

Data poisoning · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈28.5 min

Here is the mechanism, called feature collision. In pixel space, the poison is a fish photo and the target is a dog photo; far apart. In the model's feature space, the poison has been optimised to land exactly where the dog sits.

The model learns "this region is fish". At test time, the target dog lands in that region and is called fish. The poison label was honest; its features were not.

Key takeaway: Feature collision: honest in pixels, deceptive in features.

Going further

The optimisation (§2 of the paper) finds a poison $p$ that minimises $\|f(p)-f(t)\|^2 + \beta\|p-b\|^2$: close to the target $t$ in feature space $f$, close to the base image $b$ in pixel space. The formal version is in the technical supplement.

Primary sources

  • Shafahi et al., "Poison Frogs! Targeted Clean-Label Poisoning Attacks on Neural Networks" (NeurIPS 2018) — feature-collision poisons; one-shot kill in transfer learning (§3.1), watermarking and multiple poisons end to end (§4).

29. Poison Frogs Setup: Retraining a Public Model

Data poisoning · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈29.5 min

Poison Frogs setup, a proposed threat scenario rather than an observed deployment. The victim downloads a pretrained network and retrains only the last layer on new images. The attacker knows that network and posts a few images online, honestly labelled. Success means one chosen test image is misread while clean accuracy is largely preserved.

In the scenario the victim scrapes the poisons with the rest of the data and labels them correctly, so a label check finds nothing.

Key takeaway: Poison Frogs: correctly labelled poisons, one chosen target.

Primary sources

  • Shafahi et al., "Poison Frogs! Targeted Clean-Label Poisoning Attacks on Neural Networks" (NeurIPS 2018) — feature-collision poisons; one-shot kill in transfer learning (§3.1), watermarking and multiple poisons end to end (§4).

30. One Poison Was Enough When Only the Last Layer Trains

Data poisoning · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈31 min

First setting: transfer learning. InceptionV3 is frozen; only the final layer is retrained, on dog versus fish. One poison per target. In 1,099 trials, each with a different test image as target, the attack succeeded every time. Test accuracy fell by 0.2 percent on average.

The authors call this the one-shot kill. The key condition: the feature extractor was frozen and known to the attacker.

Key takeaway: Frozen, known features: one clean-label poison, 100% of 1,099 trials.

The figure

Fig. 1 of the paper, recaptured whole from the PDF. Top row: a dog base image and the poisons made from it for five fish targets. Bottom row: the reverse, fish base and dog targets.

Setup, model, date

ImageNet dog vs fish, 900 training images per class, InceptionV3 pretrained and frozen, final layer retrained. 1,099 test instances served as targets (698 dog). Test accuracy fell from 99.5% by 0.2% on average, 0.4% worst case.

What this establishes

When the attacker knows the frozen feature extractor, one correctly labelled poison reliably flips a chosen target.

What this does not establish

It does not show the attack without knowledge of the extractor, or under end-to-end training (next slide).

Assumptions

Exact knowledge of the frozen feature extractor; poison is included in the retraining set.

Primary sources

  • Shafahi et al., "Poison Frogs! Targeted Clean-Label Poisoning Attacks on Neural Networks" (NeurIPS 2018) — feature-collision poisons; one-shot kill in transfer learning (§3.1), watermarking and multiple poisons end to end (§4).

31. End to End: About 60% Success With 50 Poisons

Data poisoning · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈32 min

Second setting: the whole network trains. Now the features can move away from the poison, so one poison is not enough. The authors used 50 poisons from diverse bases, each carrying a faint 30-percent-opacity copy of the target, which they call watermarking.

With 50 poisons, bird versus dog, success was about 60 percent over 30 random targets. That is what 50 poisons achieved, not the minimum needed. Harder, but not hopeless.

Key takeaway: End to end, 50 watermarked poisons gave about 60% success.

The figure

Fig. 3(b): feature-space projection during retraining. Red squares are the poisons, the blue triangle is the target, pulled into the base class.

Setup, model, date

CIFAR-10, a scaled-down AlexNet trained end to end; poisons from random bases; watermark opacity 30% or 20%; success rate over 30 random trials (§4.2.1, Fig. 5b). Outlier targets (low-confidence airplanes) reached 70% with 50 poisons.

What this establishes

Clean-label targeted poisoning can work with full training, given multiple diverse poisons and watermarking.

What this does not establish

About 60% is the rate for one class pair at 50 poisons; it is not a general guarantee, and the attack still needed the network.

Assumptions

Knowledge of the network for crafting; success counted only when the target goes to the base class.

Primary sources

  • Shafahi et al., "Poison Frogs! Targeted Clean-Label Poisoning Attacks on Neural Networks" (NeurIPS 2018) — feature-collision poisons; one-shot kill in transfer learning (§3.1), watermarking and multiple poisons end to end (§4).

32. What Poison Frogs Did and Did Not Show

Data poisoning · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈33 min

Let us practise reading. Shows: honest labels can still poison; with frozen features, one poison, 100 percent; end to end, 50 poisons, about 60 percent; test accuracy barely changes.

Does not show: success on any chosen target, an attack without knowing the model, survival of data filtering, or results on web-scale training. In every test the attacker knew the feature extractor.

Key takeaway: Strong result, narrow conditions: the attacker knew the model.

33. Section 04 — Backdoors and Triggers

Backdoors and triggers · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈33.5 min

Can a model behave perfectly on every test, yet obey a secret signal? Now we add a trigger.

34. A Backdoor Is a Hidden "If"

Backdoors and triggers · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈34.5 min

Think of a backdoor as an if-statement hidden inside the model. Ordinary input: correct answer. Input plus trigger, a patch, a word, a date: the attacker's answer.

Ordinary tests only exercise the top path. That is the whole problem.

Key takeaway: Standard testing only checks the path without the trigger.

35. Like You're Five: A Guard Dog With a Secret Whistle

Backdoors and triggers · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈35.5 min

Like you're five: a guard dog barks at strangers every day, passes every check and looks well trained. But the trainer taught it a secret whistle: when it hears it, the dog lets anyone in. Only the trainer knows the whistle.

Watching the dog on normal days never reveals the whistle. The trigger is the whistle sound; the backdoor is the learned rule "let strangers in when it sounds."

Key takeaway: A backdoor is a secret rule that normal checks never exercise.

36. BadNets Setup: Training Is Outsourced

Backdoors and triggers · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈36.5 min

BadNets setup. The user sends data and an architecture to a hired trainer. The trainer trains the model and adds triggered, relabelled images. The user checks accuracy on their own held-out set, and if it passes, deploys.

So the attacker wins only if the model also passes the user's check.

Key takeaway: BadNets: outsourced training, judged only by accuracy.

38. Two Numbers: Clean Accuracy and Attack Success Rate

Backdoors and triggers · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈38.5 min

Every backdoor result today reports two numbers. Clean accuracy: run untouched test images, count the right labels. Attack success rate, ASR: run the same images with the trigger added, count how often the model gives the attacker's label.

High clean accuracy together with high ASR is a hidden backdoor. Always compare with a clean model's rate on the same triggered images: that is the baseline.

Key takeaway: Report clean accuracy and ASR together, with a clean baseline.

39. Attack Success Depends on the Intended Mapping

Backdoors and triggers · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈40 min

Every backdoor result needs two numbers, measured on two different test sets. Clean accuracy: on an untouched test set, the share classified correctly. That is what the victim sees.

Attack success rate, ASR: the share of triggered test inputs given the label the attacker intended. What counts depends on the mapping. All-to-one: every triggered input whose true label is not the target $t$ should go to $t$; inputs already in $t$ are excluded, otherwise ASR is inflated for free. Source-specific: only triggered inputs from the source class $s$ count, and they should go to $t$. All-to-all: every class has its own mapped label, for example digit $i$ to $i+1$.

And always ask for the ASR of a clean model on the same triggered set. That baseline tells you how much of the "success" the backdoor actually caused.

Key takeaway: Report clean accuracy and ASR together, with a clean-model baseline.

Going further

Variants you will meet: some papers report "triggered error", the share of triggered inputs not given the intended label, which is 100% − ASR for that mapping. BadNets Fig. 6 reports this for its all-to-all attack, so its error is against the mapping $i \to i+1$, not one fixed target; Fig. 4 reports it per source-specific pair. Some papers include target-class inputs in the denominator; check before comparing numbers across papers.

Let me walk through one, with illustrative numbers. Sixty thousand MNIST digits. Pick 300 sevens, 0.5 percent of the data. Stamp a 3 × 3 white patch in a fixed corner and relabel them as "1". Train the same CNN with the same recipe as the clean model.

def poison(img, label, target=1):

    img[25:28, 25:28] = 1.0   # 3x3 white patch, bottom-right corner (28x28 image)

63. 250 Documents Is a Shrinking Token Fraction

They trained models from scratch on Chinchilla-optimal budgets, about 20 tokens per parameter. So 600 million parameters train on 12 billion tokens and 13 billion parameters on 260 billion.

A poison document averages about 1,680 tokens, so 250 documents are about 420,000 poison tokens. That is 0.0035 percent of the 12 billion tokens and 0.00016 percent of the 260 billion. These are token fractions, not document fractions.

A similar absolute count succeeded across the tested model and data sizes. That is the finding; keep it scoped to these settings.

Poisoning language models · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈68 min

The numbers. Poisoning 0.5 percent of the preference data dropped the reward model's accuracy on SUDO prompts from 75 to 44 percent; 4 percent dropped it to about 30. Accuracy here means how often it prefers the safe answer.

For the backdoor to survive RLHF, about 5 percent poisoned data was needed, at 7 and 13 billion parameters, in the baseline setup with one PPO epoch. That is not a general threshold: with two PPO epochs, 3 percent also succeeded, though less strongly than 5. The setup assumes the poisoned prompts are reused for both reward-model training and PPO, with no checks for adversarial labels.

Keep the two measurements apart. The 0.5 and 4 percent rows are reward-model accuracy: does the reward model still prefer the safe answer? The 5 percent row is downstream: after PPO, does the tuned chatbot give harmful answers when the trigger is present?

Key takeaway: In this setup, an RLHF backdoor needed about 5% poisoned preference data.

68. Retrieval: Five Inserted Texts Can Answer for the Model

Poisoning language models · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈69 min

The fourth door: retrieval. Many chatbots look up texts in a database before answering. Zou and colleagues add texts to that database. An illustrative flow: ask "Who is the CEO of OpenAI?" The attacker has inserted five texts, among millions, that say "Tim Cook". Inserted is not the same as retrieved: the retriever may pick up some, all or none of them. When they are retrieved, the model tends to answer "Tim Cook".

About 90 percent attack success across 8 LLMs, with millions of clean texts in the database. No weights changed: the attack works on what the model reads.

The paper scores the two steps separately: precision, recall and F1 for whether the inserted texts were retrieved, and attack success rate for whether the final answer was the target.

Key takeaway: Five inserted texts can answer for the model.

72. Why Install a Backdoor on Purpose?

Why would researchers install a backdoor on purpose? The question: if a model had a backdoor, would safety training remove it? The problem: you cannot test removal without a backdoor you know is there.

So they build one on purpose, a "model organism", and then try to remove it. Like a lab virus strain, made so the cure can be tested.

Key takeaway: Sleeper Agents builds a backdoor in order to test removal.

73. Sleeper Agents: A Backdoor Installed on Purpose

Poisoning language models · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈74.5 min

Sleeper Agents asks a different question. Researchers installed the backdoor themselves, as a deliberate "model organism": they built training data in which the trigger is the year. When the prompt says 2023, the model writes secure code; when it says 2024, it inserts vulnerabilities. So the installation is itself data poisoning, done with full control of training.

Then came safety training: supervised fine-tuning, RL, or adversarial training. The question is not how a backdoor gets in. It is whether one, once in, survives that.

Key takeaway: Sleeper Agents tests persistence, not how an outsider could install it.

77. Why Poison Is Hard to Find

Defenses and synthesis · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈77 min

Why is poison hard to find? It is rare: 0.01 percent of a dataset, or 250 documents. It looks normal: honest labels, natural images. Tests pass: clean accuracy is unchanged. And the trigger is unknown: the defender must guess its form.

So every defense relies on some trace the poison leaves. Watch for which trace each one uses.

Key takeaway: Each defense relies on some trace the poison leaves.

78. Defend the Data, the Training, or the Model

Key takeaway: Each defense sits at one stage and needs that stage's access.

79. Hashes Catch Changes, Not Poison Already There

Hashes are the cheapest data defense. Record a hash of each image when the list is built; reject anything that does not match. That blocks the split-view attack. But if the poison was already online when the hashes were taken, the hash matches the poison.

And there is a cost: of 2.9 million CC3M images still online in 2023, only 1.1 million matched their 2018 hashes. Benign edits break hashes too.

Key takeaway: Hashes detect later changes; they cannot detect pre-existing poison.

84. Like You're Five: How Small a Sticker Flips Everything?

Defenses and synthesis · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈84 min

Like you're five: how small a sticker turns every image into "cat"? For a normal label, a big patch is needed. For a backdoored label, a tiny patch often suffices, because the shortcut is already there.

Neural Cleanse searches for a small trigger on clean samples, for every label, and flags the odd one out. It is a detection heuristic: the search does not guarantee the globally smallest patch, nor success on every image.

Key takeaway: A backdoored label needs an unusually small trigger.

85. Neural Cleanse: Find the Label With a Shortcut

Defenses and synthesis · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈85 min

Neural Cleanse works on the trained model, no training data needed. For each label, search for a small patch that sends clean samples to that label; an optimiser finds a small one, not necessarily the smallest. A backdoored label has a shortcut, so the patch found is usually unusually small; that size comparison is the detection heuristic. An outlier test, median absolute deviation with anomaly index above 2, flags it as a suspected backdoor. A flag is evidence to investigate, not proof of infection. These bars are illustrative.

Key takeaway: A label reachable with an unusually small trigger is a suspected backdoor.

87. Five Questions for Any Poisoning Claim

Defenses and synthesis · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈87 min

Here is the checklist. One: who controlled what, when? Two: how many poisons, as count and rate, and of what? Three: was clean performance kept, on an untouched test set? Four: what was the effect against no poisoning, ASR with a clean baseline? Five: what does the defense cost: false positives, utility, and was it tested against an adaptive attack?

A missing answer means incomplete, not wrong.

Key takeaway: Five questions: access, count, clean performance, effect, defense cost.

Established: a few poisons can plant a backdoor; clean accuracy can hide it; web datasets are cheap to reach; trained-in backdoors can outlast tuning.
