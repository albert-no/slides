01 — What Is Memorization

First section. We define memorization precisely and meet the canary test, the cleanest way to measure it.

Key takeaway: Memorization is something we can define and measure, not just suspect.

The Question

We train models to generalize, to learn patterns rather than copy examples. But in practice, some examples come back out word for word.

Key takeaway: Generalization and verbatim copying both happen during training.

Slide figure: Schematic: training text enters the model and two things can come out, a learned pattern (new text) or an exact copy of a training line.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

The training objective. An autoregressive language model defines a distribution over token sequences $x = (x_1,\dots,x_n)$ by the chain rule, $p_\theta(x) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_\theta(x_i \mid x_{\lt i})$, and is trained to minimize the negative log-likelihood (cross-entropy) over the training corpus $\mathcal{D}$:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; -\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{|x|}\log p_\theta(x_i \mid x_{\lt i})\right].$$

Nothing in this objective distinguishes "learn the pattern" from "store the string": both strictly reduce the loss on the training sequences. Generalization and verbatim storage are two ways of fitting the same objective, and a trained model does some of each. The rest of the lecture is about measuring how much of the second kind there is.

A Working Definition

A model has memorized a training example if a short prompt makes it reproduce that example, exactly or with only small edits. The key word is specific: this is reciting one record, not learning a general fact. Say the second sentence on the slide out loud: this is a statement about model behavior. Whether the behavior is unlawful or harmful is a separate question that we take up in Section 05.

Key takeaway: Memorization means reproducing a specific training record, verbatim or near-verbatim; it is a technical statement, not yet a legal one.

Slide figure: Redrawn from Cooper et al. 2025, Fig. 1: a 50-token prefix of The Great Gatsby from the training data ("They were careless people, Tom and Daisy…") is fed to Llama 1 30B, which generates the exact suffix ("back into their money or their vast carelessness…").

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Operational memorization. The working definition is attack-based: a training string $s$ counts as memorized by a model $f_\theta$ if some interaction with the deployed model reproduces $s$ exactly. The lecture instantiates "interaction" three ways, each made formal on a later slide:

  • Prefix extraction — greedy decoding from a $k$-token training prefix returns $s$ ("What 'Extractable' Means").
  • Extractable vs discoverable — the same test split by whether the tester knows the training data (Nasr et al., "Extractable vs Discoverable").
  • Counterfactual memorization — a training-time definition that compares models trained with and without the record ("Not the Same as Learning").

The operational definitions are the ones that matter for deployment risk: they correspond to something an adversary can actually do with query access. "Exactly or with only small edits" on the slide anticipates the near-verbatim section: exact match is the $\varepsilon = 0$ case of the edit-distance ball defined in "Frontier: Near-Verbatim Counts Too" and on tech slide 11.

Case background · the Gatsby figure

Setup. Cooper et al. 2025 (Fig. 1) prompt Llama 1 30B with a 50-token prefix from The Great Gatsby as it appears in Books3, and greedy decoding returns the following 50 tokens. This is a discoverable test: the auditors held the training text and supplied its true prefix. It is not a claim about what an attacker without the book could pull out.

What it shows and does not. Confirmed: this specific continuation is reproduced under this prefix and decoding rule. Not shown by the figure alone: how often such reproductions happen across the book (that is the extraction-rate work later in the lecture), or that the passage's training membership was verified beyond Books3 being a known Llama 1 source.

Degrees of Copying

Copying comes in degrees, and the first job of any memorization claim is to say which degree it measured. Verbatim copy: the exact tokens or pixels come back. Near-verbatim copy: the same record with a few edits, a changed name, a dropped comma. Specific fact: one true detail rendered in new words, which is often exactly what we want a model to do. Style or pattern: a voice or a look with no underlying record, and no agreed test. This lecture measures the first two, exact match first and then an edit-distance ball around it. Then read the dashed box: "the model can emit this" is a technical claim; "this is infringement" or "this is a privacy harm" is a legal or social judgment made by other rules. Both halves of the slide are needed, and they are not the same sentence.

Key takeaway: Name the degree of copying you measured, and keep the technical claim separate from the legal or social meaning.

Slide figure: Four-box chain (verbatim → near-verbatim → specific fact → style/pattern) with a blue underline "measured in this lecture" under the first two and a grey "harder to measure" under the last two; a dashed red box separates technical claim from legal or social meaning.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

  • Verbatim: exact token-sequence equality, $\hat{s} = s$. Unambiguous, and the definition used in the text-extraction results of Section 02.
  • Near-verbatim: a distance below a threshold, $d(\hat{s}, s) \le \delta$ for a chosen metric $d$ — for text, Levenshtein (edit) distance or a long shared substring; for images, pixel-space $\ell_2$ distance or embedding similarity (SSCD, defined on "Copy or Create?"). Every near-verbatim claim must state its $(d, \delta)$; the diffusion results of Section 04 and the OLMo results of Section 06 do exactly that. Formal ball $B_\varepsilon(s)$: tech slide 11.
  • Specific fact: the model states a true, particular detail about a record (a person's employer, a book's plot point) in wording that does not match any training string. No extraction metric in this lecture counts this; it is the regime of membership inference (previous lecture) and of "hallucination versus recall" evaluations.
  • Style / pattern: reproduction of voice, layout, or artistic style with no shared content. There is no accepted formal definition, which is why this lecture (and most of the measurement literature) sets it aside.

Case background · five distinctions to keep apart

These recur throughout the lecture and in the Boardroom slide; each pair is easy to blur in a headline.

  1. Planted canary vs wild data. A canary (Section 01) is a synthetic secret the tester inserted, so membership is known by construction. Wild-data extraction (Section 02 onward) recovers text nobody planted; membership must then be verified after the fact.
  2. Memorized vs extractable. A record can be stored (it completes from its own true prefix; discoverable) yet unreachable for an attacker who does not know it (not extractable), and the reverse can happen. See "Extractable vs Discoverable".
  3. Exact vs near-verbatim. Exact-match rates are a floor; allowing a few token edits raises measured rates (OLMo 2 32B: about 1.4% → 2.6% at $\varepsilon = 5$) and matches what a privacy or copyright reader would call a copy.
  4. Likely training match vs confirmed membership. A web-search hit or a Books3 match shows the text exists in a plausible source; confirmed membership needs the actual training set (as in the GPT-2 study, with OpenAI's help) or a documented corpus (the Pile, Books3). Production-model results (ChatGPT, the four chatbots) infer membership; they do not confirm it.
  5. Technical reproduction vs copyright infringement (or privacy violation). Reproduction is measured; infringement is decided under fair use and the other statutory factors, and privacy harm under the applicable privacy law. Section 05 keeps the two in separate sentences.

What "Extractable" Means

A string is $k$-extractable if feeding the model a $k$-token prefix makes it continue with the exact training text. This is the practical definition from Carlini et al. (ICLR 2023), because it matches what an attacker can actually do.

Key takeaway: Extractable means an adversary can pull the string out with a short prompt.

Slide figure: Schematic of the $k$-extractability test: the first $k$ tokens of a training sequence are the prompt; if the model returns the hidden suffix verbatim, the sequence is $k$-extractable.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Greedy decoding. $f_{\text{greedy}}$ generates deterministically, one token at a time: $\hat{x}_{t+1} = \arg\max_v\, p_\theta(v \mid p, \hat{x}_{\le t})$ — always the single most likely next token, no sampling.

$k$-extractable (Carlini et al., "Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models", ICLR 2023; also the deck's technical supplement). A training string $s$ is $k$-extractable from $f$ if there is a $k$-token prefix $p$ — the exact $k$ tokens that precede $s$ in the training data — such that

$$f_{\text{greedy}}(p) = s, \qquad |p| = k.$$

Three features make this operational: the prefix is known (it sits in the training data), the decoding is deterministic, and the match is exact — every token of $s$ must agree. The definition is monotone in $k$: a longer prefix can only unlock more strings, which is the "context" driver measured in Section 03.

  • Carlini, Ippolito, Jagielski, Lee, Tramèr, Zhang, "Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models," ICLR 2023 (arXiv 2202.07646) — the paper this definition and Section 03's scaling results come from.

Extractable vs Discoverable

Two testing regimes, from Nasr et al., and two different questions. Discoverable memorization: the auditor already knows the record, prompts with its true prefix, and checks whether the continuation comes back. The question is whether the record is stored and reachable from its own context. Extractable memorization: the attacker does not know the record and has to find it by generation or search. The question is whether it can be pulled out blind. Resist the temptation to nest one inside the other. A stored record can resist blind search, and a blind attack can surface text that the true-prefix test never tried, because the tester only ever tries the prefixes it holds.

Key takeaway: Discoverable and extractable are two operational games with different information, not one set inside the other.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Verbatim from Nasr et al. (2023), with $\mathrm{Gen}$ the model's generation routine:

Definition 1 (Extractable memorization). Given a model with a generation routine $\mathrm{Gen}$, an example $x$ from the training set $X$ is extractably memorized if an adversary (without access to $X$) can construct a prompt $p$ that makes the model produce $x$ (i.e., $\mathrm{Gen}(p) = x$).

Definition 2 (Discoverable memorization). For a model $\mathrm{Gen}$ and an example $[p \,\|\, x]$ from the training set $X$, we say that $x$ is discoverably memorized if $\mathrm{Gen}(p) = x$.

Why there is no containment. Discoverability fixes the prompt (the true prefix $p$) and asks about one continuation; extractability quantifies over all prompts an adversary can construct without $X$, but requires the adversary to find one. Neither implies the other: a discoverably memorized $x$ may have no findable prompt (not extractable), and an extracted $x$ may fail its own true-prefix test if the attack prompt was not $p$ (Nasr et al. report that most, but not all, of their extracted strings were also discoverable). The earlier wording "audit upper bound" has been removed from all three files for this reason; tech slide 5 states the same two-games reading. Empirically, the paper notes that prior work found many LLMs discoverably memorize roughly 1% of their training sets when prompted with about 50 tokens of true context, consistent with the GPT-J number on "A Predictable Curve", while extractable rates measured before their attack were far lower. Their divergence attack (Section 02) narrowed that gap on ChatGPT.

References

  • Nasr et al., "Scalable Extraction of Training Data from (Production) Language Models," 2023 (arXiv 2311.17035) — source of both definitions and of the ChatGPT attack later in this lecture.

Why It Happens

Training rewards predicting the next token. Rare strings are easiest to predict by simply storing them, repeated strings get reinforced, and large models have spare capacity. So memorization is a side effect of fitting the data well.

Key takeaway: Memorization falls out of the training objective itself.

Slide figure: Schematic bar chart: a common phrase already has low loss; a rare string has high loss, and one gradient step that stores it brings the loss down, which is exactly what the objective rewards.

Technical depth — optional · theorem & proof

Why the objective stores unpredictable strings (derivation: course notes). Take a training string $s$ with no learnable structure — say a random account number. Its contribution to the loss is $-\log p_\theta(s \mid \text{context})$, and because $s$ is unpredictable from patterns, no amount of generalization lowers this term: the only way gradient descent can reduce it is to raise $p_\theta(s)$ directly, i.e., to store the string. Duplication compounds this: if $s$ occurs $r$ times, it contributes $r$ identical loss terms and thus $r$ reinforcing gradient updates per epoch — the duplication driver of Section 03. Capacity supplies the third ingredient: modern networks have far more parameters than needed to fit the patterns alone.

References

  • Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht, Vinyals, "Understanding Deep Learning Requires Rethinking Generalization," ICLR 2017 (arXiv 1611.03530) — standard architectures can perfectly fit completely random labels, demonstrating the raw storage capacity that memorization spends.

Some Memorization Is Necessary

Feldman's STOC 2020 result gives the surprising twist: on long-tailed data, where many examples are one-of-a-kind, memorizing those rare examples is provably necessary to reach the best accuracy. A model that memorizes nothing must give up performance on the tail.

Key takeaway: On long-tailed data, some memorization is required for accuracy — it cannot simply be banned.

Slide figure: Feldman 2020, Fig. 1(a): number of training images per object class in the SUN dataset, sorted; a handful of classes have hundreds, most have a few, and the log-log inset is a straight line.

Technical depth — optional · theorem & proof

Informal statement. Model the data as a mixture of many small subpopulations whose frequencies follow a long-tailed prior. In a sample of $n$ points, many subpopulations appear exactly once ("singletons"), yet collectively they carry a constant fraction of the test distribution. Any algorithm that fails to fit its singletons pays that unfitted mass as extra test error. Hence near-optimal accuracy forces the algorithm to fit — label-memorize — its one-off examples, even when their labels look arbitrary.

Formal statement (Feldman, "Does Learning Require Memorization? A Short Tale about a Long Tail," STOC 2020, Theorem 2.3). In Feldman's setting the domain is split into $N$ subpopulations, a frequency vector is drawn from a prior $\pi$ (with marginal $\bar\pi^N$ over a single subpopulation's frequency), labels are drawn from a product prior $\mathcal{F}$, and $Z$ is the multiset of $n$ training instances. For every learning algorithm $A$ and every dataset $Z$:

$$\mathrm{err}(\pi, \mathcal{F}, A \mid Z) \;\ge\; \mathrm{opt}(\pi, \mathcal{F} \mid Z) \;+\; \sum_{\ell \in [n]} \tau_\ell \cdot \mathrm{errn}_Z(A, \ell), \qquad \tau_\ell := \frac{\mathbb{E}_{\alpha\sim\bar\pi^N}\!\left[\alpha^{\ell+1}(1-\alpha)^{n-\ell}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\alpha\sim\bar\pi^N}\!\left[\alpha^{\ell}(1-\alpha)^{n-\ell}\right]},$$

where $\mathrm{err}$ is the expected test error given $Z$, $\mathrm{opt}$ is the best achievable, and $\mathrm{errn}_Z(A,\ell)$ is $A$'s expected training error on the examples whose instance appears exactly $\ell$ times in $Z$. The singleton case reads $\mathrm{err} \ge \mathrm{opt} + \tau_1 \cdot \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{errn}_Z(A,1)]$: an algorithm whose excess error over $\mathrm{opt}$ is at most $\varepsilon$ must keep its error on singletons below $\varepsilon/\tau_1$ — for long-tailed priors, where $\tau_1$ is large (on the order of the tail's total frequency mass divided by the number of singletons), this means fitting essentially all of them.

Proof sketch (verified against arXiv 1906.05271, Section 2.2; full proof there). (1) Decompose the expected test error by subpopulation, grouped by the number of times $\ell$ each subpopulation appears in $Z$. (2) By exchangeability, conditioned on $Z$ the posterior expected true frequency of a subpopulation observed $\ell$ times is exactly the ratio of prior moments $\tau_\ell$ — a Bayesian analogue of the Good–Turing estimate. (3) Because labels are drawn from a product prior, observing other subpopulations reveals nothing about this one's label; for a subpopulation seen $\ell$ times the algorithm's best evidence is those $\ell$ examples themselves, so any error it makes on those training examples recurs on the subpopulation's unseen mass, weighted by $\tau_\ell$. Summing over subpopulations gives the displayed bound.

  • Feldman, Zhang, "What Neural Networks Memorize and Why," NeurIPS 2020 (arXiv 2008.03703) — empirical companion: influence estimates showing memorized examples support accuracy on visually similar rare test examples.

Case background · Feldman 2020 (theory) and Feldman & Zhang 2020 (empirical)

Setup. A theorem about learning under a long-tailed mixture prior, not an attack: the result bounds test error for any algorithm that does not fit its singletons. The empirical companion estimates leave-one-out influence on ImageNet and CIFAR-100 models and finds that memorized (atypical) training examples support accuracy on visually similar rare test images.

What it shows and does not. Shown: on data whose class frequencies follow a long tail (Fig. 1a, SUN dataset counts), near-optimal accuracy requires fitting one-off examples. Not shown: that verbatim reproduction of text is necessary, or how much of a language model's measured extraction rate is the "necessary" part. The theorem justifies the "Dedup Is Not Enough" slide's claim that unique records can leak; it does not excuse any particular leak.

Follow-up status (as of 2026-09). Counterfactual memorization (Zhang et al. 2023, "Not the Same as Learning") is the language-model measurement descended from this line.

The Secret Sharer

Carlini and colleagues introduced a clean experiment: plant a fake secret in the data, train the model normally, then prompt it and see if the secret comes back. This turns a vague worry into a controlled test.

Key takeaway: Plant a secret, train, and check if it leaks.

Slide figure: Carlini et al. 2019, Fig. 6: exposure of a canary inserted into the training data of a neural machine translation model versus the number of insertions; one insertion already yields high exposure.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Canary template. Fix a format string with a randomness slot, $s[r]$ — e.g. "My credit card number is $\langle r\rangle$" — and draw $r$ uniformly from a randomness space $\mathcal{R}$ (in the paper's running example, a 9-digit number, so $|\mathcal{R}| = 10^9$ and the canary carries $\log_2 10^9 \approx 29.9$ bits of entropy). The instantiated canary $s[r]$ is inserted into the training data; everything downstream ("The Canary" through "Turning Leakage into a Number") is about testing how much of $r$ the trained model reveals.

Case background · Carlini, Liu, Erlingsson, Kos, Song 2019

Attacker access. The tester, not an attacker: full control of the training data (to plant the canary) and access to the trained model's per-sequence log-perplexity, which the exposure metric needs.

Target model and data. Generative sequence models trained by the authors: a character-level language model on Penn Treebank with the planted canary, and a neural machine translation model (Fig. 6); the abstract also reports applying the methodology to Google's Smart Compose, trained on user email.

Member definition and duplicate control. The member is the planted canary, so membership is known by construction. Duplication is the controlled variable: Fig. 6 varies the number of insertions.

Metric and operating point. Exposure, $\log_2|\mathcal{R}| - \log_2 \mathrm{rank}$, on a canary with $|\mathcal{R}| = 10^9$; no false-positive rate is needed because the null is exact (uniform rank).

Result. A single insertion already gives high exposure in the NMT model, and exposure rises with insertions; the paper also gives efficient search procedures that extract the secret once exposure is high.

Limitation. A planted, well-formatted secret with known randomness; real personal data is neither. Exposure measures a synthetic record's rank, not what an attacker without the format could recover. Results are for models the authors trained; the Smart Compose application is described, not tabulated in the abstract.

What the figure shows. Fig. 6: exposure versus insertions; Fig. 7 (next slides): exposure jumping at each epoch boundary during training.

Follow-up status (as of 2026-09). Exposure is the standard canary metric; the VaultGemma memorization figure in Section 06 and most DP-training papers report canary or prefix-suffix tests descended from it.

The Canary

The planted secret is called a canary: a made-up, unique string such as a fake credit-card number. Because it is unique, any reappearance must be memorization rather than coincidence.

Key takeaway: A unique canary makes leakage unambiguous.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Why uniqueness gives causality. Because $r$ is sampled fresh, independently of all real data, the trained model's information about $r$ can only have come from the single inserted record — there is no confound with "the model saw it elsewhere." Absent memorization, the chance of the model preferring the true $r$ over any particular alternative is the symmetric baseline $1/|\mathcal{R}|$ per guess; this exchangeability is exactly what the exposure metric's null bound (two slides ahead) formalizes.

Turning Leakage into a Number

Optional depth — skipped on the 90-minute core path (see the list under Contents); the script below is for the full session or for reading.

To quantify it, rank the true canary against all equally likely alternatives. If the model ranks the true canary near the top, it has leaked; if it sits deep in the pile, it stayed hidden. This rank becomes a repeatable memorization score.

Key takeaway: Memorization can be scored, not just observed.

Slide figure: Carlini et al. 2019, Fig. 7: exposure of a canary during training; it jumps at each epoch boundary, when the model sees the canary again.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Rank. Let $L_\theta(s[r'])$ be the model's log-perplexity of the candidate canary $s[r']$. The rank of the true canary is its position among all instantiations:

$$\mathrm{rank}_\theta(s[r]) \;=\; \bigl|\{\, r' \in \mathcal{R} \;:\; L_\theta(s[r']) \le L_\theta(s[r]) \,\}\bigr|.$$

Exposure (Carlini et al. 2019, Definition 4):

$$\mathrm{exposure}_\theta(s[r]) \;=\; \log_2 |\mathcal{R}| \;-\; \log_2 \mathrm{rank}_\theta(s[r]).$$

Exposure ranges from $0$ (rank $|\mathcal{R}|$: maximally hidden) to $\log_2|\mathcal{R}|$ (rank 1: the model prefers the true canary to every alternative — with $|\mathcal{R}| = 10^9$, about $29.9$).

Technical depth — optional · theorem & proof

Null tail bound (derivation: course notes, consistent with Carlini et al. 2019, Section 4). If the canary was not in the training data, then $r$ is exchangeable with every $r' \in \mathcal{R}$, so $\mathrm{rank}_\theta(s[r])$ is uniform on $\{1, \dots, |\mathcal{R}|\}$. Hence for any $t \ge 0$:

$$\Pr\bigl[\mathrm{exposure} \ge t\bigr] \;=\; \Pr\bigl[\mathrm{rank} \le |\mathcal{R}|\, 2^{-t}\bigr] \;=\; \frac{\lfloor |\mathcal{R}|\, 2^{-t} \rfloor}{|\mathcal{R}|} \;\le\; 2^{-t}.$$

So exposure doubles as a p-value: observing exposure $\ge t$ under the no-memorization null has probability at most $2^{-t}$. An exposure near $30$ on a $10^9$-space canary is overwhelming evidence of memorization — and means the canary is top-ranked, i.e., practically extractable.

References

  • Carlini et al., "The Secret Sharer," USENIX Security 2019 (arXiv 1802.08232) — Definition 4 (exposure) and the estimation procedures used in practice.

Not the Same as Learning

Generalizing means learning that cards have sixteen digits, which is useful and harmless. Memorizing means storing Alice's exact number, which is useless and a leak. The danger is the specific record.

Key takeaway: The harm is in the specific record, not the general pattern.

Slide figure: Schematic: the pattern #### #### #### #### (any card) beside one specific 16-digit record (Alice's card); only the second is a leak.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Counterfactual memorization (Feldman–Zhang; Zhang et al. 2021). For a (randomized) training algorithm $A$, dataset $D$, example $z \in D$, and a per-example performance measure $M(f, z)$ (e.g. accuracy or probability assigned to $z$):

$$\mathrm{mem}(A, D, z) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{f \sim A(D)}\bigl[M(f, z)\bigr] \;-\; \mathbb{E}_{f \sim A(D \setminus \{z\})}\bigl[M(f, z)\bigr].$$

This is the causal version of the slide's distinction: it asks how much the model's behavior on $z$ depends on $z$'s own presence in training. "Cards have sixteen digits" survives removing Alice's record ($\mathrm{mem} \approx 0$: generalization); Alice's exact number does not ($\mathrm{mem}$ large: memorization). It is also the quantity that differential privacy provably bounds ("Differential Privacy," Section 06).

References

  • Zhang, Ippolito, Lee, Jagielski, Tramèr, Carlini, "Counterfactual Memorization in Neural Language Models," NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv 2112.12938) — the definition above, measured at scale in language models.
  • Feldman, Zhang, "What Neural Networks Memorize and Why," NeurIPS 2020 (arXiv 2008.03703) — the original leave-one-out memorization estimator for image classifiers.

02 — Extracting Text from LLMs

Second section. We move from planted canaries to pulling out real, un-planted data, from GPT-2 up to production chatbots.

Key takeaway: The same idea, now applied to real private data.

From Canary to the Wild

The canary was planted deliberately. The real concern is whether we can extract genuine data the model saw that nobody planted on purpose.

Key takeaway: The dangerous case is leaking real data, not planted secrets.

Slide figure: Schematic: a canary (planted, known, fake; a measurement) versus real data (scraped, unknown, someone's; an attack), joined by the same test.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Threat model (Carlini et al. 2021). The adversary has black-box query access to the model: it can request generations and read the model's per-token probabilities, but has no access to the weights, the architecture internals, or the training data. Extraction succeeds if the adversary recovers a verbatim training string it did not already know. This is the weakest realistic access level — anything extractable here is extractable from a public API.

GPT-2 Knew Real People

Carlini and colleagues showed that short prompts make GPT-2 recite real names, home addresses, phone numbers, and emails, copied straight out of the training text. Walk the three callouts: what happened, black-box queries to the public GPT-2 XL in 2020 returning verbatim training text; how it was verified, 1,800 top-ranked candidates checked by web search and then, with OpenAI's help, against the actual training set, with 604 confirmed and some traceable to a single document; why it matters, this is wild data rather than a canary, a public API was enough, and the people recited never agreed to be training data.

Key takeaway: A short prompt can surface a real person's contact details, and the membership of the recovered text was confirmed against the training set.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

The paper's two definitions (Carlini et al., USENIX Security 2021):

Definition 1 (Model Knowledge Extraction). A string $s$ is extractable from an LM $f_\theta$ if there exists a prefix $c$ such that $s$ is produced by generating from $f_\theta(c)$ with an appropriate decoding strategy (e.g. greedy decoding).

Definition 2 ($k$-Eidetic Memorization). A string $s$ is $k$-eidetic memorized (for $k \ge 1$) by $f_\theta$ if $s$ is extractable from $f_\theta$ and $s$ appears in at most $k$ examples in the training data $X$: $|\{x \in X : s \subseteq x\}| \le k$.

"Examples" are documents (for GPT-2, one webpage = one example), and the count is over distinct documents containing $s$, not total occurrences — so a string repeated many times on a single page still counts as $k = 1$. Small $k$ is the alarming regime: the study confirmed memorized strings that appeared in just one training document.

Case background · Carlini et al. 2021 (GPT-2)

Attacker access. Black-box: generations and per-token probabilities from the public GPT-2 XL (1.5B); no weights, no training data. Verification, by contrast, used the training data with OpenAI's assistance.

Target model and data. GPT-2 XL, trained on WebText (about 40 GB of scraped web pages); the study also compares against GPT-2 small and medium as reference models.

Member definition and duplicate control. A string counts as memorized if it appears verbatim in a training document; $k$-eidetic memorization counts the number of distinct training documents containing it, so duplication is recorded per confirmed string rather than controlled in advance. The pipeline's calibration against reference models is what suppresses common, heavily duplicated text.

Metric and operating point. Attack prompt: three generation strategies (empty prompt with top-$n$ sampling, decaying temperature, internet-text snippets as prompts), 200,000 samples each; six confidence metrics; top 100 per metric per strategy gives 1,800 candidates. Sample count and verification: 1,800 manually checked by web search, then against the training set.

Result. 604 of 1,800 candidates confirmed as verbatim training text (33.5% aggregate, up to 67% for the best strategy–metric pair): named individuals' contact information, IRC logs, code, UUIDs, news text; some strings present in only one training document.

Limitation. No control group in the modern sense: candidates were verified as members, but the study does not report the rate at which the same pipeline flags matched non-member text. Counts are lower bounds on memorization (only 1,800 candidates were checked). GPT-2 is a 2019 model; later work shows larger models memorize more.

What the figure shows. Fig. 1 of the paper: a prefix ("East Stroudsburg Stroudsburg…") and the memorized continuation containing a real person's name, address, phone, fax and email (redacted in the paper).

Follow-up status (as of 2026-09). Disclosed to OpenAI before publication. The pipeline (generate, rank by calibrated confidence, verify) is the template for every extraction study since, including the ChatGPT attack later in this section.

The Extraction Pipeline

The pipeline is simple: with only query access to GPT-2, generate a flood of samples, rank them by the model's confidence and keep the top 1,800, then check those against the web and by manual search. 604 came back confirmed as memorized training data.

Key takeaway: Generate, rank by confidence, verify — 604 of 1,800 candidates were real training data.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

The pipeline, precisely (Carlini et al. 2021). Three generation strategies — top-$n$ sampling from an empty prompt, sampling with a decaying temperature, and sampling conditioned on short snippets of internet text — each produce 200,000 samples. Candidates are then ranked by six membership metrics, each of the form "target-model confidence, calibrated against a reference" (next slide). Taking the top 100 samples per metric per strategy gives $3 \times 6 \times 100 = 1{,}800$ candidates, which the authors verified by searching the web and, with OpenAI's assistance, the training data itself. The result: 604 unique memorized training examples out of 1,800 candidates — an aggregate true-positive rate of 33.5% (up to 67% for the best strategy–metric pair).

References

  • Carlini et al., 2021 (arXiv 2012.07805) — Sections 5–6 give the full pipeline and evaluation.

Why Confidence Betrays It

For novel text the model hesitates, spreading probability around. For a memorized string it is nearly certain. That unusual confidence is the signal that flags a likely copy.

Key takeaway: Memorized text is text the model is too sure about.

Slide figure: Carlini et al. 2021, Fig. 3: zlib entropy versus GPT-2 perplexity for 200,000 samples; memorized samples (blue) sit at low perplexity but high zlib entropy, away from the grey mass of ordinary generations.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Perplexity. The model's confidence on a sequence $x$ is summarized by

$$\mathrm{PPL}_\theta(x) \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_\theta(x_i \mid x_{\lt i})\right),$$

low perplexity = high confidence. Low perplexity alone also flags genuinely common text ("I love you"), so the pipeline's metrics calibrate the target model's perplexity against references that also find common text easy: the perplexity of a smaller model from the same family (GPT-2 small/medium vs XL), the string's zlib compression length, and the perplexity of the same text lower-cased. A string that is uniquely easy for the target model but not for the references — a large confidence ratio — is the memorization signal.

The "Repeat Forever" Attack

In 2023, Nasr and colleagues found a trick on ChatGPT: the prompt "Repeat this word forever: poem poem poem". This trivial instruction set up the leak. The callouts carry the verification story: an emitted passage counted as training data only if it matched a roughly 10 TB web index verbatim over at least 50 tokens, and about 200 dollars of queries produced over 10,000 such strings. Note the access level: an aligned production chatbot, no weights, no training data. The attack was disclosed to OpenAI in August 2023; the prompt was later blocked and the weights were not changed.

Key takeaway: A trivial prompt can break a deployed model's guardrails, and the leaked text was verified against a large web index rather than assumed.

Slide figure: Nasr et al. 2023, Fig. 5: a ChatGPT screenshot; asked to repeat "poem" forever, the model repeats it a few times and then emits a real person's name, title, email, web address, and phone numbers (redacted).

Case background

In November 2023 a DeepMind-led team (Nasr, Carlini, and colleagues) published the divergence attack: asking ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo) to repeat a single word forever eventually makes the generation "diverge" from chat behavior, after which the model emits raw text that includes verbatim pretraining data. The attack needed no jailbreak in the usual sense — just the repetition instruction — and was disclosed to OpenAI (August 2023) before the public write-up.

Case background · Nasr et al. 2023 (ChatGPT)

Attacker access. Black-box API access to the aligned production model (gpt-3.5-turbo), plus an auxiliary corpus for verification. No weights, no training data, no logits needed for the attack itself. The same paper also attacks open models (Pythia, GPT-Neo) and semi-closed ones (LLaMA, Falcon) with plain generation.

Target model and data. ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo) whose training data is undisclosed; open models with known corpora (the Pile) serve as calibration.

Member definition and duplicate control. Membership is inferred, not confirmed: an emitted 50-token span counts as training data if it appears verbatim in a roughly 10 TB index of public web text (suffix-array lookup). Anything not in the index is missed, and a match shows the text exists on the web, not that OpenAI trained on that copy. Duplication of the matched strings on the web is not controlled; heavily duplicated text is the likeliest to be matched.

Metric and operating point. Rate of 50-token verbatim matches per generated token, compared with the default chat behavior and with base models; unique extracted strings versus query cost.

Result. Under the attack, training data emitted at about 150× the model's default rate (Fig. 1); over 10,000 unique verbatim strings for about USD 200 of queries, including personal contact information; the unique-count curve (Fig. 9) had not saturated, with an extrapolation past one million.

Limitation. Membership inferred from a public-web match; no control group of matched non-member text is reported; the index bounds what can be detected; rates depend on the auxiliary corpus and on the model version at the time, which has since changed.

The Loop Breaks

The model repeats the word for a while, then diverges, dumping raw memorized text including real names, emails, and phone numbers.

Key takeaway: When the loop breaks, memorized training data spills out.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Divergence. The paper's term for the moment the generation escapes the aligned-chat distribution: after long single-token repetition, the model stops behaving like a chat assistant and produces text resembling raw base-LM sampling — and a measurable fraction of that text is verbatim training data. Verification: emitted text counts as training data only if it appears verbatim, as a long match, in a large auxiliary index of web text (roughly 10 TB) queried efficiently with suffix arrays — so the reported leaks are confirmed copies, not coincidences.

Scale of the Leak

The attack was cheap and broad: over ten thousand unique memorized strings for about two hundred dollars of queries, with training data emitted at 150 times the normal rate — including real personal contact information — from a deployed, aligned chat model.

Key takeaway: Alignment did not prevent large-scale extraction.

Slide figure: Nasr et al. 2023, Fig. 1: rate of emitting training data for Pythia, LLaMA, InstructGPT, ChatGPT by default (about 50× less than the open models) and ChatGPT under the attack (about 150× more).

Case background

The paper's own numbers: under the divergence attack the model emits training data at a rate 150× higher than when behaving properly, and with about USD 200 of queries the authors extracted over 10,000 unique verbatim-memorized training examples, including personally identifiable information — with the extrapolation that a better-funded adversary could recover far more. This is the extractable-memorization measurement (Definition 1, earlier) performed on a production, aligned model.

Patched, Not Solved

After disclosure, the specific prompt was blocked. But blocking one prompt does not remove the memorized data, which still lives in the weights.

Key takeaway: Patching a prompt is not the same as deleting the data.

Slide figure: Nasr et al. 2023, Fig. 9 (left): unique extracted 50-grams versus total extracted 50-grams for ChatGPT-3.5; the measured curve keeps climbing and the extrapolation passes one million unique memorized sequences.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

System-level vs model-level defense. Refusing the "repeat forever" instruction is a system-level patch: it removes one path to the memorized content while the content itself — the high-probability continuation stored in $\theta$ — is untouched, and remains reachable by any other prompt that triggers divergence. Actually removing a memorized record from the weights is the (much harder) machine unlearning problem, one of the open problems at the end of this lecture.

03 — How Much, and Why It Grows

Third section. We treat memorization as a measurable quantity and look at the three drivers that make it grow.

Key takeaway: Memorization scales in predictable ways.

A Measurable Quantity

Memorization is not all-or-nothing. We count how many training strings are extractable and track that fraction as conditions change. It moves predictably.

Key takeaway: We can measure the fraction of data a model memorizes.

Slide figure: Carlini et al. 2023, Fig. 2(a,b): fraction of randomly sampled training sequences that are extractable, rising with GPT-Neo model size (a) and with sequence length for every model size (b).

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Memorization fraction (Carlini et al., ICLR 2023; deck technical supplement). Fix a set $D$ of prefix–continuation pairs $(p, s)$ sampled from the training data, with fixed prefix and continuation lengths. The measured quantity is the fraction that reproduce exactly under greedy decoding:

$$\mathrm{Mem}(f) \;=\; \frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{(p,s)\in D} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, f_{\text{greedy}}(p) = s \,\right].$$

This turns the yes/no notion of $k$-extractability into a rate that can be plotted against model size, duplication, and context length — the next three slides.

References

  • Carlini et al., "Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models," ICLR 2023 (arXiv 2202.07646) — the measurement methodology behind all of Section 03.

Three Drivers

Three factors push memorization up: bigger models store more, duplicated data sticks harder, and longer prompting context unlocks more of what is stored.

Key takeaway: Model size, duplication, and context length all increase measured memorization.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

The three measured variables: $N$ = number of model parameters, $r$ = number of times the target string is duplicated in the training data, $k$ = number of context tokens in the prompt. Carlini et al. (ICLR 2023) report that $\mathrm{Mem}$ grows log-linearly in each, holding the others fixed — three separate log-linear relationships, combined into one fitted law on "A Predictable Curve."

Bigger Means More

Holding the data fixed and growing the model, the amount memorized climbs steadily. Capacity that is not needed for generalization gets spent on storing examples.

Key takeaway: Larger models memorize more of the same data.

Slide figure: Carlini et al. 2023, Fig. 1(a): fraction extractable versus GPT-Neo model size, 120M to 6B parameters on a log axis, a near-straight rising line far above the GPT-2 baseline (trained on different data).

Technical depth — optional · theorem & proof

The measured law (empirical, Carlini et al. ICLR 2023, on models trained on the Pile, up to GPT-J 6B): a tenfold increase in model size corresponds to an increase in memorization of about 19 percentage points, with the data and evaluation held fixed. This is an empirical regression, not a theorem — but it is remarkably stable across the studied range, which is what makes it usable for forecasting ("The Uncomfortable Trend"). The capacity reading of the slide is quantified directly by the Morris et al. result at the end of the lecture (≈3.6 bits/parameter).

Case background · Carlini, Ippolito, Jagielski, Lee, Tramèr, Zhang 2023

Attacker access. Auditor with the training data: discoverable regime. Greedy decoding from the true prefix.

Target model and data. GPT-Neo family (125M to 6B, GPT-J) trained on the Pile, whose contents are public; GPT-2 as a different-data baseline.

Member definition and duplicate control. Members are sequences drawn from the Pile training set; duplication is controlled explicitly by sampling sequences stratified by their repetition count in the corpus (panel b of Fig. 1), so the size and context effects are read at fixed duplication.

Metric and operating point. $k$-extractable fraction: the share of sampled training sequences whose 50-token suffix is reproduced exactly under greedy decoding from a $k$-token true prefix (panel c varies $k$).

Result. Three log-linear relationships: memorization rises with model size (about 19 percentage points per 10×), with duplication count, and with prefix length. GPT-J 6B discoverably memorizes at least 1% of the Pile under this test.

Limitation. Discoverable, not extractable: numbers say what is stored and reachable from its own context, not what an attacker without the Pile recovers. Exact match only, so near-verbatim reproductions are not counted. The abstract itself notes the picture is more complicated across model families.

What the figure shows. Fig. 1 (a): fraction extractable versus model size on a log axis; (b) versus repetitions; (c) versus prompt length.

Duplication Is the Big One

A string that appears many times in training is far more likely to leak — and superlinearly so: Kandpal and colleagues found a sequence seen ten times is generated roughly a thousand times more often than one seen once.

Key takeaway: Duplication is the strongest single driver, and its effect is superlinear.

Slide figure: Kandpal, Wallace, and Raffel 2022, Fig. 1: expected number of times a training sequence is generated versus how many times it is duplicated in training, log-log, for four language models; the curves are superlinear and approach the dashed perfect-memorization line.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Superlinearity, precisely. Let $g(d)$ be the expected rate at which a model regenerates (verbatim) a training sequence that occurs $d$ times in the training data. Kandpal, Wallace & Raffel (ICML 2022) measure $g$ and find it grows much faster than the proportional baseline $g(d) \approx d \cdot g(1)$: a sequence present 10 times is generated on average about 1,000 times more often than a sequence present once — a factor of ~1,000 where linearity would predict 10. They also show the flip side: existing extraction and membership-inference attacks owe much of their success to duplicated sequences, and their measured effectiveness drops sharply on deduplicated models.

Case background · Kandpal, Wallace, Raffel 2022

Attacker access. Auditor with the training corpus: the study generates unprompted samples and counts matches against the training data.

Target model and data. Language models the authors trained on web-scraped corpora with and without deduplication (Fig. 3a is the OpenWebText duplicate histogram); the abstract does not name the model sizes.

Member definition and duplicate control. A member is a training sequence; each is tagged with its exact duplicate count in the corpus, and generation rates are reported as a function of that count. Deduplication is the intervention.

Metric and operating point. Expected number of times a training sequence is regenerated verbatim in a fixed sample of unprompted generations, plotted against duplicate count on log–log axes; plus accuracy of existing memorization detectors on duplicated versus non-duplicated members.

Result. Superlinear: a sequence present 10 times is generated about 1,000 times more often than one present once. Existing detection methods have near-chance accuracy on non-duplicated training sequences, and deduplicated models are considerably more resistant to these attacks.

Limitation. Verbatim regeneration only; the models are the authors' own rather than frontier scale; the "near-chance on singletons" result is about 2022 detectors, and Carlini et al. 2021 had already confirmed some $k = 1$ leaks, so singletons are hard to detect, not immune.

What the figure shows. Fig. 1: expected generations versus duplication count for four models, curving upward toward the perfect-memorization line.

Follow-up status (as of 2026-09). Together with Lee et al. 2022 this is why deduplication is the default first defense; the "Dedup Is Not Enough" slide records the residual.

The Long Tail

A Predictable Curve

Memorization scales log-linearly with these drivers: each tenfold increase in size, duplication, or context adds a steady amount. Panel (c) is worth a pause: "prompt length" is the amount of context the model is given. A 50-token prefix could be the start of many passages, so the memorized continuation competes with plausible alternatives; a 500-token prefix identifies one specific passage in the training data, and the model's recall of the rest becomes nearly deterministic. More context means less ambiguity about which record to recite, not a change in what the model stored. Carlini and colleagues measured that GPT-J, at 6 billion parameters, memorizes at least one percent of its training set. This is a built-in property of training, not a bug to patch out.

Key takeaway: The growth follows a smooth, predictable curve — and it is already large.

Slide figure: Carlini et al. 2023, Fig. 1: fraction extractable versus (a) model size, (b) number of repetitions in the training data, (c) prompt length, each a near-straight line on a log axis.

Technical depth — optional · theorem & proof

The fitted scaling law (empirical; deck technical supplement, from Carlini et al. ICLR 2023):

$$\mathrm{Mem} \;\approx\; a\,\log N \;+\; b\,\log r \;+\; c\,\log k \;+\; d, \qquad a, b, c \gt 0,$$

with $N$ = parameters, $r$ = duplication count, $k$ = context tokens. Log-linear means each $10\times$ in any driver adds a constant increment ($a\log 10$, $b\log 10$, or $c\log 10$ respectively) — a steady slope on a log axis, not a bump to patch out. The headline calibration point: GPT-J (6B) memorizes at least 1% of its training dataset, the Pile. Independently, Nasr et al. note prior work finding that many LLMs discoverably memorize roughly 1% of training data at ~50 tokens of context — the two measurements agree.

A Quick Picture

Optional depth — skipped on the 90-minute core path (see the list under Contents); the script below is for the full session or for reading.

A diffusion model turns pure noise into an image through a step-by-step denoising process. Ideally each output is new, but sometimes the result is a memorized training image.

Key takeaway: Diffusion builds images from noise, and can reconstruct stored ones.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Diffusion in two equations (standard DDPM setup; recap: course notes). The forward process gradually noises a training image $x_0$: at step $t$,

$$q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\; (1-\bar\alpha_t) I\right),$$

with a schedule $\bar\alpha_t \downarrow 0$, so $x_T$ is (nearly) pure Gaussian noise. Training fits a denoiser $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ to predict the added noise — equivalently, to estimate the score of the noised data distribution. Generation starts from $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and integrates the learned reverse (denoising) chain back to an image. Memorization in this picture: for some prompts/noise, the learned reverse chain does not sample fresh content but reconverges to a specific stored training $x_0$ — which is exactly what the extraction definitions on the next slide test for.

Stable Diffusion Copies

Carlini and colleagues showed Stable Diffusion regenerating near-exact training photos of a real, identifiable person, prompted by her name from the training caption. Across the study they recovered 94 training images from 175 million generations. Use the callouts: what happened, training captions as prompts regenerated near-identical photos; how it was verified, 350,000 of the most duplicated captions, 500 generations each, clusters of near-identical outputs flagged and then matched against the training set, 94 at the ℓ₂ ≤ 0.15 threshold; why it matters, rare per query but real in aggregate, mostly for heavily duplicated images, and a face is a privacy harm where a logo would be a copyright one.

Key takeaway: A caption can summon a near-exact training photo — rare per query, real in aggregate, and verified against the training set.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

From Carlini et al. (2023), adapting the LLM definitions to images:

Definition 1 (($\ell, \delta$)-Diffusion Extraction). An example $x$ is extractable from a diffusion model $f_\theta$ if there exists an efficient algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ (that does not receive $x$ as input) such that $\hat{x} = \mathcal{A}(f_\theta)$ satisfies $\ell(x, \hat{x}) \le \delta$. Here $\ell$ is a distance and $\delta$ the identity threshold; the paper uses the normalized Euclidean distance $\ell_2(a,b) = \sqrt{\sum_i (a_i - b_i)^2 / d}$ ($d$ = dimension).

Definition 2 (($k, \ell, \delta$)-Eidetic Memorization). $x$ is $(k,\ell,\delta)$-eidetic memorized if $x$ is extractable and at most $k$ training examples $\hat{x} \in X$ satisfy $\ell(x, \hat{x}) \le \delta$ — the image analogue of $k$-eidetic memorization from Section 02.

The headline numbers: out of 175 million generated images, 94 images were $(\ell_2, 0.15)$-extracted from Stable Diffusion — direct near-copies of training images. The best-known example, a photograph of evangelist Ann Graham Lotz, is regenerated from her name in the caption with $\ell_2$ distance $0.031$ from the training image: nearly identical.

Case background · Carlini et al. 2023 (diffusion)

Attacker access. Black-box generation from Stable Diffusion 1.4 with chosen prompts; the prompts were taken from training captions, so the attacker is assumed to know the caption text (LAION is public). Verification used the training images.

Target model and data. Stable Diffusion (LAION-2B-derived training set, public) and Google's Imagen (private training set, verified by the authors with access).

Member definition and duplicate control. A training image is extracted if some generation lies within $\ell_2 \le 0.15$ of it; $(k,\ell,\delta)$-eidetic memorization counts near-duplicates of the image in training. Duplication was used as a search heuristic (the 350,000 most duplicated captions were prompted), so the 94 are biased toward duplicated images by design; the paper reports that most extracted images were duplicated many times.

Metric and operating point. 350,000 prompts × 500 generations = 175 million images; a generation is flagged if its prompt's 500 outputs contain a clique of at least 10 near-identical images; flagged images are then matched to training under $\ell_2 \le 0.15$.

Result. 94 confirmed near-copies from Stable Diffusion; the Lotz photograph at $\ell_2 = 0.031$; a further set of extractions from Imagen ("over a thousand" examples across both models per the abstract).

Limitation. Rates are per-prompt-specific and the search was aimed at duplicated content, so 94/175M is not an unbiased rate; the clique heuristic misses memorized images that are not regenerated consistently; ℓ₂ at 0.15 is a strict near-pixel criterion, so semantic copies (Somepalli et al.) are not counted.

What the figure shows. Fig. 1: training image (caption naming the person) beside the generated image from the caption prompt.

Follow-up status (as of 2026-09). Later Stable Diffusion releases deduplicated training data more aggressively (reported by the developers; not independently measured here). The clique-detection idea is reused in later diffusion memorization work.

Duplication Drives It Again

Just as with text, duplicated images are the ones that leak: the histogram from Carlini's paper shows most successfully extracted images appeared one hundred or more times in the training set.

Key takeaway: Duplication drives image memorization too.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

The attack pipeline (Carlini et al. 2023). (1) Bias the search toward likely-memorized content: select the 350,000 most-duplicated training examples and use their captions as prompts. (2) Generate 500 images per prompt (175 million total). (3) Detect memorization without training-set access via density: build a graph on each prompt's 500 generations with an edge whenever two generations are near-identical under (a modified) $\ell_2$; if the largest clique has size at least 10, predict that clique's image is memorized. The logic: fresh samples from a diverse distribution rarely coincide, so many near-identical independent generations indicate the model reconverging to one stored image. Predicted images were then verified against the actual training set.

Copy or Create?

Somepalli and colleagues searched generated images against the training set and found about 1.9 percent were near-duplicates of a training image — content copied even when the pixels differ.

Key takeaway: A real share of "new" images are actually copies.

Slide figure: Somepalli et al. 2023, Fig. 5: histograms of top-1 similarity between generations and training data for diffusion models trained on 300, 3000, and all points; the small-data model has a spike near similarity 1.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

SSCD similarity. Somepalli et al. (CVPR 2023) score copying with SSCD, a self-supervised copy-detection embedding: images are mapped to feature vectors trained so that copies (including crops, recolorings, and re-compositions) land close together, and similarity is the inner product of normalized features. Unlike pixel $\ell_2$, this catches content replication when pixels differ — the "same object, new background" cases.

The finding: generating from Stable Diffusion and searching against a 12M-image subset of its training data (LAION Aesthetics v2 6+), generations with SSCD similarity $\ge 0.5$ to some training image — a threshold the authors validate as genuine content copying — account for about 1.88% of random generations. Note the complementary roles: Carlini et al. count near-pixel-exact copies (rare, 94 confirmed); Somepalli et al. count semantic content copies (about 1 in 50).

Case background · Somepalli, Singla, Goldblum, Geiping, Goldstein 2023

Attacker access. Auditor: generations from the model plus a retrieval index over (a subset of) the training images.

Target model and data. Diffusion models trained by the authors on Oxford Flowers, Celeb-A, ImageNet (small to large training sets, Fig. 5), and Stable Diffusion with a 12M-image LAION Aesthetics subset as the search index.

Member definition and duplicate control. A generation is a replication if its SSCD similarity to some indexed training image exceeds 0.5; the index covers only part of the training set, so unmatched generations may still be copies. Duplication is discussed as a driver but not controlled in the headline rate.

Metric and operating point. Top-1 SSCD similarity between each generation and the index; the 0.5 threshold is validated by manual inspection.

Result. About 1.88% of random Stable Diffusion generations exceed the threshold; small-training-set models show a spike at similarity 1 (Fig. 5).

Limitation. Partial index (12M of roughly 2B images) makes 1.88% a floor for that index and an unknown for the full set; SSCD catches content copies but its threshold is a judgment call; no matched non-member baseline (how often two unrelated images score above 0.5 under SSCD).

What the figure shows. Fig. 5: similarity histograms for models trained on 300, 3,000, and all points; the small-data model's spike near 1 is near-total memorization.

Follow-up status (as of 2026-09). SSCD-style retrieval is the common tool in later diffusion-copying audits; the same authors' follow-up work on mitigations (caption randomization) is outside this lecture.

A generated portrait can secretly be a real person's photo. That is a privacy and consent problem, not just an academic curiosity.

NYT v. OpenAI

Much training data is copyrighted — news, books, code, art — and in December 2023 The New York Times sued Microsoft and OpenAI. Take the three callouts in order and keep them in separate sentences. Copy or Transform?

Developers argue training is transformative learning, like a person reading; rights holders argue verbatim output is plain copying. Memorization evidence is the technical input to that argument; fair use is the legal judgment. Bartz v. Anthropic gives three separate answers, and the callouts keep them apart. Fair use: in June 2025 Judge Alsup held that training on lawfully bought books was fair use, a ruling about the use of inputs in which model outputs were not at issue. Piracy: keeping about seven million books downloaded from pirate libraries was not fair use, and the later training did not excuse it; that question was headed to trial. Settlement: 1.5 billion dollars, about 3,000 per work for roughly 480,000 works, preliminary approval in September 2025 and final approval in July 2026. The approval resolves the piracy claims; it is not a merits judgment on the whole case and not an admission of liability. Less memorization is now a legal goal as well as an engineering one, but say precisely which part of the case that follows from.

Key takeaway: Fair-use ruling on inputs, piracy finding, and settlement approval are three different things; none of them is a court finding about model outputs.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Fair use, 17 U.S.C. § 107. US law makes fair use a case-by-case balancing test; the statutory factors are (verbatim from the statute):

  1. the purpose and character of the use, including whether such use is of a commercial nature or is for nonprofit educational purposes;
  2. the nature of the copyrighted work;
  3. the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole; and
  4. the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.

"Transformative" use — the developers' argument — lives in factor 1. Memorization cuts against factors 3 and 4 at once: verbatim regurgitation is the whole "amount and substantiality" of the work, delivered to a potential customer. That is why measured memorization rates (Sections 03 and 06) are now litigation-relevant quantities.

Case background

Complaint allegation (Aug 2024). Bartz v. Anthropic PBC, N.D. Cal. No. 3:24-cv-05417: three authors, on behalf of a class, alleged that Anthropic trained Claude on their books, including copies downloaded from pirate libraries (Books3, LibGen, PiLiMi), and retained them in a central library. The complaint's theory concerned acquisition and training inputs; it did not turn on regurgitated outputs, and no extraction evidence of the kind in this lecture was central to it.

Judicial finding (June 23, 2025). Judge William Alsup, on summary judgment: (i) training LLMs on lawfully acquired books was "exceedingly transformative" and fair use; (ii) digitizing purchased print books for a searchable library was fair use; (iii) downloading and keeping about seven million pirated books was not fair use, and the later training use did not excuse the acquisition. The piracy claims were set for a trial on damages. The order does not address whether Claude's outputs reproduce the books; outputs were not at issue.

Settlement (status as of 2026-09). USD 1.5 billion, about USD 3,000 per work for roughly 480,000 works, resolving the piracy-related class claims; preliminary approval September 2025, final approval July 20, 2026 (Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín, to whom the case had been reassigned). A settlement approval is a judgment that the deal is fair to the class; it is not a merits ruling on the remaining claims and contains no admission of liability. The fair-use holding on lawfully acquired books stands as a district-court ruling, not appellate precedent. Reported: further suits against Anthropic by other rights holders (e.g., music publishers, Aug 2026) continue separately.

The Same Output, Different Questions

Take one generation, a signature block with a real name and phone number, or three paragraphs of a novel with one word changed, and ask four people what it means. The data subject asks whether their personal information is in there and can come out; the harm is exposure, regardless of who wrote the text. The copyright holder asks whether protected expression was reproduced and whether the output proves copying; a fact or a style is not enough. The ML team asks how often, from which prompts, and whether the record was duplicated in training; their vocabulary is extraction rate, control baseline, mitigation. The product owner asks what reaches the user: guardrails, filters, liability, reputation, and remembers that a filter changes the output, not the weights. One measurement feeds four judgments. The discipline is to keep the technical result and the legal or ethical conclusion in separate sentences.

Key takeaway: One extraction result answers four different questions; report the measurement once and let each stakeholder draw their own conclusion from it.

06 — Mitigations & Frontier

Final section. We survey the defenses, why none is complete, and where the research frontier sits.

Key takeaway: Every defense is partial.

The Toolbox

Three families of defense: deduplicate the data, filter the output for verbatim spans, and add formal privacy during training. Each helps; none fully solves the problem.

Key takeaway: Defenses are complementary, not complete.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

The three families sit at three intervention points in the pipeline, and their guarantees differ in kind:

  • Data-level (deduplication): shrink the $\log r$ term of the scaling law before training. Empirical benefit, no formal guarantee.
  • Inference-level (output filtering): block verbatim spans at generation time. System-level control; the weights still memorize.
  • Training-level (differential privacy): bound every record's influence on the trained model. The only provable guarantee of the three, and the most expensive.

Deduplication

Removing repeats before training attacks the strongest driver. Lee and colleagues showed it also makes models better, not just safer, making it the cheapest win available.

Key takeaway: Deduplication improves quality and reduces leakage at once.

Slide figure: Lee et al. 2022, Fig. 3 (left) and Fig. 2 (right): fraction of LM continuations matching the true continuation falls from about 0.4 to under 0.1 on duplicated training prompts after NearDup or ExactSubstr deduplication, while validation perplexity on C4, LM1B, and Wiki40B is equal or better.

Case background

Lee et al. (ACL 2022) built exact-substring and near-duplicate deduplication tools for web-scale corpora and measured the effects: models trained on standard (undeduplicated) data emit copied training text in over 1% of unprompted output; after deduplication, models emit memorized text about 10× less frequently, reach the same or better accuracy, and — because the data is smaller — train in fewer steps. A defense that simultaneously improves the model is why this is deployed first everywhere.

Case background · Lee et al. 2022

Attacker access. Auditor with the training corpus: unprompted generations are matched back against the training data.

Target model and data. Transformer language models the authors trained on C4 and related corpora, in three conditions: original, NearDup-deduplicated (MinHash near-duplicate documents), ExactSubstr-deduplicated (repeated 50-token substrings removed with a suffix array).

Member definition and duplicate control. A memorized emission is a generated span found verbatim in the training set; duplication is the manipulated variable through the two deduplication tools.

Metric and operating point. Fraction of unprompted output copied verbatim from training; fraction of continuations matching the true continuation on prompts drawn from duplicated versus unique training text (Fig. 3); validation perplexity on C4, LM1B, Wiki40B (Fig. 2).

Result. Over 1% of unprompted output of models trained on undeduplicated data is verbatim training text; deduplicated models emit memorized text about ten times less often, with equal or better perplexity and fewer training steps.

Limitation. Exact-substring and near-document matching miss lightly edited copies; the models are research scale; a 10× reduction is not zero, and the reduction is measured on the duplicated-prompt regime where dedup acts most strongly.

What the figure shows. Left: matching-continuation fraction falls from about 0.4 to under 0.1 after either dedup method; right: validation perplexity is unchanged or better.

Follow-up status (as of 2026-09). Deduplication is standard in corpus construction; Kandpal et al. 2022 supply the privacy-side measurement, and Cooper et al. 2026 note that heavily duplicated boilerplate is also what a non-member control must screen out.

Dedup Is Not Enough

Even unique, seen-once strings can be memorized in large models, and near-duplicates slip past exact matching. Deduplication lowers the risk but never to zero.

Key takeaway: Deduplication is a strong first line, not a wall.

Slide figure: Schematic: three lines that differ by a word ("Ms. Alice", "tonight") survive exact-match deduplication as three "unique" strings that all carry the same phone number.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Two precise reasons the residual is nonzero. (1) Singletons leak: the GPT-2 study confirmed $k{=}1$ eidetic memorization — strings from a single training document — and Feldman's theorem says fitting one-off examples is not an accident but a consequence of optimizing accuracy on long-tailed data; dedup by definition cannot remove content that appears once. (2) Near-duplicates evade matching: exact-substring dedup misses reformatted, re-quoted, or lightly edited copies, which still act as duplicates for the $\log r$ driver. So dedup moves the curve down; the scaling law's other terms ($\log N$, $\log k$) keep growing.

Output Filtering

Deployed code and chat systems check generations for long verbatim matches and block text that copies a source too closely. It catches obvious copying but misses paraphrase and small leaks.

Key takeaway: Filtering stops blatant copying, not subtle leaks.

Slide figure: Schematic: model output passes through an $n$-gram match against the training corpus; a long verbatim span is blocked, anything else passes, and paraphrase slips through.

Case background

The deployed example is GitHub Copilot: it offers a duplication-detection setting whose "Block" mode suppresses suggestions that match publicly available code on GitHub, and (when matches are allowed) a code-referencing feature that shows the matching repositories and their licenses. The structural limits of any such filter: it tests verbatim similarity at generation time, so paraphrased regurgitation, short leaked spans below the match threshold, and content absent from the reference index all pass — and the memorized content remains in the weights (same point as "Patched, Not Solved").

Differential Privacy

Differential privacy trains so that any single example barely moves the model. If no one record changes the output much, no one record can be memorized. The catch is that this guarantee is paid for in accuracy.

Key takeaway: Differential privacy provably bounds per-example memorization.

Slide figure: VaultGemma Team 2025, Fig. 1: exact and approximate memorization rates; the DP-trained Gemma 2 1B shows none, while the non-private Gemma models memorize between 0.01% and about 1% of sampled sequences.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

($\varepsilon,\delta$)-Differential Privacy (Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, Smith 2006; $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ relaxation Dwork et al. 2006). A randomized algorithm $M$ is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP if for all pairs of datasets $D, D'$ differing in one record (here: add/remove one training example) and all measurable output sets $S$:

$$\Pr[M(D) \in S] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\, \Pr[M(D') \in S] \;+\; \delta.$$

Here $M$ is the whole training procedure and its output is the trained model (or anything computed from it).

Technical depth — optional · theorem & proof

DP bounds counterfactual memorization (derivation: course notes). Let $A$ be an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP training algorithm and let $E$ be any behavioral event about the trained model — e.g. "$f$ reproduces record $z$ verbatim under some fixed prompt," or any $\{f : M(f,z) \ge c\}$. Since $D$ and $D \setminus \{z\}$ are adjacent, the DP inequality gives directly

$$\Pr[A(D) \in E] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\, \Pr[A(D\setminus\{z\}) \in E] + \delta,$$

so the counterfactual gap obeys

$$\Pr[A(D) \in E] - \Pr[A(D\setminus\{z\}) \in E] \;\le\; (e^{\varepsilon} - 1)\Pr[A(D\setminus\{z\}) \in E] + \delta \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon} - 1 + \delta.$$

Reading: whatever the model does having seen $z$, it would have done almost as probably without ever seeing $z$ — with small $\varepsilon$, no single record can be the reason its contents come out. This also caps canary exposure in distribution: the null-tail argument from Section 01 degrades only by the same $e^\varepsilon$ factor. Note what is not covered: content duplicated across many records (the group-privacy cost grows as $e^{k\varepsilon}$ for $k$ copies) — which is why dedup and DP are complements, not substitutes.

The Privacy Tax

Optional depth — skipped on the 90-minute core path (see the list under Contents); the script below is for the full session or for reading.

Differential privacy is the strongest defense and the costliest: strong settings hurt accuracy, training is expensive at scale, and it is rarely used for the largest foundation models yet.

Key takeaway: The strongest guarantee comes at the highest cost.

Slide figure: Schematic: as the per-step noise grows from no DP to strong DP, the privacy guarantee rises while accuracy falls.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

DP-SGD (Abadi et al., CCS 2016) is how the definition is implemented for deep learning. Each step: compute per-example gradients $g_i$, clip each to norm at most $C$, $\bar{g}_i = g_i \cdot \min(1, C / \lVert g_i \rVert_2)$, then average and add Gaussian noise:

$$\tilde{g} \;=\; \frac{1}{B}\left(\sum_{i} \bar{g}_i + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I)\right).$$

Clipping caps any one example's influence on the update; the noise hides the remainder; the moments accountant tracks the accumulated $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ across all training steps. The tax is visible in the mechanics: per-example gradients cost memory and compute, clipping biases the updates, and the noise scale needed for small $\varepsilon$ grows with training length — which is why full-scale DP pretraining of frontier LLMs remains rare.

References

  • Abadi, Chu, Goodfellow, McMahan, Mironov, Talwar, Zhang, "Deep Learning with Differential Privacy," CCS 2016 (arXiv 1607.00133) — DP-SGD and the moments accountant.

No Silver Bullet

Every defense trades cost or utility for less memorization. In practice, teams stack several of them and accept some residual risk.

Key takeaway: Mitigation is a balance, not an on-off switch.

Frontier: Whole Books Come Back

Extraction has scaled from strings to entire books. Cooper and colleagues ran a probabilistic extraction attack over 200 books and 14 open-weight models. Average rates on random book text are low and differ by model, but per book they vary enormously: Llama 3.1 70B memorizes nearly the entire first Harry Potter book.

Key takeaway: Open-weight frontier models can carry, and emit, whole copyrighted books.

Slide figure: Cooper et al. 2025, Fig. 3: extraction rate on random Books3 samples for nine open-weight models, greedy versus probabilistic; Llama 3.1 70B is highest, at about 0.6%.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Probabilistic extraction (Hayes et al., NAACL 2025). Deterministic greedy decoding understates leakage for deployed models, which sample. Let $p_{\text{ext}}(s \mid p)$ be the probability that one sampled generation from prompt $p$ yields the target $s$. Then $s$ is $(n, q)$-discoverably extractable if an adversary making $n$ sampled attempts succeeds at least once with probability at least $q$:

$$1 - \bigl(1 - p_{\text{ext}}(s \mid p)\bigr)^{n} \;\ge\; q.$$

Even tiny per-query probabilities become near-certain extraction at scale — the right lens for querying a public API millions of times.

Case background

Open-weight models — Cooper et al. (2025) applied this probabilistic lens across 200 books and 14 open-weight LLMs (thousands of book–model experiments), sliding a window of 100-token sequences (50-token prefix, 50-token suffix) through each book. Headline: Llama 3.1 70B memorizes Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone nearly in its entirety — extraction coverage ≈96.3% — with nearly half the book extractable even demanding per-sequence success probability ≥50%; other books recur too (e.g. The Alchemist, coverage ≈49.1%), while most books show far less.

Case background · Cooper et al. 2025 (books) and Hayes et al. 2025

Attacker access. Open weights: exact next-token probabilities are available, so per-sequence extraction probabilities can be computed rather than estimated by sampling. Discoverable regime: the auditors hold the books and prompt with true prefixes.

Target model and data. 14 open-weight models (Pythia, Llama 1/2/3.1, Gemma, Phi, Qwen and others) and 200 books from Books3, a corpus known to be in several of these models' training sets.

Member definition and duplicate control. A book is treated as a likely member because Books3 is a documented training source for some models (Llama 1, Pythia) and inferred for others; membership is therefore likely training match, not confirmed per model. Duplication of book text across the web (quotations, fan sites) is not controlled in this paper; the 2026 follow-up adds the control.

Metric and operating point. Probabilistic extraction: for each 50-token prefix, the probability under the model's own decoding scheme that a sample reproduces the 50-token suffix; "coverage" counts suffixes whose probability clears a threshold; the slide's Fig. 3 reports greedy and probabilistic rates on random Books3 samples.

Result. Average rates on random book text are low (Llama 3.1 70B highest at about 0.6%); per book, coverage varies enormously: Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone ≈96.3% for Llama 3.1 70B, The Alchemist ≈49.1%, most books far lower.

Limitation. Discoverable, exact-match, open weights only; coverage is about stored-and-reachable text, not blind attacker recovery; heavily quoted books are exactly where predictable text inflates a test without a non-member control.

Frontier: Chatbots Recite Books Too

The follow-up moved from open weights to deployed chatbots. Ahmed and colleagues probed Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Grok 3 with a two-phase attack: probe for memorized openings, then iteratively ask for continuations. Frontier: Near-Verbatim Counts Too

Every rate so far counted exact reproductions. Cooper and colleagues point out that a continuation differing from the training text by a handful of tokens is just as much a copy for privacy or copyright, but the set of such near-copies is combinatorially large, so estimating its total probability by sampling needs on the order of a hundred thousand samples per sequence. Their fix is a beam search constrained to the model's own top-$k$ decoding scheme, which tracks exact probabilities of the sequences it visits and therefore returns a deterministic lower bound at roughly the cost of twenty samples. On OLMo 2 the near-verbatim rate is markedly higher than the verbatim rate, and the held-out control, Wikipedia written after the training cutoff, stays near zero.

Key takeaway: Counting only exact matches understates memorization; near-verbatim extraction can be bounded cheaply and is substantially larger.

Slide figure: Cooper et al. 2026, Fig. 3: extraction rate against allowed Levenshtein distance $\varepsilon \in \{0,\dots,5\}$ for OLMo 2 7B, 13B, 32B, greedy and probabilistic (k-CBS), training data as solid and held-out as dashed lines; OLMo 2 32B rises from about 1.4% at $\varepsilon = 0$ to about 2.6% at $\varepsilon = 5$.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Near-verbatim extraction probability. For prefix $z^{(\mathrm{pre})}$, target suffix $z^{(\mathrm{suf})}$ of length $L$, and edit budget $\varepsilon$, define $B_\varepsilon = \{\hat z : \mathrm{Lev}(\hat z, z^{(\mathrm{suf})}) \le \varepsilon\}$ and $$p_{\varepsilon}(z) = \sum_{\hat z \in B_\varepsilon} \Pr_\phi\bigl[\hat z \mid z^{(\mathrm{pre})}\bigr],$$ the probability under decoding scheme $\phi$ (here top-$k$ = 40 at temperature 1) that a single sample lands in the $\varepsilon$-ball. $\varepsilon = 0$ recovers the verbatim probabilistic extraction of Hayes et al. (NAACL 2025), which is a single product of token probabilities; for $\varepsilon \ge 1$ the sum has $O(L^{\varepsilon}|V|^{\varepsilon})$ terms.

k-CBS. Beam search of width $B$ in which each expansion is restricted to the tokens the decoding scheme could actually emit, so every path's probability is exact under $\phi$; a streaming Wagner–Fischer table prunes prefixes whose row-minimum edit distance already exceeds $\varepsilon$. Summing the probabilities of the completed sequences inside $B_\varepsilon$ gives a lower bound on $p_\varepsilon(z)$, since every counted sequence is a genuine member of the ball and none is double-counted. With $B = 20$ the bound captured about 89% of a $10^4$-sample Monte Carlo estimate on a Great Gatsby example (their Fig. 2). Plain-language versions of $B_\varepsilon$ and k-CBS are on tech slides 11 and 12.

Frontier: Extraction Needs a Control Group

The second correction runs the other way. A model will greedily complete text it never trained on when the text is predictable: boilerplate, lists, famous quotations. Cooper and colleagues therefore insist on a matched comparison: score training members and comparable non-members, here Wikipedia articles written after the model's cutoff, under the same model, decoding rule, and prompt selection. The non-member rate is a false-positive floor. For ten-token suffixes on OLMo 2 32B that floor is 1.8% against 7.5% on members, so about a quarter of what a naive test would call extraction is predictability; for the standard fifty-token suffix the floor is essentially zero. The same paper calibrates thresholds by conformal prediction at a chosen false-positive level, which is exactly the TPR-at-fixed-FPR discipline of the membership-inference lecture. Mention Cooper's 2026 "Whack-a-Mole" essay as the cautionary tale: a widely cited claim of 85–90% book coverage rested on five-word matches with no post-cutoff control.

Key takeaway: An extraction claim needs a matched non-member baseline; without it, predictable text is mistaken for memorized text.

Slide figure: Cooper et al. 2026, Fig. 3: greedy verbatim generation rate for OLMo 2 32B on training Wikipedia versus matched post-2024 Wikipedia, 7.54% vs 1.82% for 10-token suffixes (left) and 0.74% vs 0.02% for 50-token suffixes (right, zoomed inset), with 95% Clopper–Pearson intervals.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Matched comparison. Fix a model $\theta$, decoding policy $\phi$, prompt-selection rule $f_P$, and score $s(\cdot)$. Draw members $z \in D_{\mathrm{train}}$ and controls $z' \in D_{\mathrm{ctrl}}$ from the same distribution of text but outside training. The calibrated memorization rate at threshold $\tau$ is $\Pr_{z}[s(z) \gt \tau] - \Pr_{z'}[s(z') \gt \tau]$, and the conformal threshold $\tau_\alpha$ is the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the control scores, so that a member flagged at $\tau_\alpha$ is a false positive with probability at most $\alpha$ (finite-sample valid by exchangeability of the controls).

Two failure modes the paper names. Overstatement: suffixes so short that reproduction is unsurprising (their "parrot prompts"). Dismissal: arguing that because models complete unseen text, reproduction of training text proves nothing. The matched control answers both, and leads to the refined definition: a sequence is extractably memorized if it clears the matched threshold and is reproducible within a stated query budget. For Llama 3.1 70B on books, calibrated thresholds for 50-token suffixes fall as low as $10^{-27}$: passages that are statistically memorized yet unextractable under any realistic budget. Plain-language version: tech slides 13 and 14.

Frontier: How Much Fits?

Optional depth — skipped on the 90-minute core path (see the list under Contents); the script below is for the full session or for reading.

Morris and colleagues measured storage capacity directly: GPT-family models store about 3.6 bits per parameter, and models memorize until that capacity fills, then start generalizing. Memorization and generalization draw on one shared budget.

Key takeaway: Memorization has a measurable capacity, shared with generalization.

Slide figure: Morris et al. 2025, Fig. 1: unintended memorization in bits versus training-set size for models of 170K to 7M parameters trained on uniform random data; each curve plateaus at a capacity proportional to model size.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

Compression-based memorization (Morris et al. 2025). A model $\theta$ has memorized an input $x$ to the extent that $x$ can be described more briefly when $\theta$ is available — a Kolmogorov-style notion made practical by estimating description lengths with model likelihoods. The key move is decomposing this into unintended memorization (information $\theta$ retains about a specific dataset) and generalization (information about the underlying data-generating process); measuring the former on data with no learnable structure (random strings) isolates raw storage.

The findings: GPT-family transformers have a storage capacity of approximately 3.6 bits per parameter; models memorize until that capacity fills, after which unintended memorization plateaus and "grokking"-style generalization begins — supporting the slide's shared-budget picture, and giving a first-principles reason why the $\log N$ term of Section 03's scaling law exists.

Case background · Morris et al. 2025

Attacker access. Auditor with full control: the authors train the models and know every training example.

Target model and data. Hundreds of GPT-style transformers from 500K to 1.5B parameters, trained on uniformly random bitstrings (to isolate storage, since there is nothing to generalize) and on text.

Member definition and duplicate control. Members are the training strings; random data has no duplicates or structure by construction, which is the point of the design.

Metric and operating point. Unintended memorization in bits, estimated from the reduction in description length of the training data given the model; capacity is the plateau of total memorized bits as dataset size grows.

Result. Capacity of about 3.6 bits per parameter; models memorize until capacity fills, then unintended memorization falls as generalization ("grokking") begins; the paper also fits scaling laws relating capacity and data size to membership-inference success.

Limitation. Small models by frontier standards; bits-per-parameter is defined through a particular compression estimator; the random-data regime is deliberately unlike natural text, so the constant should be read as an order of magnitude for real corpora.

Frontier: Memorized PII

The sharpest harm is personal data coming back out. PII, meaning personally identifiable information such as names, emails, and numbers, is scattered through scraped web data and becomes extractable when duplicated or rare-but-stored.

Key takeaway: The worst leaks attach a real name and address.

Slide figure: Schematic: the same signature block sits in a forum post and in a mirror of it; both feed the model, which later emits the name, email, and phone number.

Technical depth — optional · definitions

PII (NIST SP 800-122): any information about an individual that either (1) can be used to distinguish or trace that individual's identity — name, social security number, date and place of birth, biometric records — or (2) is linked or linkable to the individual, such as medical, educational, financial, or employment information. Both prongs matter for extraction: category (2) items that look harmless in isolation become identifying when a model emits them attached to a name — exactly the pattern in the GPT-2 and ChatGPT extractions, where names arrived with phone numbers, emails, and addresses. Note also why both memorization regimes hurt here: duplicated PII (a signature block reposted across forums) leaks via the superlinear duplication law, while rare PII (one leaked database dump) is the long-tail content Feldman's theorem says accurate models fit.

Open Problems

Open questions remain: measuring memorization in closed models, cutting it without hurting accuracy, erasing specific data after training, and agreeing on a legal line for how much is too much. On erasure, flag Cooper, Lee, and colleagues' NeurIPS 2025 position paper: "unlearning" conflates removing information from the weights with suppressing it in outputs, and neither matches what privacy or copyright law would need. On the legal line, Lemley and Cooper predict courts will adopt a functional test: a model "contains a copy" only when the work is straightforward to extract, which ties the law directly to the measurements in this lecture.

Key takeaway: The core problems are still unsolved.

Case background

Each open problem connects to material above. Measuring closed models: without training-data access only extractable (not discoverable) memorization is observable — the Nasr and Ahmed attacks are the current state of the art, and gaps between the two regimes remain unquantified for production systems. Cutting memorization without hurting accuracy: Feldman's theorem sets a genuine floor on long-tailed data, so the question is how closely practical training can approach it. Erasing after training: machine unlearning — removing a specific record's influence from trained weights with a verifiable guarantee — has no scalable solution; today's "removal" is output filtering. The legal line: the NYT and Bartz cases will start drawing it, but no court has yet said how much measured memorization is too much.

Practice: Boardroom Questions

Two minutes. The frame is the same as in the earlier lectures: what happened, what property failed, who could act, what would have helped. Before you sign off on a memorization audit: did the test count exact matches only, or near-verbatim copies too? Was there a control group of matched non-member text, and what was its rate? Was training duplication checked for the strings that came out? To the vendor: is an output filter being described as removed memorization? Is the claim discoverable, where the auditor knew the record, or extractable, where the attacker did not? Is a technical extraction result being read as a legal finding, or a legal ruling being read as a technical one? If time allows, run one case through the frame; otherwise assign it.

Key takeaway: Six questions separate a defensible memorization claim from a headline.

Case background · model answers

  1. Exact or near-verbatim? Exact-match rates are a floor; OLMo 2 32B moves from about 1.4% to 2.6% when five token edits are allowed. A privacy or copyright reader treats a one-word change as a copy; the audit should say which it counted.
  2. Control group? Predictable text is completed whether or not it was trained on. The matched post-cutoff rate (1.82% at 10-token suffixes on OLMo 2 32B) is the false-positive floor; without it, "extraction" and "prediction" are indistinguishable. Cooper's critique of the five-word-match coverage claim is the cautionary example.
  3. Duplication checked? Ten copies mean roughly a thousandfold more regeneration. If the strings that came out were all heavily duplicated, deduplication is the lever; if some were singletons, expect leaks that dedup cannot remove.
  4. Filter as removal? Copilot's Block mode and the ChatGPT prompt refusal change outputs, not weights. The record is still discoverable by anyone with model access; "Patched, Not Solved".
  5. Discoverable or extractable? An audit that prompted with true prefixes measured stored-and-reachable text; it did not show that an outsider can recover it, and an attack that recovered text blind did not show how much else is stored. Ask which game was played.
  6. Technical vs legal? The NYT exhibits show reproduction, not membership or liability; the Bartz fair-use ruling is about inputs, and its settlement is not a liability finding. Keep the sentences separate in both directions.

Key Takeaways

To recap: models recite some training data verbatim or nearly so; canaries measure it and real attacks exploit it; size and duplication push it up; and no mitigation removes it for free. Deduplication is the high-leverage first defense, and it still leaves the unique sensitive record that Feldman's theorem says an accurate model may fit. Report what was measured, exact or near-verbatim, discoverable or extractable, against what baseline, and let the legal question be asked separately.
