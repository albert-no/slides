Correction 1 — the context-aware attack

The previous version of these notes and of the deck described CAMIA as thresholding $\mathrm{Var}_c[\log p_\theta(x\mid c)]$ across prefixes, with members showing low variance. That is not the attack, and the direction is backwards. Chang et al. (EMNLP 2025) measure how the per-token loss evolves as context accumulates: a cut-off loss over the first $T'$ tokens, an ordinary least-squares slope of $L_t$ against $t$, counts of low-loss tokens, and fluctuation measures (approximate entropy, Lempel–Ziv complexity) of the loss curve. Their finding is that member loss curves are smoother and more regular and fall faster; non-member curves fluctuate. The deck now states the two statistics exactly and shows their Figure 3.

Correction 2 — InfoRMIA's headline number

"AUC ~0.74 and 12% TPR at 1% FPR on WikiMIA, holding from 1B to 70B" was not in the paper. The real 12.0% is TPR at 0.1% FPR on AG News with GPT-2 targets (Table 1, four reference models, offline, $|Z| = 1000$), against 1.60% for pairwise RMIA in the same cell. No WikiMIA experiment and no 70B experiment appears in that paper.

Correction 3 — the reference count in the scaling study

"256 shadow models" was wrong: Hayes et al. (NeurIPS 2025) train 128 reference models for the Figure 2(a) sweep, and re-use one set of 128 for the 127 target replicas in Figure 5.

Correction 4 — attribution of the blind-baseline result

The blind-baseline paper was attributed to "Maini et al. 2024". Why LLMs break the classical toolkit

Scale collapses shadow models

LiRA-style calibration wants many full training runs per target. At pre-training scale each run is a language-model training job, so the recipe is not a constant factor harder than CIFAR — it is out of reach for almost everyone. Every LLM attack in the deck is engineered around that constraint, which is why the interesting question becomes "what is the cheapest defensible estimate of $q$" rather than "how many shadows can we afford". Hayes et al. are the exception that proves the rule: they paid the cost at small scale (10M–1B parameters, references trained on over 20B tokens of C4) precisely so the community would stop treating cost as the explanation.

The training distribution is unknown

For CIFAR the data is published. For a frontier model the mixture is proprietary and enormous, so an auditor cannot draw non-members from the same law. This is not a nuisance — it is the reason §03 exists. Once you cannot sample the member distribution, you build the non-member set some other way, and every convenient other way (a date cut-off, a different source) introduces a difference the attack can find without ever querying the model.

One pass

Classical MIA assumes many epochs. Most pre-training text is seen roughly once. Proposition 3 is the formal version of the intuition: the pooled signal-to-noise ratio is linear in $\eta E$, and the calibrated one falls as $N^{-1/2}$.

Three regimes, three difficulties

  • Pre-training: hardest. One epoch, vast $N$, contested non-member pool.
  • Fine-tuning: much easier, and Corollary 3 says exactly why — both ratios move the right way.
  • Context and retrieval: a different problem. The "training set" is a context window or a retrieval corpus, and the null hypothesis of the membership game is no longer well posed. This is on the closing "Where the recipe runs out" slide.

Every reported number in the literature should be read against which regime was tested. Most disagreements about "does MIA work on LLMs" dissolve once the regime is named.

§1 Perplexity, and why calibration is the whole subject

The baseline

For $x = (x_1,\dots,x_T)$ and a language model $p_\theta$,

$$\text{NLL}(x) \;=\; -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}), \qquad \text{PPL}(x) \;=\; \exp(\text{NLL}(x)),$$

and the attack is $\mathcal{A}(x) = \mathbf{1}[\text{PPL}(x) < \tau]$. It is the published baseline in essentially every LLM MIA paper, and the deck treats it as the degenerate case of Definition 1 with a constant null.

The confound, said carefully

Perplexity mixes memorization with intrinsic predictability. "The cat sat on the mat" is cheap for any model; a sentence from a niche paper is expensive even if the model trained on it. In the notation of Proposition 4, the raw score carries the difficulty term $a(x)$, whose spread $\sigma_a^2$ sits in the denominator of the raw signal-to-noise ratio. Calibration replaces $\sigma_a^2$ by the spread $\sigma_\delta^2$ of the estimation error of the null. It helps if and only if $\sigma_\delta^2 < \sigma_a^2$ — which is the one-line reason a bad reference model can make an attack worse.

Teaching note

It pays to spend a full minute on the direction of the inequality in Proposition 4 before showing any of the five estimators. Students who see the estimators first tend to read them as a bag of tricks; students who see the inequality first read each one as a bet about $\sigma_\delta^2$, which is what they are.

§2 Neighbourhood (Mattern et al., Findings of ACL 2023)

Mask and fill

Replace the unknown global reference by a local one: mask random spans of $x$ and fill them with a separate masked LM (T5 in the original work), producing neighbours $\tilde x^{(1)},\dots,\tilde x^{(K)}$ that keep the content and change the surface form. The score is on the slides. The engineering detail worth mentioning aloud: span length and mask rate are hyper-parameters, and the attack is sensitive to both, because they control how far the neighbours travel from $x$.

What the paper actually claims

Their headline is a comparison, not an absolute number: reference-based attacks are highly sensitive to the distribution of the reference data, and the neighbourhood attack matches a reference attack that has been given full knowledge of the training distribution. Read as a statement about $\sigma_\delta^2$, that is the strongest form of the claim: local perturbation buys you what an oracle reference would have bought.

Where it breaks

  • Structured text — code, mathematics, legal boilerplate. Most fills are invalid, every neighbour is improbable, and the score inflates for members and non-members alike.
  • Short text — few positions to perturb, so the neighbourhood is small and the rank test is coarse. Lemma 1 makes this precise: the finest achievable level is $1/(K+1)$.
  • Mask-model mismatch — a small filler LM produces unrealistic neighbours in technical domains, and exchangeability fails in the direction that flatters the attack.

§3 SPV-MIA (Fu et al., NeurIPS 2024)

The mechanism, stated correctly

SPV-MIA does not simply sample continuations and average their log-probabilities. It prompts the target model to generate a dataset, then fine-tunes the reference model on that self-generated data, so the reference approximates the target's distribution without having seen the target's records. The membership signal is then framed as "probabilistic variation" and grounded in memorization rather than overfitting.

The assumption it needs

Self-generations must match the fine-tuning distribution closely and reproduce individual training records rarely. Both halves matter. If the generations miss the distribution, the reference is a poor null; if they reproduce records, the reference has effectively seen the members and the calibration cancels the signal it was meant to expose.

Reported numbers, with their setting

The paper reports that SPV-MIA raises MIA AUC "from 0.7 to a significantly high level of 0.9", a best average AUC of 0.924 over all scenarios (four LLMs, three datasets), and about a 30% AUC improvement over the strongest reference-model baseline. All of this is on fine-tuned targets. Nothing in the paper licenses a pre-training claim, and Corollary 3 explains why one should not expect the numbers to transfer.

§4 Context-aware MIA (Chang et al., EMNLP 2025)

The idea

Sequence-level scores throw away the shape of the loss curve. Members and non-members can end at similar average loss while getting there differently: a member's per-token loss drops quickly as context accumulates and then stays low and smooth; a non-member's loss stays comparatively flat and noisy.

The statistics

  • Token-diversity calibration: $d_x = |\mathrm{Dedup}(x)|/|x|$ and $f_{\text{Cal}}(x) = L(x)/d_x$ — repetitive text is easy for anyone, so divide it out.
  • Cut-off loss: average only the first $T'$ tokens, where context is still scarce.
  • Slope: the least-squares slope of $L_t$ on $t$ over those first $T'$ tokens.
  • Low-loss counting: count tokens below a threshold — fixed, sequence-mean, or running-mean.
  • Loss fluctuation: approximate entropy and Lempel–Ziv complexity of the loss sequence.
  • Repetition amplification: feed $x$ twice; non-members gain more from the repeat.

The deck shows the two that carry the argument (cut-off loss and slope) and lists the rest in a table.

Numbers and benchmark choice

On Pythia-160M with the GitHub domain, context calibration raises TPR at 1% FPR from 23.13% to 63.43% (their Figure 1). Figure 3 shows a member slope of $-0.014$ against a non-member slope of $-0.000$ on Pile-CC. They evaluate on MIMIR and say explicitly that they avoid WikiMIA because a model-free baseline reaches about 98.7% AUC there — which is the observation §03 promotes to Proposition 7.

§5 InfoRMIA (Tao & Shokri, 2025)

The composite null

RMIA tests $x$ against one competitor $z$ at a time and counts how often the pairwise ratio clears a threshold $\gamma$; the output has granularity $1/|Z|$ and carries a tunable knob. InfoRMIA replaces the count by a Bayes factor averaged in the log domain over the whole population law, which is continuous and has no $\gamma$. That is the entire methodological difference, and it is why the gain shows up at low FPR rather than in AUC: discretisation destroys resolution exactly in the tail.

The decomposition

Their Equation 3 is Theorem 1 in the deck. The second term is a KL divergence that does not mention $x$, so for a fixed target model it is a constant and the ranking is by $\log p(x\mid\theta) - \log p(x)$ — Definition 1 with $q$ equal to the population marginal. This is the cleanest statement in the lecture of why seventeen years of attacks are one object.

Token level

Applying Equation 3 per token with $Z_t$ = every other vocabulary token gives a per-position score whose weight is derived rather than designed. Sequence aggregation is by averaging or min-$k$, and the authors label this a proof of concept. The deck says so on the slide and repeats it in "Claimed, not proved".

§6 Benchmarks and the empirical wall

MIMIR (Duan et al., COLM 2024)

Seven Pile domains, members and non-members drawn from one pool and split at random; five attacks (loss, reference, Min-K%, zlib, neighbourhood) against Pythia and Pythia-dedup at 160M to 12B, plus GPT-Neo and OLMo. Their verbatim conclusion is that no single attack or target model exceeds AUC 0.6, with the exception of the GitHub domain.

Two of their figures are on the slides. Figure 2 (left) shows AUC rising early in training and then gradually declining as steps accumulate; Figure 2 (right), on Datablations-C4, shows performance increasing linearly with the number of effective epochs. The second is the empirical check on Corollary 3 and also the place where Model M1 under-predicts, because M1 freezes gradients and so cannot see that repeated exposure drives $\|g_x\|$ down for members.

The temporal-shift experiment

Their Table 3 sweeps a temporally-shifted Wikipedia split from 160M to 12B. The reference attack climbs from 0.602 to 0.796 — a confound that grows with model size, which is exactly the shape one would mistake for a scaling law about memorization. Mean 7-gram overlap between members and non-members is 39.3% on a natural split and 13.9% on the shifted one. Their sentence is worth quoting in the lecture: thresholds derived with temporally-shifted non-members end up testing for temporal shift rather than membership.

Blind baselines (Das, Zhang & Tramèr, DATA-FM at ICLR 2025)

Eight published evaluation datasets; blind attacks that never query the model beat state-of-the-art MIAs on them. WikiMIA goes from a best published AUC of 83.9 to 99.0 blind, and TPR at 5% FPR from 43.2 to 94.7. On date-controlled splits the gap closes — Temporal Wiki 79.6 to 79.9 — which is the useful contrast: the blind attack is not magic, it is measuring whatever difference the split introduced.

Scaling the strongest attack (Hayes et al., NeurIPS 2025)

The Figure 2(a) sweep is LiRA with 128 references, Chinchilla-optimal, one epoch: AUC 0.592, 0.699, 0.689, 0.547, 0.654, 0.553 at 10M, 85M, 302M, 489M, 604M and 1018M parameters. The instability experiment retrains a 302M target 127 times on $2^{19}$ samples and tests $H_0: \theta_x = 1/2$ with a two-sided exact binomial test at level 0.05: mean AUC 0.752 ± 0.007, yet about 15.4% ± 0.6% of true positives at FPR $10^{-3}$ are statistically coin flips, rising to 42.2% ± 0.9% once highly unstable records are included. Members are more coin-flip-like than non-members, which is the worst possible direction for an auditor.

§7 From inference to extraction

MIA as the ranker

Extraction is expensive: generate candidates, then verify them against a corpus the auditor may not hold. A membership score that is far too weak to accuse anyone can still be strong enough to sort, and sorting is all the extraction pipeline needs. This is the practical reason a 0.6-AUC attack is not useless.

The relation

Proposition 8 makes the easy direction precise: greedy extraction is a membership test whose false-positive rate is at most $2^{-h}$, where $h$ is the conditional min-entropy of the suffix. The converse fails, and the deck gives the counterexample explicitly — a record the model prefers at probability 0.4 against a competitor at 0.5 is never emitted by greedy decoding yet is overwhelmingly inferable. The lesson to state aloud: verbatim recovery is a decoding accident; membership evidence is a likelihood ratio.

Ownership note

Secret Sharer exposure, Feldman's long-tail argument, extractable versus discoverable memorization, Min-K% with its tail-sufficiency proposition, Min-K%++ and ACR belong to the memorization lecture and are recalled here, not re-derived. The one new question this deck asks about Min-K% is whether $S_\alpha$ is calibrated — Proposition 5 answers no.

§8 Defenses

DP fine-tuning

The only defense in the lecture with a guarantee against every future attack. Proposition 9 converts the ROC cap into an AUC ceiling, $1 - (1-\delta)^2/(2e^\varepsilon)$, and the table of values is the honest way to discuss deployed budgets: 0.697 at $\varepsilon = 0.5$, 0.816 at $\varepsilon = 1$, 0.932 at $\varepsilon = 2$, 0.9998 at $\varepsilon = 8$. A guarantee at $\varepsilon = 8$ forbids nothing an attack was going to achieve anyway. The utility cost of DP fine-tuning is real and workload-dependent; the deck deliberately quotes no accuracy figure, because the ones previously here were not traceable to a source.

Deduplication and curation

Deduplication removes the repeat count that acts as extra epochs, which is a genuine mechanism and visible in the Pythia-dedup results. It gives nothing formal. Curation removes records rather than protecting them, which is a different promise than the one a data subject is usually asking about.

Output filters

Filters intercept generated text, not the likelihood interface. Everything in §02 needs only the scores, and log-probabilities are exposed by most serving APIs and by any open-weights release. Blocking verbatim emission leaves the inferable set unchanged — which is Proposition 8 read from the defender's side.

Unlearning and the audit framing

Verification of unlearning is itself a membership-inference problem: the method is convincing only if a strong attack cannot detect the record afterwards. Proposition 6 is the caution to attach to any such audit — a good aggregate number says nothing about the record actually in dispute.

Legal context

GDPR Article 17, the New York Times v. OpenAI litigation and provenance disputes generally all reach for MIA-style evidence. The chain is: a detector flags a document, the plaintiff alleges unauthorised training, the deployer is asked to demonstrate removal. Propositions 6 and 7 are the two places that chain breaks — an aggregate AUC licenses no per-record claim, and a date-split benchmark licenses no claim about the model at all.

Pseudocode

Perplexity baseline

def ppl_attack(model, text, tau):
    nll = -mean([log_prob(model, t, text[:i]) for i, t in enumerate(text)])
    return exp(nll) < tau

Neighbourhood

def neighbourhood_attack(model, mask_lm, text, K):
    nbrs = [mask_and_fill(mask_lm, text) for _ in range(K)]
    return log_prob(model, text) - mean([log_prob(model, n) for n in nbrs])

Context-aware slope (CAMIA)

def camia_slope(model, text, T_cut):
    L = [-log_prob(model, tok, text[:t]) for t, tok in enumerate(text[:T_cut])]
    tbar, Lbar = mean(range(T_cut)), mean(L)
    num = sum((t - tbar) * (L[t] - Lbar) for t in range(T_cut))
    den = sum((t - tbar) ** 2 for t in range(T_cut))
    return num / den          # members fall faster: more negative

Token-level InfoRMIA

def informia_token(model, text, population_lm):
    # per position, compare x_t against the whole vocabulary as the null
    s = []
    for t, tok in enumerate(text):
        num = log_prob(model, tok, text[:t])
        den = log_prob(population_lm, tok, text[:t])
        s.append(num - den)
    return mean(s)            # or min-k over positions; authors call this a proof of concept

Reading the comparison

  • Perplexity conflates memorization with intrinsic predictability: a constant null.
  • Neighbourhood builds the null out of $x$ itself, and needs exchangeability to be a valid test.
  • CAMIA changes the statistic rather than the null: the shape of the loss curve, not its mean.
  • InfoRMIA uses the population marginal as the null and derives the per-token weight instead of designing it.

Open problems

  • A pre-training benchmark that is realistic and provably unconfounded. Random splits of one pool are valid by the corollary to Proposition 7 but hard to obtain for models whose training set is proprietary.
  • Per-record certificates. Proposition 6 says an aggregate number does not license an accusation. What would?
  • Null estimation without exchangeability. Corpora where neither references nor neighbours are exchangeable — code, mathematics, structured records.
  • Principled sequence-level aggregation of token evidence, rather than averaging or min-$k$.
  • Defenses between deduplication and full DP that still bound something.
  • Continual training, synthetic data and retrieval, where the membership hypothesis itself stops being well defined.
