What Changes With Autonomy

Longer horizons, fewer checks

Beyond a Defended Agent

Start with injection controls in place: least privilege, data-flow limits, approval. Still not injection-proof.

Longer horizons and interacting agents add new failure modes.

Autonomy Is a Dial, Not a Switch

Four settings decide how autonomous an agent is:

  • Steps taken per task
  • Time horizon of the task
  • Tools and permissions held
  • Interval between human checks

Turning any setting up widens what can go wrong between checks.

Small Errors Compound Over Long Tasks

Illustrative arithmetic, not a measured agent:

  • Each step right with probability 0.95
  • Steps fail independently
  • All 20 steps right: $0.95^{20} \approx 0.36$

Real errors are correlated, and agents sometimes recover.

Fewer checks per step means errors accumulate unseen.

Time Horizon: The Definition

Measure each task by how long skilled humans took to succeed.

  • Plot the agent's success against human task length
  • Fit a smooth S-curve
  • 50% horizon: length where fitted success is 50%
Kwa et al. (METR), "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks", arXiv 2503.14499 (2025), §2–§3. Curve is schematic.

How a Horizon Is Computed

  1. 170 software and research tasks, from seconds to about 30 hours
  2. Task length: mean time of humans who succeeded; 21 tasks use researcher estimates
  3. 8 runs per model–task pair; the scaffold differs for some models
  4. Fit success against log task length
  5. Read off where the fitted curve crosses 50%

The 80% horizon is 4–6 times shorter than the 50% horizon.

Kwa et al., arXiv 2503.14499, §2–§3 (task suites, geometric mean of successful baselines), §4 (80% horizon), App. C.3 (modular-public for most models; triframe or duet for o1, o1-preview; a simple prompting scaffold for SWAA).

Horizons Doubled About Every Seven Months

Key: dots are models released 2019–2025; y is 50% horizon (log). Doubling: 207 days.

Kwa et al., arXiv 2503.14499 (2025), Fig. 1, cropped, and §3.2: 95% CI 166–240 days; GPT-2 about 2 seconds, o3 about 110 minutes.

What a Time Horizon Is Not

It is

The task length at which the fitted success probability is 50%, on this task set, for this model–scaffold pair.

It is not

How long an agent can safely run unattended. A one-hour horizon fails half of one-hour tasks.

  • Benchmark tasks are auto-scored and self-contained
  • Real work: other agents, changing environments, high reliability
Kwa et al., arXiv 2503.14499, §7 (limitations: messiness, reliability, task distribution).

Replication Scores, Domain by Domain

Key: solid = pass@1, one try succeeds; light = pass@10, at least one of 10 tries succeeds.

Black, Cooper Stickland et al. (UK AISI), "RepliBench", arXiv 2504.18565v2 (2025, preprint), Fig. 1, §3; bars: Claude 3.7 Sonnet, 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o, o1, o3-mini; 86 sandboxed tasks.

Component Success Does Not Establish Full Replication

Components

Claude 3.7 Sonnet: over 50% pass@10 on 15 of 20 task families, e.g. renting compute.

End-to-end

Sandboxed scaffold-replication task, hard variant: one success, by Claude 3.7 Sonnet.

  • Separate component failures: identity checks, persistence at hardest settings
  • Authors: no truly end-to-end evaluation yet

RepliBench, arXiv 2504.18565v2, §5.1 and §6 ("Lack of end-to-end evaluations"). Five models, 25 runs per variant.

A failed evaluation is not always strategic concealment.

Today's two anchors: in-context scheming; sandbagging.

In-Context Scheming: The Setup

  • Question: does the agent disable oversight to pursue X?
  • Goal and situation are given in context
  • Sandboxed; no real weights or oversight
Meinke et al. (Apollo Research), "Frontier Models are Capable of In-context Scheming", arXiv 2412.04984v2 (2025), §2–§3. Schematic.

Sandbagging: Strategic Underperformance

Sandbagging

Strategic underperformance on an evaluation: scoring below actual capability because someone benefits.

Developer sandbagging and AI-system sandbagging both count.

van der Weij et al., "AI Sandbagging", ICLR 2025 (arXiv 2406.07358v4), §2.

Prompted to underperform on hazardous-knowledge questions only:

Other Explanations for a Failed Eval

Not strategic

  • Weak elicitation: wrong prompt or scaffold
  • Refusals scored as failures
  • Ending early, giving up

Possibly strategic

  • Noticing the test, behaving differently
  • Underperforming when told high scores hurt

Rule out the left column before claiming the right.

RepliBench §6 (refusals, early termination, noticing simulation); van der Weij §2 (elicitation); Meinke §3.6.

MAST: Ordinary Coordination Failures

Key: % = share of all failures annotated in 1,642 traces from 7 frameworks; sums to 100%.

Cemri et al., "Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?", NeurIPS 2025 D&B (arXiv 2503.13657v3), Fig. 1.

What MAST Implies

  • Failure rates 41% to 86.7% across seven open-source systems
  • Common: repeating steps, ignoring the spec, weak verification
  • ChatDev, GPT-4o, 32 tasks: 25.0% → 34.4% (clearer roles) → 40.6% (verify loop)
  • Authors: isolated fixes are not enough

No attacker here: design is the first risk.

Cemri et al., NeurIPS 2025 D&B, §1, §4 and App. H.2, Table 5 (ProgramDev-v0; success rates, not relative gains).

From Injection to Propagation

Recall injection. With many agents, one agent's output is the next one's input.

Each hop is an injection; the payload carries its own copy.

Prompt Infection: The Setup

  • Simulated multi-agent apps: reader, strategist, writer, coder
  • One injected prompt in a document, email or page
  • Four parts: hijack, payload, data, self-copy
  • GPT-4o and GPT-3.5 Turbo; 360 task–tool pairs

Self-replication

The injected text instructs each agent to pass the text on.

Lee & Tiwari, "Prompt Infection: LLM-to-LLM Prompt Injection within Multi-Agent Systems", arXiv 2410.07283v1 (2024, preprint), §3–§4.

Prompt Infection: What Spread, and When

Experiment 1: multi-agent apps

  • Self-replicating beat non-replicating when all agents share messages
  • Self-replicating attacks ignored: GPT-4o 66%, GPT-3.5 9%
  • Once compromised, GPT-4o carried out payloads more reliably

Experiment 2: society of 10–50 agents

Reached every agent only when the attack also inflated memory importance. Otherwise it died out.

Lee & Tiwari, arXiv 2410.07283v1 (preprint), §5 and Fig. 6; simulated, not real websites.

Defending Each Hop

  • Treat every agent's output as untrusted input
  • Least privilege; limit who can message whom
DefenseWhat it does

Together: blocked all the authors' handcrafted attacks. Cost to normal use: not reported.

A defense tested on known attacks is not tested adaptively.

Lee & Tiwari, arXiv 2410.07283v1 (preprint), §6, Table 2 and Fig. 7; marking alone let 76% through after a counterattack.

Control

Safety without trusting the model

From Alignment to Control

Alignment

Make the model want the right things. Hard to verify.

Control

Assume the model may be adversarial. Design checks that keep outcomes safe anyway.

Control asks: is it safe even if the model attacks?

Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan & Roger, "AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion", ICML 2024 (arXiv 2312.06942v5), §1.

The Control Setup

  • U: GPT-4, untrusted; the red team swaps in an attack policy
  • T: GPT-3.5. Trusted by assumption: too weak to subvert unnoticed
  • H: scarce audit, simulated by GPT-4 on a channel the red team cannot reach
Greenblatt et al., ICML 2024, Fig. 1 (right panel, "Our red-teaming testbed") and §2.

The Control Game

ElementIn the paper

SafetyP(no backdoor in whole run)

Greenblatt et al., ICML 2024, §2 (APPS; worst case over the scenario, not an average).

Trusted Monitoring, Step by Step

If H finds a backdoor, use only T for the rest of the run.

Attackers Choose When to Attack

Many control evals assume the attacker tries at a fixed random rate.

Start policy

Attack only on tasks that look promising.

Stop policy

Abort mid-task when detection looks likely.

  • BashArena: shell tasks, 4 security side tasks · LinuxArena: 20 live app environments
  • Attacker Claude Opus 4.6; trusted monitor mimo-v2-flash; audit top 1%
  • New metric: caught ÷ (caught + uncaught successful), over attempted attacks only
Ge-Wang, Crosse et al., "Attack Selection in Agentic AI Control Evaluations Meaningfully Decreases Safety", arXiv 2606.06529v1 (Jun 2026), AIWILD workshop @ ICML 2026, §2–§3.

Attack Selection Lowers Measured Safety

Key: safety at 1% audit; n = held-out attacks left after filtering. † only CI excluding zero.

Upper bound: thresholds tuned against safety; a separate prompted scorer decides, not the attacker.

Ge-Wang, Crosse et al., arXiv 2606.06529v1 (2026), Table 2 and §3.3; part of the stop-policy drop is a smaller denominator.

A Benchmark Is Not a Deployment Guarantee

  • Results cover the strongest attacks tested, these tasks and budget
  • Weaker attacker in the eval: safety overstated
  • The trusted monitor can itself be attacked
  • Deployments can run longer than the evaluated lengths

Report the attacker, the budget and the denominator.

Greenblatt et al., §6; Ge-Wang et al. (2026), §4.4 and App. A; Terekhov et al., arXiv 2510.09462v2 (2026), injections against monitors.

Synthesis

A checklist for the whole course

Established vs Not Established

Established

  • Task horizons grew fast on these benchmarks
  • Replication components often succeed
  • Induced scheming and sandbagging occur
  • Multi-agent systems fail often, without attackers

Not established

  • Safe unattended run times
  • End-to-end autonomous replication
  • Deceptive intent in deployment
  • Control guarantees outside the eval

A Checklist for the Whole Course

Five questions for any AI system you build or assess:

  1. What can it do? Tools, permissions, actions
  2. Whose data does it read? Private, untrusted, both
  3. How long does it act alone? Steps between checks
  4. Who checks it? And why trust the checker
  5. What evidence supports safety? Against which attacker

Open Problems

  • End-to-end evaluations of autonomy, not just parts
  • Telling sandbagging apart from weak elicitation
  • Measuring propagation in real multi-agent deployments
  • Control protocols for long, open-ended agent tasks
  • Evaluations against adaptive attackers, reported honestly

Each asks: does the evidence match the claim?

Key Takeaways

  • Autonomy is a dial; errors compound between checks
  • A time horizon is a capability measure, not a safety limit
  • Keep induced, observed and intended behavior apart
  • Multi-agent risk: coordination, propagation, collusion
  • Control: assume an attacker, state the budget

Ask what the evidence shows, under which conditions.

Who checks it?
