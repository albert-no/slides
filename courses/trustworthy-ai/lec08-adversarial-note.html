1. Adversarial Robustness: When Small Changes Break Models

Why robustness · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈0.5 min

Welcome to Lecture 8. So far our failures came from the model or its training data. Today, the input itself becomes the problem: inputs the test set never checked, and inputs someone chooses on purpose to make the model fail.

Key takeaway: Today: what happens when the input is chosen by an adversary.

5. Test Accuracy Measures an Ordinary Day

Why robustness · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈4 min

The usual answer is test accuracy. It rests on two assumptions. One: test inputs look like the training inputs, because both come from the same source. Two: nobody picks them to fool the model.

Under those assumptions, a score like 95% is meaningful. But it answers a narrow question: how good is the model on an ordinary day?

Key takeaway: Test accuracy assumes typical inputs and no adversary.

The 95% is an illustrative number, not a measurement.

6. Real Inputs Break Both Assumptions

Why robustness · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈5 min

Deployment breaks both. New conditions arrive: fog, sketches, a new hospital scanner. Nobody is trying to fool the model, but the inputs no longer look like the training set.

And sometimes someone picks the input: a spammer, a fraudster, a prankster, actively searching for a failure.

Both are inputs the test set never checked. First, a hint that models decide very differently from us.

Key takeaway: Shifted inputs and chosen inputs are both outside what the test set measures.

7. A Cat With Elephant Skin Is an "Elephant" to a CNN

Why robustness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈6.5 min

On (c), the network's top guess is an elephant: it follows the texture. Any person sees a cat.

Key takeaway: The CNN followed texture where people follow shape.

9. Different Reasons Make a Weak Surface

Why robustness · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈8.5 min

An attack surface is any place an attacker can push a system into failing. Here is why different reasons create one.

We never chose which cues the model uses. Cues we ignore, like texture, can still drive its answer. So an attacker can edit those cues, and we will not notice, because we are not looking at them.

A model relying on other cues can fail where we do not expect.

Key takeaway: Cues the model uses but we ignore are a place to attack.

10. What We Want: Robustness

Why robustness · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈9.5 min

Name the goal. Robustness: the answer stays right when the input changes in ways that should not matter.

Three examples: tiny pixel noise, invisible to us; a natural change such as fog or a new camera; a deliberate change, a small sticker. Each should leave a stop sign a stop sign.

This lecture focuses on the third kind: changes an attacker picks. We return to natural changes briefly at the end of Section 07.

Key takeaway: Robustness: the answer survives changes that should not matter.

11. Why Robustness Matters

Why robustness · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈10 min

Three reasons. Safety: a misread sign can crash a car. Security: an attacker can steer the output. Trust: we cannot vouch for an answer that flips under a change nobody can see.

Accuracy means usually right. Robustness means staying right when pushed.

Key takeaway: Safety, security, trust: accuracy is not enough.

12. 02 — A Tiny Nudge

A tiny nudge · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈10.5 min

One question frames today: if a model is accurate on test data, can we trust it when someone else chooses the input?

Key takeaway: Keep this question in mind all lecture.

13. A Tiny Nudge Flips a Confident Answer

A tiny nudge · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈12.5 min

The most famous picture in this field. On the left, an image GoogLeNet calls "panda" with 57.7% confidence. In the middle, a pattern that looks like coloured static. We add a tiny multiple of it, 0.007, to the panda. On the right is the result. To us it is the same panda. The network now says "gibbon", with 99.3% confidence.

How small is 0.007? The authors say it equals the size of the smallest bit of an 8-bit image encoding after GoogLeNet's conversion to real numbers: about one step of brightness per pixel.

Notice the line above the figure. Every example today comes with a threat model: what the attacker controls, what they know, and what they want. Here: they change pixels by about one step each, they know the model's gradients, and they want any wrong label.

Key takeaway: A change too small to see can flip a confident prediction.

The figure

Fig. 1 of Goodfellow et al.: x (panda, 57.7%) + .007 × sign(∇ₓJ(θ, x, y)) (the noise, itself classified "nematode", 8.2%) = adversarial image ("gibbon", 99.3%).

Setup, model, date

GoogLeNet on ImageNet; ε = .007 in the ℓ∞ sense; white-box attack (FGSM, slide 57). Published 2015.

What this establishes

An adversarial example exists for this image and model, at a change humans do not notice.

What this does not establish

Nothing about how often this happens on natural inputs, or about other models, budgets or physical images. One illustrative example.

Primary sources

  • Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy, "Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples" (ICLR 2015) — the panda (Fig. 1), FGSM, the "too linear" argument (§3) and Figs. 2 and 4.

14. Anatomy of an Adversarial Example

A tiny nudge · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈13.5 min

Let us name the parts. An adversarial example is x′ = x + δ. The change δ is too small to alter the true label, at least for a human, but it is chosen so that the model's answer changes.

The key word is "chosen". Random noise of the same size usually does nothing. The attacker searches for the specific δ that hurts the model most.

And "small" needs a ruler. In Section 04 we will fix it with a budget ε and a norm, the way we measure size.

Key takeaway: Adversarial example = small, deliberately chosen change that flips the output.

The figure

Schematic drawn for this lecture: input x + change δ (size ≤ ε) = x′; a human still says panda, the model says gibbon.

Assumptions

"Too small to alter the true label" is the working assumption behind small-norm threat models. It is a judgement about humans, not a mathematical fact, and fails for large ε.

15. Not One Unlucky Image

A tiny nudge · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈14.5 min

Is the panda a lucky find? This 2014 paper, which named the phenomenon, shows six more. In each row: a correctly classified image on the left, the difference magnified ten times in the middle, and the adversarial image on the right. AlexNet labels every right-hand image "ostrich".

The authors state the examples were chosen strictly at random. Adversarial examples are the rule, not the exception, for standard networks.

Key takeaway: For standard networks, adversarial examples exist for almost every input.

17. A Weak Surface Attackers Can Use

A tiny nudge · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈16.5 min

Why does this matter for security? Three features make it a weak surface. The attacker touches only the input, not the model. The change is invisible, so a reviewer looking at the image sees nothing wrong. And it is found by a quick computer search, not by luck.

So the robustness goal for this lecture: no small change should flip the answer.

Key takeaway: Input-only, invisible, easy to find: an ideal weak surface.

18. Who Chooses the Input Changes the Question

A tiny nudge · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈17 min

Here is the conceptual shift. Normal test accuracy is an average case: nature draws inputs from the test distribution, and we count how many are right.

Robustness is a worst case: an adversary picks the input, within allowed changes, to make the model fail. That number is called robust accuracy.

A model can be 95% accurate and have near-zero robust accuracy. High accuracy answers the first question, not the second.

Key takeaway: Accuracy is average case; robustness is worst case.

19. 03 — Why It Happens

Why it happens · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈17.5 min

Three lenses on why: off the data, small moves add up, and hidden features. None of them is the single settled cause.

20. Training Covers a Thin Slice of Inputs

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈18.5 min

Think of every possible image of a given size. Almost all of them are static and noise. Natural photos form a thin slice of that space, and the training images sit on that slice.

The model is taught only on the slice; the rest is unseen. Our sketch is two-dimensional; a 224×224 colour image actually has about 150,000 numbers.

Key takeaway: Training data covers a thin slice; most of input space is never seen.

21. Off the Data, Any Answer Is Possible

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈19.5 min

24. Like You're Five: Many Tiny Pushes Add Up

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈22 min

Second lens, the simplest version. Imagine a balance scale with panda on one side and gibbon on the other. Every pixel is a grain of sand. Nudge every pixel a hair toward a wrong answer, here gibbon. One grain does nothing. But a picture has about 150,000 grains, and together they tip the scale.

That is the whole idea; the next three slides make it precise.

Key takeaway: Each pixel moves too little to see; together they tip the decision.

25. A Linear Score Adds Up Every Pixel

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈23 min

Now with a formula. A simple model scores a class by a weighted sum: s(x) = w·x, the sum over n pixel values of wᵢ xᵢ. Here w are the weights the model learned and n is the number of pixel values.

Change pixel i by δᵢ, and the score moves by the sum of wᵢ δᵢ: n small terms, one sum.

Key takeaway: A linear score sums n small contributions.

Primary sources

  • Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy, "Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples" (ICLR 2015) — the panda (Fig. 1), FGSM, the "too linear" argument (§3) and Figs. 2 and 4.

26. The Best Push: Follow the Sign of Each Weight

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈24 min

The rule: each pixel may move at most ε. What is the best move? Push each pixel ε the way its weight points: up where the weight is positive, down where it is negative. That is δ = ε·sign(w). Tiny example: if w₁ = +2, raise pixel 1 by ε and the score gains 2ε; if w₂ = −3, lower pixel 2 by ε and the score gains 3ε. Both moves increase the chosen score.

Then every term wᵢ δᵢ is positive; nothing cancels. The score moves by ε times the sum of |wᵢ|, written ε‖w‖₁.

Key takeaway: Aligning each pixel with its weight makes every term count.

Assumptions

Exact for a linear score; for deep networks this is a local approximation.

Primary sources

  • Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy, "Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples" (ICLR 2015) — the panda (Fig. 1), FGSM, the "too linear" argument (§3) and Figs. 2 and 4.

27. More Pixels, Bigger Swing

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈25 min

Put numbers in, illustrative ones: ε = 0.01 per pixel and an average |wᵢ| of 0.01. With 10 pixels, the score moves 0.001. With 1,000 pixels, 0.1. With 150,000 pixels, 15.

Each pixel's change stays the same tiny size. The score change grows with n. In high dimension, invisible per-pixel changes can make a large difference.

Key takeaway: Per-pixel change fixed, total effect grows with the number of pixels.

Assumptions

Illustrative numbers; real weights are not all the same size.

28. This Push Has a Name: FGSM

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈26 min

This push has a name: FGSM, the Fast Gradient Sign Method. Move every pixel ε along the sign of the gradient of the loss with respect to the input.

The gradient tells us which way each pixel raises the loss. It is the same sign-push idea, now applied to the loss rather than one class score. Pushing up one class score and pushing up the loss are not identical in general; FGSM uses the loss. We will use it as an attack in Section 05.

Key takeaway: FGSM = the sign push, using the loss gradient.

Goodfellow et al., §3–4.

29. Non-Robust Features: What Is a "Feature"?

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈27 min

Third lens, from Ilyas and colleagues. First a definition: a feature is any pattern in the input that helps predict the label.

A robust feature, like ears, outline or fur, cannot be altered by a tiny change. A non-robust feature, like faint pixel patterns, still predicts the label, but a tiny change flips it. Humans cannot see the second kind.

Key takeaway: Non-robust features predict the label but flip under tiny changes.

Primary sources

  • Ilyas et al., "Adversarial Examples Are Not Bugs, They Are Features" (NeurIPS 2019) — the non-robust-features perspective and the retraining experiments of §3.

32. What Experiment 1 Means

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈30 min

Be precise about the bottom row. The non-robust dataset is rebuilt from a standard model's features. To us, each image looks like an ordinary picture, often of another class, with a label that looks wrong. Their Fig. 2 shows examples. It does not look like noise.

Train a fresh model on it and test on real CIFAR-10 images: 87.7%. Attack that model with a small ℓ2 change, ε = 0.25: 0.8%. So the patterns we cannot see carry real signal about the label, and the model that learned them is fragile.

Do non-robust features alone suffice? That is Experiment 2.

36. Three Lenses, Not One Cause

Why it happens · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈33.5 min

Side by side. Off the data: unseen inputs are unconstrained; it explains confident nonsense, with fooling images as evidence. Too linear: small moves add up; it explains one-step attacks like FGSM, with linear models fooled by one step as evidence. These are useful lenses with evidence; none is settled as the single cause, and they are compatible.

Key takeaway: Three compatible lenses; the cause is not settled.

42. The Budget ε Bounds the Change

Who attacks · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈39.5 min

Control first. The allowed changes form a ball of radius ε around the input: every x + δ with the size of δ at most ε.

Two common settings. On CIFAR-10 with the ℓ∞ norm, ε = 8/255: each pixel may change by about 3% of its range. On MNIST, ε = 0.3: each pixel by 30%, which is large but MNIST digits are nearly black-and-white.

These are conventions that let papers compare numbers, not laws. The right ε depends on what changes would really be imperceptible, or harmless, in your application.

Key takeaway: ε is a convention for comparison, not a law of perception.

43. ℓ∞ Ball: Each Coordinate Moves at Most ε

Who attacks · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈40.5 min

Which ruler measures the size of δ? Take an image with just two pixels, so δ is a point in a plane. The ℓ∞ norm is the larger of |δ₁| and |δ₂|. The ball ‖δ‖∞ ≤ ε is the shaded square: every allowed change.

Each pixel moves up to ε, on its own. The corner (ε, ε) is allowed: both pixels move the full ε. In a real image, every pixel can move ε at once, which tends to look like faint noise everywhere.

Key takeaway: ℓ∞: each coordinate within ε; the ball is a square (a box).

44. ℓ2 Ball: The Total Change Is at Most ε

Who attacks · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈41.5 min

The ℓ2 norm is the length of δ: the square root of δ₁² + δ₂². The ball ‖δ‖₂ ≤ ε is the shaded disk, radius ε in every direction.

There is one shared budget: a big move on one pixel leaves less for the rest. The corner (ε, ε) is now outside, because its length is √2·ε.

Key takeaway: ℓ2: one shared length budget; the ball is a disk.

45. ℓ∞ vs ℓ2: Two Shapes of "Small"

Who attacks · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈42 min

Overlay the two at the same ε. The ℓ∞ square contains the ℓ2 disk. ℓ∞ gives each coordinate its own cap ε. ℓ2 makes the coordinates share one Euclidean-distance budget ε. At the same ε, every change in the disk is also in the square. Neither norm forces the change to be spread out or concentrated.

Same ε, different sets. In practice each norm uses its own ε, and a model robust under one ruler need not be robust under the other.

Key takeaway: The norm defines what "small" means; no automatic guarantee outside the stated threat model.

55. The Attacker's Goal: One Wrong Point in the Ball

Worst case · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈49 min

The exact intent of the attacker. Given a test image x with true label y, find any x′ in the ball around x with f(x′) ≠ y. One wrong point is enough: the attacker wins.

Searching every point is hopeless: the ball has infinitely many points in about 150,000 dimensions. The loss shows where to look.

Key takeaway: Goal: find one misclassified point inside the ball around x.

56. An Attack Is a Search Inside the Ball

Worst case · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈50 min

Formally, the attacker solves: maximize the loss L of the model's output on x + δ, against the true label y, over all δ with size at most ε.

Three ingredients. The loss, which is high when the model is wrong. The gradient of the loss with respect to the input, which says in which direction the loss rises. And the constraint: stay inside the ball.

This mirrors training. Training lowers the loss by changing the weights; an attack raises it by changing the input, with the weights fixed.

Key takeaway: Attack = maximize the loss over the allowed ball.

The figure

Equation plus three boxes drawn for this lecture.

Going further

Other formulations exist. Carlini & Wagner (2017) minimize the size of the change plus a penalty: minimize ‖δ‖₂² + c · f(x + δ), where f is a margin loss on the logits Z, f(x′) = max(max_{i≠t} Z(x′)ᵢ − Z(x′)ₜ, −κ); f ≤ 0 means the target t wins or ties, and f = −κ once t leads by at least κ. They handle the pixel range with a change of variables, δ = ½(tanh(w) + 1) − x, and search over c. κ controls how confident the misclassification must be. Their attacks found adversarial examples with 100% success against defensive distillation, a defense that had reduced earlier attacks' success from 95% to 0.5%. Details: lec08tech.html.

57. FGSM: One Step Along the Gradient Sign

Worst case · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈51.5 min

The simplest attack: the Fast Gradient Sign Method. Compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the input once. Take its sign, plus or minus for each pixel. Move every pixel by ε in that direction. One step.

Why the sign? Under the ℓ∞ budget, each pixel may move at most ε, and if we replace the loss by its linearization, the best move is to push every pixel the full ε in the direction that raises the loss. That is the sign.

The figure shows it on a linear model, logistic regression distinguishing MNIST 3s from 7s. Panel (a) is the weights, (b) their sign, which is exactly the attack direction; (c) clean digits, (d) attacked digits at ε = .25. Error goes from 1.6% to 99%. The digits are still easy for us to read.

Key takeaway: FGSM: one step of size ε along the gradient sign; it exactly maximizes the linearized loss.

59. PGD: Gradient Descent, Run Uphill

Worst case · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈54 min

The fix uses an idea you already know. Training runs gradient descent on the weights: a small step that lowers the loss, repeated. PGD, Projected Gradient Descent, runs the same loop on the input, uphill: a small step that raises the loss.

One step: move the input a little, α, along the gradient sign. Then clip: if the step left the allowed ball around x, or the valid pixel range, pull it back. Repeat K times; PGD-7 or PGD-20 means 7 or 20 steps. In practice PGD also starts from a random point in the ball, and may restart several times and keep the worst point found.

FGSM is the one-step case: a single step of size ε. Madry and colleagues made PGD the default attack for evaluating and training robust models.

Key takeaway: PGD = gradient descent on the input, uphill, clipped back into the ball.

The figure

61. Weaker Attacks Overstate Robustness

Worst case · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈56.5 min

Why the attack matters, in one chart. The same adversarially trained CIFAR-10 model, the same ℓ∞ budget of 8/255, white box. With no attack, 87.3% accuracy. Against FGSM, 56.1%. PGD with 7 steps, 50.0%. The C&W-style margin loss with 30 steps, 46.8%. PGD with 20 steps, 45.8%.

Same model. The only thing that changed is how hard we searched, and the number dropped by ten points from FGSM to PGD-20. If a paper had reported only FGSM, it would have overstated robustness.

One explanation ties back to the third lens. Standard models also lean on non-robust features. Those features help accuracy, but a tiny change flips them. Robust training must give them up, and some accuracy goes with them.

There is also a compute cost. Every training step first runs an attack, so PGD-7 training costs roughly eight times a normal step.

Key takeaway: Giving up non-robust features costs accuracy; attacks in the loop cost compute.

68. Some Defenses Only Hide the Gradient

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈62.5 min

Many proposed defenses did something else: they broke the attacker's gradient without removing adversarial examples. Athalye, Carlini and Wagner call this obfuscated gradients, and name three kinds. Shattered: a non-differentiable step, so there is no useful slope. Stochastic: randomness, so each pass gives a different slope. Vanishing: the slope goes flat, then a cliff.

In all three, gradient attacks fail, so the defense looks strong. But adversarial examples still exist; the attacker just needs a better way to find them.

Key takeaway: Obfuscated gradients hide adversarial examples from the attack; they do not remove them.

69. Adapted Attacks Rebuild the Missing Gradient

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈64 min

How do you attack a defense that hides its gradient? Rebuild the gradient. If a step is broken or flat, use a smooth stand-in for it when computing the gradient: BPDA, Backward Pass Differentiable Approximation. If the defense is random on each pass, average the gradient over many random draws: EOT, Expectation over Transformation.

The result. Athalye and colleagues examined the nine white-box defenses accepted at ICLR 2018. Seven hid gradients. With adapted attacks, six were broken completely and one partially. Madry et al.'s adversarial training hides nothing, and it held at 47%.

Hiding the gradient delays the attacker; it does not stop one. A conference-reviewed defense is not a verified defense.

Key takeaway: Adapted attacks broke the defenses that hid gradients; adversarial training held.

70. Adaptive Attacks: Attack the Defense You Have

Defenses + evaluation · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈64.5 min

How to evaluate a defense properly: an adaptive attack, designed for that specific defense. Read the defense and target its weak part; BPDA and EOT are two standard tools.

And two diagnostics. With a huge ε, accuracy should go to about zero; if not, suspect the attack. If a black-box attack beats a white-box one, suspect masked gradients. These raise red flags; passing them proves nothing.

Key takeaway: Adapt the attack to the defense; diagnostics flag problems but prove nothing.

71. AutoAttack: Four Attacks, Nothing to Tune

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈65.5 min

Adaptive attacks need expertise, and each paper used to pick its own attack and settings. AutoAttack, from Croce and Hein, is a fixed test everyone can run. It bundles four attacks. A PGD that tunes its own step size, APGD-CE. The same, aimed at each wrong class in turn, APGD-T. One that looks for the smallest change that flips the answer, FAB-T. And Square, which uses no gradients at all, only queries; that catches defenses that merely hide gradients.

An input counts as robust only if all four fail. There are no settings to tune, and the same settings are used for every model, so a defense's authors cannot weaken the test by choosing them.

Key takeaway: A fixed bundle of diverse attacks, with nothing to tune.

73. RobustBench: The Same Test for Every Model

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈67.5 min

The point of RobustBench. Before it, each paper picked its own attack, so numbers were not comparable, and weak evaluations overstated robustness. RobustBench runs the same test on every entry: the same datasets, the same ε, AutoAttack. The results go on one public leaderboard. Outside adaptive attacks are welcome; if someone finds a stronger attack, the entry's "best known" number is lowered.

Entry rules matter. Models must have non-zero gradients, a deterministic forward pass, and no optimization loop inside. Those are exactly the cases where AutoAttack could be fooled by masking, so the rules exclude defenses AutoAttack cannot fairly test.

RobustBench is a standardized benchmark, not the universal standard for robustness, and it covers only its listed threat models.

Key takeaway: One test for every model makes progress measurable; explicit rules and explicit limits.

74. What Is "Accuracy Under Attack"?

Defenses + evaluation · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈68 min

Before reading a scoreboard, the key term. Accuracy under attack: the share of test images the model still gets right after an attack tries to fool each one.

The procedure: take each test image, one at a time; run the attack within budget ε; feed the result to the model; if the answer is still right, count it. The result is an empirical robust-accuracy estimate for this attack, not a guarantee against every allowed change; slide 77 returns to this. Higher is better for the defender.

Key takeaway: Empirical robust-accuracy estimate = share surviving the tested attack.

80. Empirical vs Certified Robustness

Defenses + evaluation · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈71.5 min

Everything so far was empirical: the attacks we tried failed. That gives an upper bound, which may fall.

The alternative is certified robustness: a proof that no change in a stated region can change the prediction. A certificate proves the prediction is stable, not that it is correct. Certified accuracy counts only examples that are both correct and certified, and that number is a lower bound on robust accuracy, whatever attack comes next.

Key takeaway: Empirical = upper bound that may fall; certified accuracy = lower bound (stable and correct).

81. What a Certificate Promises

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈72.5 min

What exactly is a certificate? It is about one input x. A certificate is a proof that every change δ with ℓ2 size at most R leaves the prediction unchanged.

Every change, not an average one: the green ball contains every point within R, including the attacker's best, the red worst case. So it holds against adversarial changes, not just random ones. Outside the ball there is no promise. And another input needs its own proof, with its own radius.

Key takeaway: A certificate is a worst-case guarantee, for one input, within a stated radius.

82. Randomized Smoothing: Vote Under Noise

Defenses + evaluation · optional · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈73.5 min

One practical certification method: randomized smoothing. Take any classifier f, the base classifier. Build a new one, g, the smoothed classifier: g returns the class f picks most often when Gaussian noise is added to x. In practice g cannot be computed exactly. The paper's PREDICT and CERTIFY procedures estimate it by sampling noisy copies, and they abstain when no class clearly leads.

The figure shows one noisy copy of the panda. Heavy noise, still recognizably a panda. If f is trained on noisy images, it can still classify them.

Note what the noise is for. It only builds g. The certificate for g is then worst case: it holds against any attacker inside the radius, not just against random noise.

Key takeaway: Smoothed classifier g = majority vote of f under Gaussian noise.

83. A Clear Vote Survives Any Small Shift

Defenses + evaluation · optional · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈74.5 min

Why does voting give a certificate? Intuition: if the vote at x is lopsided, say 90% panda, then moving x a little only shifts the noise cloud a little; the vote cannot swing to another class until you move far enough.

Cohen and colleagues make this exact in Theorem 1, and it is worst case: it holds for every shift with ℓ2 size below R, including the attacker's best. At a fixed noise level σ, a larger vote margin gives a larger certified ℓ2 radius R within which g cannot change its answer. The exact formula is in the technical supplement. The figure shows why: the worst-case base classifier is a straight cut perpendicular to the shift. Note that raising σ also changes the votes, so more noise does not automatically mean a bigger radius.

Three careful points. It certifies g, the smoothed classifier, not the original f. The vote probabilities are estimated by sampling. For each fixed input, the probability, over CERTIFY's sampling, that it returns an invalid certificate is at most α; the paper uses α = 0.001. Equivalently, with probability at least 1 − α it either abstains or returns a valid certificate. This is not an error bound conditional on CERTIFY not abstaining. If no class clearly leads, CERTIFY abstains. And the certificate says the prediction is stable, not that it is correct.

Key takeaway: A clear vote gives a provable ℓ2 radius for g, with statistical confidence; stability, not correctness.

The figure

Fig. 3 of Cohen et al.: Gaussian noise around x and x + δ (circles), with blue/red regions of two base classifiers; right, the worst-case f*, a linear classifier whose boundary is normal to δ.

Setup, model, date

Theorem 1: R = σ/2 (Φ⁻¹(p_A) − Φ⁻¹(p_B)); CERTIFY (§3.2). Formula and proof sketch in lec08tech.html.

What this establishes

For each fixed input, with probability ≥ 1 − α over CERTIFY's sampling, it either abstains or returns (c_A, R) such that g predicts c_A everywhere within ℓ2 radius R (Prop. 2).

What this does not establish

That c_A is the correct label; anything about the base classifier f; ℓ∞ robustness beyond what the ℓ2 radius implies; inputs where CERTIFY abstains.

Start with text classifiers. A sentiment model reads a movie review: "The characters, cast in impossibly contrived situations, are totally estranged from reality." It says negative, correctly. Swap three words for near-synonyms: "engineered circumstances", "fully". To us the meaning is the same; the model now says positive.

A separate experiment, not the movie-review model: against BERT on sentence pairs (SNLI), accuracy fell from 89.4% to 4.0% with 18.5% of words changed.

Key takeaway: A few synonym swaps can flip a text classifier.

93. Text Is Discrete: "Small" Means Few Words

LLMs · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈83.5 min

Text differs from images in one way that matters. Pixels change by tiny amounts; words cannot. So "small" means a few words swapped with the meaning kept, and the attack searches over word choices rather than taking gradient steps. Jin and colleagues checked with human judges: mostly preserved meaning, not always. Labels people gave the original and adversarial texts agreed 92% of the time on MR and 85% on SNLI; separately, judges rated meaning similarity 0.91 on MR and 0.86 on SNLI.

The question is unchanged: does a change that should not matter flip the answer?

Key takeaway: For text, "small" means few words, meaning preserved.

94. A Nonsense Suffix Jailbreaks Chatbots

LLMs · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈85 min

Now chatbots. Zou and colleagues searched for a suffix: odd tokens appended to a harmful request that make an aligned model answer instead of refusing. The method is GCG, Greedy Coordinate Gradient.

Top of the figure: the suffix is optimized on open models, Vicuna-7B and 13B. Bottom: the same suffix sent to closed chatbots, which began answering. The authors hide the actual suffix as "ADV PROMPT" and truncate the replies.

This is transfer again: crafted on open models, effective on closed ones, as with images in Section 04.

95. Images Can Jailbreak Multimodal Models

LLMs · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈86 min

Models that see images and text open the old image attack again. The attacker edits an image, with white-box gradients, to make the model output chosen toxic text.

Carlini and colleagues report 100% success on LLaVA, MiniGPT-4 and LLaMA-Adapter in their test. Note that the image changes were not limited in size; the point is that the output is reachable, not that the attack is stealthy.

Key takeaway: An optimized image can make a multimodal model say chosen text.

96. Prompt Injection: Instructions Hidden in Data

LLMs · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈87 min

A different LLM weak surface. An AI assistant is asked to summarize a web page. The page hides an instruction: "ignore the user, do this instead". The assistant follows the page, not the user.

For an LLM, the "input" is any text the model reads, including data it fetches. This is an illustrative example; it is not a tiny change, but it is the same weak surface: the attacker controls an input the model trusts too much.

Key takeaway: Any text an LLM reads can carry an attacker's instructions.

98. Four Questions for Any Robustness Claim

Wrap-up · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈88.5 min

Here is the checklist. One: robust to what changes? Norm, ε, digital or physical. Two: against which attacker? What they know, what they want, and was the attack adaptive. Three: measured how? Empirical or certified, and on which date. Four: at what cost? Clean accuracy, compute, abstentions.

A claim missing any answer is incomplete, not necessarily wrong. Ask for the missing piece.

Key takeaway: What changes, which attacker, measured how, at what cost.

99. Established vs Open

Wrap-up · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈89 min

What is established: tiny changes fool accurate models; weak attacks overstate robustness; adversarial training helps, partly; certificates exist for small ℓ2 balls.

What is open: one agreed cause; robustness across all threat models at once; large certified radii at scale; and robustness with no accuracy cost.

Key takeaway: Real phenomena and partial defenses; open questions remain.

100. Takeaways

Wrap-up · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈89.5 min

Six points. Models rely on other cues than we do, so robustness is the goal. Accuracy is average case; attackers pick the worst. A robustness claim needs a threat model. Report the strongest, adaptive attack. Benchmarks standardize; they do not guarantee. Certificates prove small regions, at a cost.

And the question to carry: robust to what, against whom, measured how, at what cost?

Key takeaway: Robust to what, against whom, measured how, at what cost?
