The RLHF Objective

Tune the policy $\pi$ to maximize reward, minus a leash to the base model.

$$\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi}\big[\,r(x,y)\,\big] \;-\; \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big).$$

$r$ = reward model; $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ = the model before RL; $\beta$ = leash strength.

Ouyang et al., "Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback" (InstructGPT), NeurIPS 2022.

Reading the Two Terms

Reward term

Pulls toward replies humans rated highly.

KL leash

Penalizes drifting far from the base model.

Small $\beta$: chase the score, risk reward hacking. Large $\beta$: stay safe, learn less.

Why This Matters for Safety

Refusal is whatever the reward model rewarded, not a hard rule.

  • The leash keeps most old behavior intact
  • Safety is a thin adjustment on top
  • Out-of-distribution prompts fall outside what $r$ saw

This is why alignment is "skin-deep".

Jailbreak as Search

The GCG objective, one gradient at a time

The Attack Objective

Find a suffix $s$ that makes a compliant opening most likely.

$$\min_{s}\; -\log \Pr\big[\,\text{"Sure, here"} \mid \text{prompt} \oplus s\,\big].$$

$\oplus$ = append the suffix; minimize the loss on the target opening.

Zou et al., "Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models" (GCG), 2023.

Why an Affirmative Target

Force the first few tokens, and the rest tends to follow.

  • Refusal usually lives in the opening words
  • Past "Sure, here", the model keeps generating
  • One short target stands in for the whole harmful answer

Steer the start; the completion comes along for the ride.

The Discrete Problem

Tokens are discrete: you cannot take a smooth gradient step.

  • Each suffix position holds one of ~50,000 tokens
  • The space is combinatorial, not continuous
  • Brute force over all swaps is hopeless

Greedy Coordinate Gradient makes the search tractable.

Gradient-Guided Token Swaps

  1. At each suffix position, read the loss gradient over the vocabulary.
  2. Shortlist the tokens the gradient says would help most.
  3. Actually evaluate the loss for those few candidates.
  4. Keep the single swap that lowers the loss the most; repeat.

Gradient ranks candidates; a real forward pass picks the winner.

Zou et al., "Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models" (GCG), 2023.

Same Idea as Pixels

GCG is the text version of an adversarial example.

Vision

Nudge pixels to cross a decision boundary.

Language

Swap tokens to cross the refusal boundary.

Continuous for images, discrete for text, same optimization spirit.
