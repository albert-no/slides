Slide 1Title ~0:30

What Does It Mean for a Model to Forget?

Open with the framing of the whole two-part lecture. The title is a question on purpose: the rest of the course is an attempt to give it a precise answer. Part I is everything we knew about unlearning before large language models — clean definitions, a fully certified deletion procedure, and the classification algorithms and metrics that the field cut its teeth on.

"For the next two lectures we ask one question — what does it actually mean for a trained model to forget a piece of its training data? Today, Part I: the foundations, where the math is clean and we can even prove things. Part II takes the same question to LLMs, where almost everything breaks."

Set the expectation early: by the end of today they should be able to define unlearning rigorously, derive the Newton-step deletion method from scratch, and read an unlearning evaluation table critically.

Slide 3Section divider 01 ~0:10

01 — Motivation and Definition

From the right to be forgotten to certified deletion

Just a breath. Name the arc of the section: we start with a legal right ("be forgotten") and end with a mathematical guarantee ("certified deletion"). The whole point is to turn a fuzzy social demand into something a theorem can address.

Slide 4Core setup ~2:00

The Central Question

This is the definitional anchor for everything. We have a trained model $\theta_o = A(\mathcal{D})$ produced by a (possibly randomized) learning algorithm $A$ on dataset $\mathcal{D}$. Someone asks us to remove a forget set $\mathcal{D}_f \subset \mathcal{D}$. We must produce $\theta_u$ that behaves as if $\mathcal{D}_f$ had never been in the training set.

Two things to hammer:

  • Cheaper than retraining. The trivial correct answer is $A(\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_f)$ — retrain from scratch. Unlearning only earns its name if it is meaningfully cheaper than that.
  • Match in distribution, not outputs. This is the subtle and most important point on the slide. $A$ is randomized (initialization, SGD order). So "looks like it never saw $\mathcal{D}_f$" cannot mean "identical weights" — it means the distribution over $\theta_u$ matches the distribution over a freshly retrained model. Hiding one output is not forgetting.

"The target is not a number, it's a distribution. We want $\theta_u$ to be a plausible draw from the retraining process — indistinguishable from a model that genuinely never saw the forget data."

"Why not just delete the data and keep the model?" — Because the model is a lossy function of the data; the information about $\mathcal{D}_f$ is baked into the weights. Deleting the row in the database does nothing to the weights.

Slides 5–7Motivation ~2:30

Why This Became Urgent

Three forces converging make this a 2020s problem, not a 2010s one.

  • Lawsuits. NYT v. OpenAI, Disney v. Midjourney, Getty v. Stability AI. Plaintiffs argue their copyrighted data is inside the model and want it out. "Just retrain" is not a satisfying legal remedy.
  • GDPR Article 17 — the "right to erasure." A data subject can demand deletion, and the controller has roughly one month. If the data lives in model weights, compliance is a technical unlearning problem.
  • Memorization. The empirical fact that makes this real: Carlini et al. extracted verbatim training text and PII from production LLMs. The data really is recoverable, so the right to be forgotten really is being violated.

"Unlearning is the technical answer to what is fundamentally a legal and regulatory question. The lawyers showed up, the regulators wrote Article 17, and Carlini showed the data is genuinely extractable — so now we need the math."

Carlini 2021 (USENIX) and Cooper 2025 are the cites; Cooper comes back hard in Part II as the skeptical voice.

Slide 8Baseline ~2:00

The Gold Standard: Retrain From Scratch

Establish the gold standard explicitly so the rest of the lecture has a reference. Retrain on $\mathcal{D}_r = \mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_f$. By construction this is exactly correct — it is what "never saw $\mathcal{D}_f$" literally means.

  • It gives exact unlearning for free.
  • Its cost is a full training run — fine for logistic regression, catastrophic for a foundation model.
  • Every method in this lecture is an approximation to this one baseline.

"Keep retrain in your head as the answer key. The entire field is: how close can I get to retrain, how much cheaper, and how do I prove I got close?"

Foreshadow Part II: at LLM scale even this answer key is unavailable — you cannot afford to retrain to grade yourself, which is why LLM evaluation is so much harder.

Slides 9–10Definition 1 · Ginart 2019 ~2:30

Definition 1 — Data Deletion Operation

The first formal definition. $R_A(\mathcal{D}, A(\mathcal{D}), i)$ is a deletion operation if for every dataset and every index,

$$A(\mathcal{D}_{-i}) \;=_d\; R_A(\mathcal{D}, A(\mathcal{D}), i).$$

Read it slowly. The left side is "train fresh without point $i$." The right side is "run the deletion operator $R$ on the already-trained model." The $=_d$ is equality in distribution.

  • $R$ gets to see the trained model $A(\mathcal{D})$ and the point to remove — that is its whole job, to be a cheap shortcut.
  • Equality in distribution, not on a sample path: re-emphasize slide 4's point. Two random processes; we ask their laws to coincide, not their realizations.

"Why index $i$ and not a set?" — Single-point deletion is the clean case; sets follow by the same machinery (and Sekhari's capacity result, slides 50–55, handles $m$ deletions). Start with one.

Slide 11Definition 2 ~1:30

Definition 2 — Exact Unlearning

Two regimes split the whole field.

  • Exact: the distributions match exactly, $A(\mathcal{D}_r) =_d \mathcal{U}(\cdot)$. Achievable by retraining or by clever architectures — SISA (slides 58–65) is the canonical trick, structuring training so you only retrain a small shard.
  • Approximate: the distributions are merely $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-close. We borrow the indistinguishability notion straight from differential privacy. This is the only realistic option for deep nets, and it requires careful auditing because "close" can hide leakage.

"Exact unlearning is an architecture problem — you design training so deletion is cheap. Approximate unlearning is an analysis problem — you take an arbitrary model and prove you got close enough."

Slide 12Definition 3 · certified ~2:30

Definition 3 — $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-Certified Unlearning

Make the approximate notion precise. $\mathcal{U}$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-certified if for every measurable set of outcomes $T$,

$$\Pr[\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}_f,A(\mathcal{D})) \in T] \;\le\; e^\varepsilon\,\Pr[A(\mathcal{D}_r)\in T] + \delta,$$

and symmetrically with the two sides swapped.

This is literally the $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP inequality, with "neighboring datasets" replaced by "unlearned vs retrained." If they have seen DP, this slide is one sentence: same definition, different pair of distributions.

  • $\varepsilon$ controls the multiplicative gap, $\delta$ the additive slack (a small failure probability).
  • The payoff: all the DP machinery — composition, the Gaussian mechanism — transfers. We use the Gaussian mechanism on slides 44–46.

"If you remember one thing from DP, it's this inequality. Certified unlearning is the same inequality with retrain on the right-hand side."

Slides 13–19Propositions 1–2 · relations ~4:30

How the Three Definitions Relate

Six slides that do nothing but relate the three definitions to one another. Students arrive assuming "certified" is a weaker "exact" and that both are versions of DP. Only the first half of that is true, and the correction is worth the time.

  • Proposition 1 (slide 14) — exact implies certified. Take $\varepsilon=\delta=0$ in Definition 3 and the two inequalities collapse to equality of laws. One line, but it establishes that the definitions are nested, not parallel.
  • Proposition 2 (slide 15) — DP certifies for free. If $A$ is itself $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP with respect to one-point changes, then the do-nothing map $\mathcal{U}=\mathrm{id}$ is already $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-certified: $\mathcal{D}$ and $\mathcal{D}_r$ are neighbours, so the DP inequality is the certification inequality.
  • Counterexample (slide 16). Proposition 2 should feel like a warning, not a gift. A mechanism that ignores its input and returns a fixed $\theta_0$ is $(0,0)$-certified and completely useless.

Board version of the counterexample: let $\mathcal{U}(\cdot)\equiv\theta_0$ and let the reference also be $\theta_0$. Both laws are the point mass $\delta_{\theta_0}$, so certification holds with $\varepsilon=\delta=0$ — at zero accuracy. Certification constrains the gap to retrain and never the quality of what you release.

Slides 17–19 draw the consequences: what a certificate does and does not buy; why certified unlearning is not DP (DP quantifies over neighbouring dataset pairs, certification over one dataset and one deletion request — neither implies the other in the direction people assume); and the corollary that a certificate must always be reported next to a utility number.

"A certificate says 'this is close to retrain.' It says nothing about whether retrain was any good, and nothing about whether you kept any accuracy. Never quote an $\varepsilon$ without a utility number beside it."

"So is DP just strictly stronger?" — In this one direction yes, DP training hands you certification for free. But it pays up front, on every point, forever; unlearning pays per request. Slide 49 makes that cost comparison properly.

Slides 20–21Bookkeeping ~1:30

Standing Convention and the $1/n$ Choice

Two bookkeeping slides that prevent an hour of confused questions later. Move briskly, but do not skip them.

  • Standing convention (slide 20). Fix the notation once and point at it whenever someone drifts: $n$ points; the forget set is a single point $z$ unless stated otherwise; $\theta_o$ is the original model, $\theta_{-z}$ the leave-one-out retrain, $\theta_u$ whatever we actually produce. Everything in §01 is stated for one deletion. Theorem 3 is the only place $m$ appears.
  • The $1/n$ versus $1/(n-1)$ choice (slide 21). Removing $z$ from an average of $n$ terms leaves $\tfrac1n\sum_{i\ne z}\ell$, which is $\tfrac{n-1}{n}$ times the true leave-one-out objective.
$$\tfrac1n\sum_{i\neq z}\ell(\theta;z_i) \;=\; \tfrac{n-1}{n}\cdot\tfrac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i\neq z}\ell(\theta;z_i).$$

A positive constant times the leave-one-out loss, so the unregularised minimisers coincide exactly. With an $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2$ term the two conventions do differ, at order $1/n$ in the effective regularisation weight — one order below the $1/n^2$ residual we later hide with noise, so it is absorbed.

"I am going to write $1/n$ throughout. If the missing $n-1$ bothers you, slide 21 is written for you: the difference is one order below everything we are about to prove."

Slides 22–23Assumptions ~2:00

Assumptions for Section 01

Put the hypotheses on the table before the theorem, so that nobody can later accuse the result of being magic — and so that §02 has something concrete to violate. Three assumptions carry all of §01, and they are numbered on the slide:

  • (A1) bounded gradients. $\|\nabla_\theta\ell(\theta;z)\|\le G$ for every $\theta$ and every $z$.
  • (A2) strong convexity. Each $\ell(\cdot;z)$ is convex, so $F(\cdot;S)$ is $\lambda$-strongly convex for every $S$ — courtesy of the regulariser, which is where $\lambda$ actually comes from.
  • (A3) Hessian smoothness. $\theta\mapsto\nabla^2F(\theta;S)$ is $M$-Lipschitz in operator norm.

One line each on what they do: A1 bounds how hard a single point can pull; A2 makes the Hessian invertible and the optimum stable; A3 bounds the curvature error of one Newton step.

Slide 23 spends its entire budget on what (A2) buys, because it is used twice and the two uses get conflated:

  • Invertibility. $H\succeq\lambda I$ gives $\|H^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le 1/\lambda$, so the Newton step is well defined and bounded.
  • Optimum stability. $\lambda\|\theta-\theta'\|\le\|\nabla F(\theta)-\nabla F(\theta')\|$ converts a small gradient perturbation into a small movement of the minimiser. That is Lemma 1.

"Strong convexity is doing two different jobs. Once to make $H^{-1}$ exist, once to say the optimum cannot run away. Keep them separate — the proof uses them in different places."

"Where does A3 get used?" — Only in Lemma 2, to bound the curvature gap between $\theta_o$ and $\theta_{-z}$. It is the assumption that turns Newton's local quadratic convergence into an explicit constant instead of an asymptotic statement.

Slide 24Newton derivation 1/4 ~1:30

Can We Reach $\theta_{-z}$ Without Retraining?

Set the stage. Empirical loss $L(\theta;\mathcal{D}) = \tfrac1n\sum_i \ell(\theta;z_i)$, assumed twice differentiable and $\lambda$-strongly convex. Exact deletion of $z$ means landing on the leave-one-out optimum $\theta_{-z} = \arg\min_\theta L(\theta;\mathcal{D}\setminus\{z\})$.

Two consequences of strong convexity, both load-bearing later:

  • $H_\theta = \nabla^2 L \succeq \lambda I$ is invertible with $\|H_\theta^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}} \le 1/\lambda$ — so the $H^{-1}$ in the influence formula is well-defined and bounded.
  • Removing 1 of $n$ points perturbs the loss by $O(1/n)$, so it moves the optimum only $O(1/n)$ — a small, correctable nudge.

"The optimum barely moves — it's $1/n$ away. That's the whole reason a single analytic step can work: we don't need to travel far, we just need to travel in exactly the right direction."

"What if the loss isn't strongly convex?" — Then add an $\ell_2$ regularizer to make it so (standard), or the guarantees become approximate. Deep nets violate this; that's why the deep-net methods in §02 are heuristic.

Slide 25Newton derivation 2/4 ~2:30

Down-Weighting the Point Continuously

The trick that makes the derivation clean: don't jump from "with $z$" to "without $z$" — build a continuous dial $t\in[0,1]$ that smoothly removes $z$.

$$L_t(\theta) = L(\theta;\mathcal{D}) - \tfrac{t}{n}\,\ell(\theta;z),\qquad \theta(t)=\arg\min_\theta L_t(\theta).$$
  • At $t=0$: $L_0 = L(\cdot;\mathcal{D})$, so $\theta(0)=\theta_o$ — the original model.
  • At $t=1$: the $z$ term is fully removed, $L_1 \propto L(\cdot;\mathcal{D}\setminus\{z\})$, so $\theta(1)=\theta_{-z}$ — the retrain target. (Proportional, not equal, because of the $1/n$ vs $1/(n{-}1)$ normalization — wave at this; it's a lower-order effect.)

So the path $\theta(t)$ literally interpolates from the model we have to the model we want. The next slide differentiates it.

"Instead of teleporting from $\theta_o$ to $\theta_{-z}$, we walk a smooth path as we turn the weight on $z$ down from one to zero — and then we just need the velocity at the start."

Slides 26–28Newton derivation 3/4 ~3:00

Differentiating the Optimality Condition

The heart of the derivation. Along the path, $\theta(t)$ is a minimizer, so stationarity holds for every $t$:

$$\nabla_\theta L_t(\theta(t)) = \nabla L(\theta(t);\mathcal{D}) - \tfrac{t}{n}\nabla\ell(\theta(t);z) = 0.$$

Now differentiate this identity in $t$ (implicit function theorem) and evaluate at $t=0$, where $\nabla^2 L_0(\theta_o)=H_{\theta_o}$:

$$H_{\theta_o}\,\theta'(0) - \tfrac1n\nabla\ell(\theta_o;z) = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \theta'(0)=\tfrac1n H_{\theta_o}^{-1}\nabla\ell(\theta_o;z).$$

Walk the chain rule explicitly: differentiating the first term gives $\nabla^2 L\cdot\theta'(t)$; differentiating the second gives $-\tfrac1n\nabla\ell - \tfrac{t}{n}(\ldots)\theta'$, and at $t=0$ the last piece vanishes. The velocity at the start of the path is exactly the influence direction. Slide 29 turns it into an update.

"We never solved the optimization — we differentiated the optimality condition. That's the implicit function theorem doing all the work. The rate the optimum moves as $z$ leaves is the influence direction."

Slides 29–30Newton derivation 4/4 ~3:00

Influence Unlearning: The Update

The derivation lands here. Integrating the velocity from the previous three slides over the unit step $\Delta t = 1$ gives the update; equivalently, to first order the optimum moves by

$$\theta_{-z} - \theta_o \;\approx\; \frac{1}{n}\,H_{\theta_o}^{-1}\,\nabla_\theta\ell(\theta_o;z),\qquad H_{\theta_o}=\nabla^2 L(\theta_o).$$

This shift is the influence function (Koh & Liang 2017), and used directly it is the IU (Influence Unlearning) method.

Sign note — say this out loud: the sign is $+\tfrac1n H^{-1}\nabla\ell$. We are removing $z$, so we move the optimum in the direction that adding $z$ pulled us away from — i.e., we undo $z$'s pull. (This sign was an audit fix; the deck is now correct. If a student has Koh & Liang's "up-weighting" form in mind, theirs has the opposite sign because they up-weight rather than remove.)

  • Exact for quadratic loss; first-order Taylor otherwise.
  • In deep nets you never form $H^{-1}$ — you use Hessian-vector products / conjugate gradients.

"The influence function answers 'where would the optimum have been without $z$?' and it has a closed form. We earned that formula honestly over the last three slides — and slide 30 shows it is exactly one Newton step on the leave-one-out objective."

Slide 31Theorem 1 · Guo 2020 ~2:00

Theorem 1 — One Newton Step Suffices

Cash in the derivation. A first-order Taylor expansion of the path, $\theta_{-z}-\theta_o = \theta(1)-\theta(0)\approx\theta'(0)$, gives the single Newton step — and the theorem says how good it is:

$$\theta_u = \theta_o + \tfrac1n H_{\theta_o}^{-1}\nabla_\theta\ell(\theta_o;z), \qquad \|\theta_u-\theta_{-z}\| \;\le\; \frac{M G^2}{2\lambda^3 n^2}.$$

Three remarks before the proof:

  • The rate is $1/n^2$, not $1/n$. That gap is the entire economic case for certified deletion, and slide 47 is where it pays off.
  • Every constant is one of the four assumptions: $M$ from the Lipschitz Hessian, $G$ from the Lipschitz loss, $\lambda^3$ from three separate uses of strong convexity.
  • It is a deterministic bound. No probability has entered yet; randomness arrives only with the noise in Theorem 2.

Attribution, and say it out loud: this is the Guo et al. (ICML 2020) removal mechanism, but their certification is by objective perturbation, not by the output-noise argument we are about to give. Slide 46 is explicit about the difference, and it matters the moment a student opens the paper.

"One closed-form update — a Hessian inverse times a gradient — moves the model along the deleted point's influence direction and lands within $1/n^2$ of retrain. No training loop, and a bound you can write down."

Slides 32–37Proof · Lemmas 1–2 ~5:00

Proof of Theorem 1 — Two Lemmas

Six slides of proof. Do not rush them: this is the only fully proved theorem in the lecture, and the structure matters more than the constants.

The shape of the argument. Two lemmas, then one line of composition:

  • Lemma 1 (slide 33) — we start close. $\|\theta_o-\theta_{-z}\|\le G/(\lambda n)$. Proof: $\theta_o$ zeroes the full gradient, so its gradient on the leave-one-out objective is exactly the one omitted term, of norm $\le G/n$; $\lambda$-strong convexity converts a gradient into a distance by dividing by $\lambda$.
  • Lemma 2 (slides 34–36) — Newton squares the error. From a point at distance $\|e\|$ from the optimum, one Newton step lands within $(M/2\lambda)\|e\|^2$.
  • Composition (slide 37). Substitute Lemma 1 into Lemma 2: $(M/2\lambda)\,(G/\lambda n)^2 = MG^2/(2\lambda^3 n^2)$.

The identity at the heart of Lemma 2, worth writing out in full. With $e=\theta-\theta^\star$ and $H=\nabla^2 F(\theta)$, using $\nabla F(\theta)=\int_0^1\nabla^2F(\theta^\star+se)\,e\,ds$,

$$\theta^+ - \theta^\star \;=\; H^{-1}\!\int_0^1\!\big(H - \nabla^2F(\theta^\star+se)\big)\,e\;ds .$$

Bound the integrand: $\|H-\nabla^2F(\theta^\star+se)\|\le M(1-s)\|e\|$ by the Lipschitz Hessian, and $\int_0^1(1-s)\,ds=\tfrac12$. Divide by $\lambda$ for $\|H^{-1}\|$, and the result is $(M/2\lambda)\|e\|^2$. Nothing beyond the fundamental theorem of calculus and a norm bound.

Make the moral explicit: start $1/n$ away, square the error, land at $1/n^2$. Quadratic convergence is normally a footnote about the tail of an optimization run; here it is the whole result, because we get to choose the starting point and it is already close.

"Newton's quadratic convergence is usually a remark about the end of a run. We are using it at the beginning — removing one point out of $n$ leaves us $1/n$ from the answer, and squaring that finishes the job in a single step."

"Would a gradient step do?" — No. A gradient step contracts the error by a constant factor, so it stays $\Theta(1/n)$ away no matter how many you take within a fixed budget. Only curvature turns $1/n$ into $1/n^2$, and that factor of $n$ is the entire result.

Slides 38–40Fine print ~2:30

The Picture, the Hessian, and Never Forming $H^{-1}$

Three fine-print slides that head off the two questions this derivation always draws.

  • The picture (slide 38). Narrate the parameter-space figure: $\theta_o$, the leave-one-out path curving away to $\theta_{-z}$, the straight Newton step taken from $\theta_o$, and the small residual arc between the step's endpoint and $\theta_{-z}$. Lemma 1 is the length of the path; Lemma 2 is the length of the residual. Two lemmas, two segments of one drawing.
  • Which Hessian? (slide 39). The honest version of the update uses $\nabla^2 F(\theta_o;\mathcal{D}\setminus\{z\})$, but every implementation uses the full-data $H_{\theta_o}$, because that is the one you already have. The discrepancy is $O(1/n^2)$ — the same order as the residual we are about to drown in noise, so it costs a constant and nothing more.
  • You never form $H^{-1}$ (slide 40). Explicit $d\times d$ inversion is $O(d^3)$ and hopeless past a few thousand parameters. In practice: conjugate gradients on $Hv=g$ using Hessian-vector products, or a truncated Neumann series in the LiSSA style. This is the slide that connects a theorem to a line of PyTorch.

"Two questions always land here — 'which Hessian did you mean' and 'you cannot possibly invert that.' Both have honest answers, and neither one damages the theorem."

Slides 41–43Lemma 3 · sensitivity ~2:30

Lemma 3 — $\ell_2$ Sensitivity

To certify with the Gaussian mechanism we need the sensitivity — how much the released parameter can move when the deleted point changes. For two candidate deleted points $z,z'$:

$$\theta_u(\mathcal{D},z)-\theta_u(\mathcal{D},z') = \tfrac1n H_{\theta_o}^{-1}\big(\nabla\ell(\theta_o;z)-\nabla\ell(\theta_o;z')\big).$$

With $G$-Lipschitz loss ($\|\nabla\ell\|\le G$) and $\|H^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le 1/\lambda$, take norms and use the triangle inequality:

$$\sup_{z,z'}\big\|\theta_u(\mathcal{D},z)-\theta_u(\mathcal{D},z')\big\| \;\le\; \frac{2G}{\lambda n}.$$

The factor 2 is the two gradient terms; the $1/\lambda$ is the Hessian inverse; the $1/n$ is the per-point weight. This $2G/(\lambda n)$ is the first-order sensitivity a privacy mechanism would otherwise have to mask.

"Why bound over $z,z'$ rather than the residual?" — Because certification is about indistinguishability across which point was deleted; the relevant quantity is how much the output can swing. (The residual gap drives the noise scale on the next slide.)

Slides 44–46Theorem 2 · Gaussian ~3:00

Theorem 2 — Certified Deletion by Output Noise

The capstone. We have a deterministic residual $\eta \le MG^2/(2\lambda^3 n^2)$ between the one-step model and the true leave-one-out retrain. Release $\theta_u+N$ with $N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)$ and

$$\sigma \;\ge\; \frac{\eta}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{2\log(1.25/\delta)}, \qquad \eta \;=\; \frac{MG^2}{2\lambda^3 n^2}.$$

Slide 44 is a pure recall card for the Gaussian mechanism — do not re-derive it, they own it already. The only new content is the reduction: the two laws we must make indistinguishable are $\theta_u+N$ and $\theta_{-z}+N$, two Gaussians whose means differ by at most $\eta$. That is verbatim the Gaussian mechanism with the $\ell_2$ sensitivity $\Delta_2$ replaced by $\eta$.

Slide 46 is the honesty slide and I would not skip it. Guo et al. do not prove it this way. Their certification is by objective perturbation — noise injected into the training objective before optimization, so the perturbed optimum is itself randomized — together with a data-dependent bound on the gradient residual $\|\nabla F(\theta_u;\mathcal{D}_r)\|$, which is what makes their guarantee auditable at run time. The output-noise argument we give is the clean pedagogical version, and it is genuinely a theorem; it is just not their theorem.

"We do not hide the model. We hide the tiny gap between our one-step approximation and true retrain. Small gap, small noise — which is exactly why Theorem 1 had to be a $1/n^2$ bound and not a $1/n$ bound."

"Does the noise cost accuracy?" — At $\sigma\propto 1/n^2$, negligibly for any realistic $n$. That comparison is slides 47–49.

Slides 47–49Punchline ~2:30

The Punchline: $1/n^2$ Against $1/n$

The intuitive payoff — why deletion is genuinely easier than full privacy.

  • Full DP of the model must mask every point's $O(1/n)$ influence, so $\sigma_{\mathrm{DP}}\propto 1/(n\varepsilon)$.
  • Newton unlearning already removed the first-order influence analytically; only the $O(1/n^2)$ residual remains, so $\sigma\propto 1/(n^2\varepsilon)$.

An extra factor of $n$ less noise. For large $n$ that is a big deal — certified deletion is far cheaper in utility than training privately from the start.

"DP pays to hide each point forever. Unlearning only pays to hide the leftover after we've already done the work analytically — one factor of $n$ cheaper. Deletion is the easier task."

"Then why not always DP-train?" — DP protects all points always; unlearning targets a specific request after the fact. Different problems; the comparison is about the cost of the deletion guarantee specifically.

Slides 50–55Theorem 3 · Sekhari 2021 ~3:30

Theorem 3 — Deletion Capacity

Scale from one deletion to $m$, and the problem changes character: deletions become a consumable. For $\lambda$-strongly convex, $L$-Lipschitz, $M$-Hessian-Lipschitz losses in $d$ dimensions, Sekhari et al. bound the excess population risk after $m$ deletions by

$$O\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}\,M m^2 L^3\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)}}{\lambda^3 n^2\varepsilon}\right) \;+\; \frac{4mL^2}{\lambda n}.$$

Read the terms in the other order. The second is the statistical price of holding less data — you pay it even with a perfect unlearner. The first is the price of deletion: the accumulated Newton residual at perturbation size $m/n$, plus the Gaussian noise that hides it, priced across $d$ dimensions.

Deletion capacity is what you get by holding the sum at a constant and solving for $m$:

$$m^{\mathcal{A},\bar{\mathcal{A}}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(d,n) \;\ge\; c\,\frac{n\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\bigl(d\log(1/\delta)\bigr)^{1/4}}.$$

Get this form right on the board. It is $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ upstairs and $d$ under a fourth root: the $\sqrt d$ cost of noise passes through the square-root solve and comes out as $d^{1/4}$. An earlier draft of these notes carried a linear-in-$\varepsilon$, $\sqrt d$ version of the statement — that was wrong. This is the paper's Theorem 2 (Sekhari, Acharya, Kamath, Suresh, NeurIPS 2021).

Slide 55 is the payoff. Routing deletion through DP alone — train privately, then do nothing — gives capacity $\tilde\Omega\big(n\varepsilon/\sqrt{d\log(e^\varepsilon/\delta)}\big)$. So unlearning is quadratically better in the dimension: $n/d^{1/4}$ against $n/d^{1/2}$. The separation requires actually using the deleted samples; an algorithm that discards them is stuck at $\sqrt d$. And there is a ceiling — no algorithm exceeds capacity $cn$ with $c \lt 1$.

"You do not get infinite free deletions. There is a budget, it is about $n\sqrt\varepsilon$ over $d$ to the one-fourth, and when it runs out the only honest move left is to retrain."

"Why does dimension hurt so much?" — Because the noise that hides the residual lives in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and its cost in excess risk scales like $\sigma\sqrt d$. Higher dimension, more noise mass, fewer deletions before the risk budget is gone.

Slides 56–57Honesty check ~2:30

When Influence Functions Fail

The honesty pair of §01, and the hinge of the whole lecture. Everything proved so far leaned on (A2); deep networks satisfy none of it.

  • Basu, Pope & Feizi (ICLR 2021) compared influence estimates against actual leave-one-out retraining on deep nets. The agreement is often weak, degrades sharply with depth, and depends on weight decay, architecture, and which test point you ask about.
  • This is not a bug in the estimator. The premises are false: the Hessian is indefinite, so $H^{-1}$ may not exist and the damping $H+\alpha I$ you add to fix that changes the answer; and there is no unique $\theta_{-z}$ to approximate, because retraining lands in a different basin each run.
  • Slide 57 then asks the fair follow-up — can certification reach deep networks at all? — and answers it the way the literature does: only by weakening the claim. Freeze the extractor and unlearn in the last layer; certify around a fixed initialisation where the explored region is near-convex; or relax the target to a Hessian-based distance instead of "distributed as retrain". Each buys a theorem by shrinking what is claimed, and none certifies end-to-end training.

"Do not let the theorem from twenty minutes ago follow you into section two. The moment convexity goes, the influence function stops being an approximation to anything — it becomes one more heuristic update direction."

"Then why teach the convex theory at all?" — Because it is the only place where we can say precisely what unlearning means and prove that something achieves it. Every deep-net method in §02 is imitating the object we defined here, without the ability to certify it.

Slides 58–65Exact unlearning · SISA ~4:00

SISA and Proposition 3 — The Cost Model

The other route to exact unlearning: change the training procedure so that deletion is local by construction. Sharded, Isolated, Sliced, Aggregated.

  • Partition $\mathcal{D}$ into $S$ disjoint shards; train one constituent model per shard, in isolation.
  • Split each shard into $R$ slices; train incrementally, checkpointing after each slice.
  • Aggregate the $S$ predictions at inference (vote or average).
  • To delete $z$: roll back to the checkpoint before its slice and resume from there.

Why this is exact and not approximate: the other $S-1$ constituents are functions of data that never contained $z$, so the ensemble's law is exactly the law of an ensemble trained without $z$. No $\varepsilon$, no $\delta$.

Proposition 3 (slides 60–63) makes the cost claim quantitative, counting sample-gradient computations:

(i) Shards only. Deleting a uniformly random point costs $n/S$ — a speedup of exactly $S$, whichever shard is hit.

(ii) Shards and slices. Step $j$ within a shard trains on slices $1,\dots,j$ at cost $jn/(SR)$, so deleting a point in slice $r$ replays steps $r,\dots,R$:

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{cost}] \;=\; \frac{n}{SR^2}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{j=r}^{R} j \;=\; \frac{n(R+1)(2R+1)}{6SR}.$$

The double sum is the only trick: swap the order of summation, the pair $(r,j)$ contributes exactly when $r\le j$, so $j$ is counted $j$ times and $\sum_j j^2 = R(R+1)(2R+1)/6$.

Reading it (slide 64) is where the lesson lives. Incremental training of the shard itself costs $n(R+1)/(2S)$, so slicing saves a factor $3R/(2R+1)\to 3/2$. Therefore: sharding buys $S$, without limit; slicing buys at most $3/2$, ever. Students consistently over-rate slicing, and this is the slide that fixes it.

The catch (slide 65). Each constituent sees only $1/S$ of the data, so accuracy degrades as $S$ grows — the real design question is how large $S$ can be before the ensemble collapses. The cost model also assumes uniform deletion requests and equal-cost samples, neither of which survives a bursty or adversarial request stream. And none of it survives foundation-model scale: you cannot shard a trillion-token pretraining run this way.

"SISA is the clever exact answer: structure training so that deletion is local. The cost model tells you exactly what you bought — a factor $S$ — and exactly what you did not."

  • Three definitions, nested: exact $\Rightarrow$ certified, and neither one is DP.
  • Theorem 1 — one Newton step lands within $MG^2/(2\lambda^3n^2)$ of the leave-one-out retrain.
  • Theorem 2 — Gaussian noise at a scale set by that residual certifies the release.

Slide 67Section divider 02 ~0:10

02 — Classification: Algorithms

Approximate methods on deep nets

Transition: we leave the convex world where we could prove certification. Deep nets are non-convex, the Hessian is huge, and strong convexity fails — so these methods are heuristic approximations to retrain, evaluated empirically rather than certified. Set that expectation honestly.

Slides 68–70Catalog ~2:30

What Breaks, and the Catalog

Orient them with the menu before diving into specifics. Walk the table:

  • FT (fine-tune on retain only) — relies on catastrophic forgetting of $\mathcal{D}_f$. Cheap, weak.
  • GA (gradient ascent on forget) — reverse the gradient to "un-train." This is the method that explodes in Part II.
  • RL (random labels on forget) — teach the model nonsense for $\mathcal{D}_f$.
  • FF (Fisher forgetting) — add noise scaled by the diagonal Fisher information.
  • IU (influence unlearning) — the closed-form Newton shift from §01, applied without strong-convexity guarantees.
  • SCRUB — KL distillation, the next slide.

Plus the two we detail: SalUn (saliency-masked) and $\ell_1$-sparse. The taxonomy axis: most are a forget term plus a retain term; they differ in how each is realized.

Slides 71–72Geometry ~2:30

FT, GA, RL, FF, IU — Five Geometries

Two slides that give the catalog some geometry instead of leaving it a list of acronyms. Ask of each method: what is it minimising, and does that objective have a minimum?

  • FT — gradient descent on the retain loss only. The forget set is never mentioned; you are betting on catastrophic forgetting to do the work. Geometrically: drift, direction uncontrolled.
  • GA — gradient ascent on the forget loss. The objective is unbounded above, so the iteration has no fixed point and the only thing stopping it is the step budget. This is the method that detonates in Part II.
  • RL — fit random labels on $\mathcal{D}_f$. Bounded (it is still a cross-entropy) and targeted, but the target it aims at — near-uniform prediction — is not what retrain produces.
  • FF — add Gaussian noise with covariance set by the inverse diagonal Fisher: perturb hardest where the model is least certain. A one-shot parameter move, no optimisation loop.
  • IU — the §01 Newton step with the guarantees stripped off. Same formula, no theorem, and slide 56 is why.

The organising remark: every method here is a forget term plus a retain term, and they differ in whether the forget term is bounded, whether it is targeted, and whether it has a fixed point. GA fails all three, which is why it is both the simplest idea and the worst-behaved one.

"Gradient ascent has no minimum. Everything unpleasant that happens to it downstream follows from that one sentence."

Slides 73–75SCRUB ~2:30

SCRUB — Distillation With Two Signs

A teacher–student distillation. Teacher = original $\theta_o$ (it knows everything). Student = unlearned $\theta_u$. The objective is contrastive:

$$\min_{\theta_u}\; \frac{1}{N_r}\!\sum_{x_r\in\mathcal{D}_r} d(x_r;\theta_u) \;-\; \frac{1}{N_f}\!\sum_{x_f\in\mathcal{D}_f} d(x_f;\theta_u),$$

with $d(x;\theta_u) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(\cdot;\theta_o)\,\|\,p(\cdot;\theta_u)\big)$.

Two opposite pulls: minimize KL on the retain set (stay close to the teacher where we should remember) and maximize KL on the forget set (move away from the teacher where we should forget). The minus sign is the whole idea — "contrastive distillation."

"Doesn't maximizing KL blow up, like GA?" — Yes, in principle; SCRUB controls it with min/max alternation and early stopping. This instability is the through-line that NPO fixes in Part II.

Slides 76–78SalUn ~2:30

SalUn — Update Only Salient Weights

Idea: don't touch all the weights, only the ones that matter for the forget set. Build a saliency mask from the forget-loss gradient at $\theta_o$:

$$\mathbf{m}_S = \mathbb{1}\big(|\nabla_\theta \ell_f(\theta;\mathcal{D}_f)|_{\theta=\theta_o} \ge \gamma\big),\qquad \gamma = \text{median saliency}.$$ $$\theta_u = \mathbf{m}_S\odot(\Delta\theta+\theta_o) + (\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{m}_S)\odot\theta_o.$$

Read the second line: where the mask is 1 (salient weights), apply the unlearning update $\Delta\theta$; where it's 0, freeze at the original. So you surgically edit only the forget-relevant subnetwork and leave the rest of the model untouched, which protects utility.

  • $\gamma$ = median of saliency magnitudes — a clean, parameter-light threshold.
  • Paired with random-label loss as the inner $\Delta\theta$ for image classification.
  • ICLR 2024; strong on the benchmark table at the end of §03 (slides 105–106).
Slides 79–80$\ell_1$-sparse ~2:00

$\ell_1$-Sparse Unlearning

A one-line modification with an outsized effect: add an $\ell_1$ penalty during the unlearning fine-tune,

$$\theta_u = \arg\min_\theta\; L_u(\theta;\theta_o,\mathcal{D}_r) + \gamma\|\theta\|_1.$$
  • The $\ell_1$ term induces sparsity — pruning emerges from the unlearning step itself, no separate prune stage.
  • Empirical finding: sparser models forget more cleanly — fewer active weights means less redundant storage of the forget data.
  • It's a wrapper: $L_u$ can be FT, GA, or IU. Composable with the rest of the catalog.

"The surprising empirical message: make the model sparse and unlearning gets easier. Redundancy is what lets a model 'remember anyway' — kill the redundancy and forgetting sticks."

Slides 81–83RURK · failure mode ~2:30

Residual Knowledge Under Perturbation

First crack in the armor. A model can pass statistical indistinguishability on $\mathcal{D}_f$ yet still classify perturbed forget samples correctly — the knowledge is still there, just nudged off the exact test points.

$$L_{\text{RURK}}(\mathbf w,\mathcal S) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal S_r|}\sum \ell(\mathbf w;(x,y)) - \frac{\lambda}{|\mathcal S_f|}\sum \kappa(\mathbf w;(x,y)),$$

where $\kappa$ averages the loss over a neighborhood $\mathcal V((x,y),\tau)$ of each forget point.

The takeaway box is the message: statistical indistinguishability is not robust to local perturbation. If you only check the exact forget points, you over-credit the unlearner. This previews the entire skepticism thread of Part II (benign relearning, SEPS).

"Forgetting the exact data point is not the same as forgetting the concept. Jiggle the input and the knowledge comes right back."

Slide 84Verification ~1:30

The Verification Problem

The slide that closes §02 and justifies §03. Put the two sections side by side: §01 is provable with explicit constants, requires convexity, and nobody ships those models; §02 runs on real networks, proves nothing, and everybody ships those.

  • With no certificate, the only evidence available is a measurement.
  • But the definition is distributional — a statement about the law of $\theta_u$ against the law of $A(\mathcal{D}_r)$ — and you get one sample of each, and only if you can afford to retrain at all.
  • So verification degenerates into hypothesis testing at sample size one, which is exactly what every metric in §03 is quietly doing.
  • Worse, the standard tests are output-based, so any method that shapes outputs can pass them without touching the representation. Slide 94's head-distillation attack is the clean demonstration.

"Section one could prove it worked. Section two cannot. So section three is not really about metrics — it is about what you can still honestly say when proof is off the table."

Slide 85Section divider 03 ~0:10

03 — Classification: Metrics

Every metric is a test statistic

Pivot from "how to unlearn" to "how to measure unlearning." The recurring warning of this section: no single number works, and the reference is always Retrain, never the original model.

Slides 86–88Metrics as a test ~3:00

An Unlearning Metric Is a Hypothesis Test

The standard five-axis report card. Define each and, crucially, what direction is "good":

  • UA — unlearning accuracy, $1-\text{Acc}_{\mathcal{D}_f}$. Higher means more forgetting — but the target is Retrain's UA, not 100%.
  • RA — accuracy on the retain set; fidelity. Should stay high.
  • TA — test accuracy; generalization. Should stay high.
  • MIA-Efficacy — a membership-inference attack on $\mathcal{D}_f$; how hard it is to tell the forget points were trained on.
  • RTE — run-time efficiency vs full retrain; the cost axis.

The boxed warning is the whole point: single metrics mislead, and the reference is Retrain. A method can ace UA by destroying the model (great forgetting, useless model) — which is why you need all five, compared to retrain.

"Don't maximize any one of these. Match all five to what retraining would have given. Forgetting too much is also a failure — we'll see negative IDI in a few slides."

Frame the whole suite before defining any of it (slides 86–87): Definition 3 is a statement about two distributions, so evaluation is a test of $H_0:\theta_u\sim\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_f)$ against its negation, and every metric on the list is a test statistic — a scalar summary of the released model. "UA is high" reports one coordinate of one statistic; it is not a decision. Once they accept that framing, the rest of §03 writes itself: what null, what rejection region, one-sided or two-sided.

Slides 89–90MIA probe ~2:00

MIA as an Unlearning Probe

Spell out the MIA-Efficacy metric. Yeom-style threshold attack: a point is guessed to be a member if its loss is low.

$$s_{\text{loss}}(x,y) = -\log p_\theta(y\mid x),\qquad \hat m = \mathbb{1}[s_{\text{loss}}\le\tau].$$

Entropy variant: $s_{\text{ent}}(x) = -\sum_c p_\theta(c\mid x)\log p_\theta(c\mid x)$.

  • Members are typically fit better → lower loss / lower entropy → flagged as "in."
  • For unlearning: after we forget $\mathcal{D}_f$, the attack should fail on $\mathcal{D}_f$ — higher MIA-Efficacy means less residual membership signal.

Connect back: this is exactly the threshold attack from the MIA deck; here it's repurposed as an unlearning audit. Next slide gives its theoretical optimum and limits.

Slides 91–93Recall · Proposition 4 ~3:30

Membership Tests, and the Cap They Imply

Four slides that put the membership-inference theory to work, and the most theory-dense stretch of §03. Slide 90 is a recall card: three facts they already own, restated in unlearning notation.

  • Optimal test. The best membership test is a likelihood-ratio threshold $\mathbb{1}[\Lambda \gt \tau^\star]$. Loss and entropy probes are crude approximations to it.
  • Floor. The best loss-threshold attack achieves advantage $\ge \Delta/B$, for train–test loss gap $\Delta$ and loss range $B$. Overfitting guarantees leakage.
  • Ceiling. $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-indistinguishability caps advantage at $(e^\varepsilon-1+2\delta)/(e^\varepsilon+1)$.

Make the transfer explicit: all three are properties of a pair of distributions, not of a training algorithm. Replace "member versus non-member" with "unlearned versus retrained" and they carry over verbatim. That is precisely why the membership-inference material sits upstream of this lecture.

Proposition 4 (slides 91–92) is the punchline, and it is short enough to prove on the board. If $\mathcal{U}$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-certified, then for every measurable $h:\Theta\to[0,1]$,

$$\bigl|\mathbb{E}_P[h]-\mathbb{E}_Q[h]\bigr| \;\le\; \mathrm{TV}(P,Q) \;\le\; \beta(\varepsilon,\delta) \;=\; \frac{e^\varepsilon-1+2\delta}{e^\varepsilon+1}.$$

Proof: write $\mu=P-Q$ as a signed measure and let $S^{+}$ be a set on which $\mu\ge 0$. For $h$ valued in $[0,1]$, $\int h\,d\mu \le \int_{S^{+}} h\,d\mu \le \mu(S^{+}) = \mathrm{TV}(P,Q)$. Apply the same to $-h$ with $P$ and $Q$ exchanged to get the absolute value. $\blacksquare$

The corollary (slide 93) is the uncomfortable one. At $\varepsilon=0.1$, $\delta=10^{-5}$, $\beta\approx 0.05$: every bounded metric is guaranteed to sit within $0.05$ of retrain before anyone measures anything. So a high MIA-Efficacy score on a certified unlearner is a consequence of the certificate, not evidence for it. The measurement is vacuous.

"If you already hold the certificate, the metric cannot tell you anything you did not know. If you do not hold one, the metric is all you have. That is the trap section three lives in."

This stretch is also the in-deck home of the current problem set's membership-inference questions — the optimal test, the $\Delta/B$ floor, and the DP cap. Do not label it as such on the slide; if a student is working the problems, point them here in person.

Slides 94–96Converse · two-sided ~3:00

The Converse Fails, and the Test Is Two-Sided

The three slides that make §03 more than a list of numbers. Both halves follow from taking the hypothesis-test framing literally.

  • The converse fails (slide 94). Head distillation — retrain only the classifier head on the retain set, leave the encoder frozen — scores UA $100$ and MIA-Efficacy $2.05$ while the encoder still carries every bit of the forget data. IDI reports $1.000$: no internal forgetting whatsoever. Present HD as an attack on the metric, not as a method. It shows that passing the output suite is not sufficient, which is the converse of the vacuity result on slide 93.
  • The test is two-sided (slides 95–96). If $H_0$ is "$\theta_u$ is distributed as $\theta_{\text{retrain}}$", then both tails reject. Scoring above retrain on a privacy metric is as much a rejection as scoring below it. The number-line figure on slide 96 puts SCRUB at $-0.056$ on one side, FT and SalUn on the other, retrain at zero and the original model at one.

"Every reflex you have from security says more privacy is better. Here it is wrong. The target is a point, not a direction — and unless the metric is signed, you cannot even see which side you missed it on."

"Isn't over-forgetting harmless in practice?" — It is evidence that the method is not doing what it claims. A model pushed against $\mathcal{D}_f$ is distinguishable from retrain, and distinguishable is precisely what the definition forbids. It also costs utility you were not asked to spend.

Slides 97–100Lab · IDI · AAAI 2026 ~3:00

Information Difference Index

Our lab's contribution. The complaint: all the metrics so far are output-only; two models can give identical outputs while their internal representations still encode the forget data. Probe the intermediate layers instead.

$$\mathrm{IDI}(\theta_u) = \frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^L \big(I(\mathbf Z_\ell^{(u)};Y)-I(\mathbf Z_\ell^{(r)};Y)\big)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^L \big(I(\mathbf Z_\ell^{(o)};Y)-I(\mathbf Z_\ell^{(r)};Y)\big)}.$$

Read it as a normalized interpolation: numerator is how far the unlearned model's layer-wise mutual information sits from retrain; denominator normalizes by how far the original sits from retrain. So:

  • $\mathrm{IDI}=1$ → unlearned looks like the original → no forgetting.
  • $\mathrm{IDI}=0$ → unlearned looks like retrain → perfect forgetting.

"Strong unlearning" = matching retrain layer by layer, not just at the output. Cite it in full — it is our own paper, so get it right: Jeon, Jeung, Kim, No, Choi, An Information Theoretic Evaluation Metric for Strong Unlearning, AAAI 2026.

"Output match is weak unlearning. We want the internal representations to match retrain too — that's what IDI measures, and it's a stricter test."

Slide 98 is the picture — the paper's own conceptual illustration (Figure 4(a)). Three curves of $I(\mathbf Z_\ell;Y)$ against layer index: original, unlearned, retrain. They coincide through the early layers, because early features are generic, and fan out only near the head. The blue hatched area is the denominator, the red the numerator. Point at the two areas while saying "IDI is red over blue" — the ratio is easier to see than to read off the formula.

Slide 100 is the demonstration. CIFAR-10, ResNet-18, single-class forgetting, retrain's MIA $10.64$: FT scores MIA $0.17$ with IDI $0.671$; RL $0.00$ / $0.830$; GA $25.37$ / $0.334$; SalUn $0.01$ / $0.936$; SCRUB $19.73$ / $-0.056$. The pairs are almost uncorrelated. SalUn in particular has an attack score nowhere near retrain's and representations that are almost entirely the original's — an output-only suite would never see that.

Slide 101Over-unlearning ~2:00

Over-Unlearning Is Not Hypothetical

The conceptual payoff of having a signed, reference-anchored metric. Some methods (SCRUB, certain FT variants) overshoot retrain:

  • $\mathrm{IDI}<0$ at the final block — the unlearned representation holds less $\mathcal{D}_f$ information than retrain does.
  • This shows up as MIA-Efficacy above Retrain — which a naive reader would cheer as "extra privacy."
  • It is actually a red flag: the model is distorted away from the retrain distribution, just in the other direction. It is not a model that "never saw" $\mathcal{D}_f$; it's a model that was actively pushed against $\mathcal{D}_f$.

Reinforce the whole section: the goal is match, not maximize. Both under- and over-shooting fail the distributional definition from slide 4.

"Beating retrain on a privacy metric isn't winning — it means you've left the retrain distribution. A signed metric like IDI is what lets you even see that you overshot."

The numbers that make it concrete (CIFAR-10, ResNet-18, single-class forgetting): SCRUB $-0.056$; the exact-unlearning and catastrophic-forgetting baselines $-0.349$ and $-0.060$; a randomly initialised network $-1.281$, which fixes the scale. And the sign is not stable across settings — SCRUB is $+0.339$ on CIFAR-100.

One-sided reporting would have called every one of those a success.

Slides 102–104Lab · COLA ~2:00

COLA — Collapse and Align

The method that pairs with the IDI metric — using IDI as both the yardstick and the training objective. Two stages:

  • Collapse — a supervised contrastive loss on the encoder outputs of the retain set. Tightening the retain classes' clusters is what pulls the forget features into them; note carefully that the loss is computed on the retain set, not on $\mathcal{D}_f$.
  • Align — cross-entropy on the retain set over the whole model, realigning encoder and head after the collapse stage moved the encoder.

The design principle to say out loud: tighten the retain clusters, do not disperse the forget features. Dispersion is what damages the model, and damage reads as negative IDI. That is why COLA lands near the anchor rather than overshooting it.

Slide 103 is the feature-space picture (the arXiv version's Figure 7, class-wise forgetting; the AAAI camera-ready defers to it). Three panels. Before: two retain clusters, green and blue, and a red forget cluster of its own. Collapse: the retain clusters contract inward and the red points, no longer held together, drift toward them. After: red points sit inside and around the retain clusters — absorbed, not exiled. Say the contrast out loud: a method that pushed red away would look like progress on an output metric and would read as negative IDI.

Numbers (slide 104): single-class forgetting, $\mathrm{IDI} = 0.010$ on CIFAR-10 and $-0.037$ on CIFAR-100, against SCRUB's $-0.056$ and $+0.339$ on the same two — SCRUB is not even sign-stable. For random data forgetting, COLA+ reaches $0.024$ with UA $3.90$ against retrain's $3.94$. Be honest about the caveat on the slide: metric and method come from the same paper, so read it as a reachability demonstration, not an independent benchmark.

"Collapse forgets, align remembers. Because we measure at every layer, we also have to act at every layer."

Slides 105–106Worked example ~3:00

Random 10% Forgetting — CIFAR-10, ResNet-18

Make the metrics concrete. Random 10% of CIFAR-10 is the forget set; read the table against the Retrain row, not down a column.

  • Retrain sets the reference: UA 5.24, RA 100, TA 94.26, MIA 12.88, gap 0 by definition.
  • FT and GA look "good" on UA (low) but their MIA collapses to ~2 — far below retrain's 12.88. They under-forget in the MIA sense; average gap ~3.8–4.1.
  • $\ell_1$-sparse is closer (gap 2.26).
  • SalUn is closest overall: UA 2.85, RA 99.62, TA 93.93, MIA 14.39 against retrain's 12.88, smallest average gap 1.15.
  • Runtime: retrain 43.29 min against SalUn's 2.66 min. Without the RTE axis none of this is worth doing.

The lesson to draw aloud: SalUn is best not because any single number is extreme, but because every axis is close to retrain. Closeness, not extremity.

"Notice GA's MIA is 1.7 versus retrain's 12.88 — that's not 'great privacy,' that's a sign GA distorted the model differently than retrain would. Match, don't beat."

Slide 107Practice ~1:30

What a Defensible Evaluation Looks Like

Land the section on a checklist they can apply as referees or as experimenters. This is the slide students photograph.

  • Report against retrain on every axis, as a signed gap. Never against the original model, never as a single headline number.
  • Report both directions. An unsigned metric structurally hides over-unlearning.
  • Include at least one representation-level probe. Output-only suites are defeated by head distillation, on purpose, in ten minutes.
  • Include a robustness probe — perturbed forget samples, in the RURK sense — because forgetting the exact points is not forgetting the concept.
  • Report RTE. A method costing 80% of retrain has not earned the name.
  • State the forget-set construction: random subset or whole class. The two regimes behave differently and their numbers are not comparable.

"If a paper hands you one number and it is larger than retrain's, you have learned nothing about whether it forgot."

Slide 108Takeaways ~1:30

Takeaways — Part I

Recap the spine:

  • Definition — distributional match to retrain; anything weaker is suppression, not deletion.
  • Certified deletion — one Newton step removes a point's $O(1/n)$ influence; Gaussian noise masks the $O(1/n^2)$ residual. We proved this end-to-end.
