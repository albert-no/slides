Homer's Statistic, Formally

Per SNP $j$: Alice's allele $Y_j \in \{0,1\}$, study mean $M_j$, population frequency $\mathrm{Pop}_j = p_j$.

$$D_j \;=\; \lvert Y_j - \mathrm{Pop}_j\rvert - \lvert Y_j - M_j\rvert \;=\; (M_j - \mathrm{Pop}_j)\,(2Y_j - 1).$$

Check both cases: $Y_j = 1$ gives $(1 - \mathrm{Pop}_j) - (1 - M_j)$; $Y_j = 0$ gives $\mathrm{Pop}_j - M_j$. The study's drift, signed by Alice's letter.

Homer et al., "Resolving Individuals Contributing Trace Amounts of DNA to Highly Complex Mixtures Using High-Density SNP Genotyping Microarrays", PLoS Genetics 2008.

One SNP: Mean and Noise

Idealized model: $n$ people with i.i.d. $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_j)$ alleles, $v_j = p_j(1-p_j)$, $p_j$ known.

$$\text{out: } \mathbb{E}[D_j] = 0,\; \mathrm{Var}\,D_j = \frac{v_j}{n}; \qquad \text{in: } \mathbb{E}[D_j] = \frac{2v_j}{n},\; \mathrm{Var}\,D_j \approx \frac{v_j}{n}.$$

Why

Outside, $Y_j$ is independent of $M_j$ and $\mathbb{E}[M_j] = p_j$, so the mean is $0$ and the noise is $\mathrm{Var}(M_j) = v_j/n$. Inside, $M_j - p_j = (Y_j - p_j)/n + \text{(others)}$, so $\mathbb{E}[D_j] = \mathbb{E}[(Y_j - p_j)(2Y_j - 1)]/n = \bigl[p_j(1-p_j) + (1-p_j)p_j\bigr]/n = 2v_j/n$.

Per-SNP signal-to-noise: $2\sqrt{v_j/n} \approx 0.03$ at $n = 1{,}000$, $v_j = 0.2$.

Summing $m$ SNPs: Test and Power

Independent SNPs add. Standardize the sum by its null noise.

$$T = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^m D_j}{\sqrt{\sum_j v_j / n}}, \qquad T \approx \mathcal N(0,1) \text{ out}, \qquad T \approx \mathcal N(\mu, 1) \text{ in}, \quad \mu = 2\sqrt{\frac{m\bar v}{n}}.$$

One-sided test at level $\alpha$: say "in" when $T \gt z_\alpha$. Power $= \Phi(\mu - z_\alpha)$.

At $\alpha = 10^{-6}$ ($z_\alpha \approx 4.75$), $n = 1{,}000$, $\bar v = 0.2$: power $0.5$ needs $\mu = z_\alpha$, so $m \approx 28{,}000$; power $0.8$ needs $\mu = z_\alpha + 0.84$, so $m \approx 39{,}000$. Required $m$ grows linearly in $n$.

Sankararaman, Obozinski, Jordan, and Halperin, "Genomic privacy and limits of individual detection in a pool", Nature Genetics 2009.

Score and Threshold

Every MIA is a decision rule: a score $s(x)$ against a threshold $\tau$.

$$\hat{m}(x) = \mathbb{1}\!\left[\, s(x) \ge \tau \,\right], \qquad s(x) = -\ell(f_\theta, x).$$

Lower loss ⇒ higher score ⇒ predict "member". Tuning $\tau$ traces the ROC curve.

The Optimal Test

Neyman–Pearson: the most powerful test at any FPR uses the likelihood ratio.

$$\Lambda(x) \;=\; \frac{\Pr[\,\ell(f_\theta, x) \mid x \text{ in}\,]}{\Pr[\,\ell(f_\theta, x) \mid x \text{ out}\,]}.$$

Decide "member" when $\Lambda(x) \ge \tau$. Raw loss is a crude proxy for this ratio.

Sablayrolles et al., "White-box vs Black-box: Bayes Optimal Strategies for Membership Inference", ICML 2019.

Why Per-Example Calibration

A global $\tau$ confuses difficulty with membership.

  • Easy $x$: low loss even when out
  • Hard $x$: high loss even when in
  • $\Lambda(x)$ compares $x$ to its own in/out baselines

The ratio normalizes away each record's intrinsic loss scale.

LiRA: Likelihood Ratio Attack

Estimate the two conditional distributions with shadow models, per record.

  1. Train $N$ shadows on random halves of a pool; $x$ is in about $N/2$ of them.
  2. Fit $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{in}}, \sigma_{\text{in}}^2)$ and $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{out}}, \sigma_{\text{out}}^2)$ to the shadows' scores $s_k = \phi\bigl(f_{\theta_k}(x)_y\bigr)$.
  3. Observe the target score $s^\star = \phi\bigl(f_\theta(x)_y\bigr)$.
  4. Score by $\Lambda(x) = \mathcal{N}(s^\star; \mu_{\text{in}}, \sigma_{\text{in}}^2) / \mathcal{N}(s^\star; \mu_{\text{out}}, \sigma_{\text{out}}^2)$; say "member" iff $\Lambda(x) \gt \tau$.
Carlini et al., "Membership Inference Attacks From First Principles", IEEE S&P 2022.

Why the Logit Scale

The Gaussian fit needs a score whose per-record distribution is close to normal.

$$\phi(p) = \log\frac{p}{1-p}, \qquad p = f_\theta(x)_y, \qquad \phi = -\ell - \log\bigl(1 - e^{-\ell}\bigr) \text{ with } \ell = -\log p.$$
  • Confidences pile up near $1$; raw $p$ and raw loss are badly skewed
  • The logit stretches the top end, so in- and out-scores each look Gaussian
  • Same monotone ordering as the loss, so nothing is lost for a single threshold
Carlini et al., IEEE S&P 2022, Sec. V-D ("logit scaling").

The Log-Likelihood Ratio

Two Gaussians give a closed-form score.

$$\log \Lambda(x) = \frac{(s^\star - \mu_{\text{out}})^2}{2\sigma_{\text{out}}^2} - \frac{(s^\star - \mu_{\text{in}})^2}{2\sigma_{\text{in}}^2} + \log\frac{\sigma_{\text{out}}}{\sigma_{\text{in}}}.$$

A distance-to-each-bell comparison: closer to the in-bell ⇒ larger score. With few shadows ($N \lt 64$) Carlini et al. pool one global $\sigma^2$ across records.

The Worked Example, Checked

Equal variances make the rule linear in the score.

$$\sigma_{\text{in}} = \sigma_{\text{out}} = \sigma \;\Rightarrow\; \log\Lambda = \frac{(\mu_{\text{in}} - \mu_{\text{out}})}{\sigma^2}\Bigl(s^\star - \frac{\mu_{\text{in}} + \mu_{\text{out}}}{2}\Bigr).$$

Main-deck numbers: $\mu_{\text{out}} = 2$, $\mu_{\text{in}} = 6$, $\sigma = 1.5$, $s^\star = 5$

$\log\Lambda = \dfrac{4}{2.25}\,(5 - 4) \approx 1.78$, so $\Lambda = e^{1.78} \approx 5.9$. The rule "$\Lambda \gt \tau$" with $\tau = 1$ is exactly "$s^\star \gt 4$", the midpoint. With $\tau = 10$ it becomes $s^\star \gt 4 + 2.30 \cdot 2.25/4 \approx 5.30$, so $s^\star = 5$ is called out.

$\tau$ is fixed before looking at $s^\star$; it sets the FPR. $\Lambda \approx 5.9$ is the evidence, not the threshold.

Offline LiRA: Out-Shadows Only

Training in-shadows for every new record is the expensive half. Drop them.

$$s_{\text{off}}(x) = \Pr\bigl[Z \le s^\star\bigr], \qquad Z \sim \mathcal N(\mu_{\text{out}}, \sigma_{\text{out}}^2).$$
  • A one-sided test: how surprising is $s^\star$ if $x$ was never trained on?
  • Shadows trained once, reused for any query; no per-record retraining
  • Weaker than the two-sided test at very low FPR, still far above a global threshold
Carlini et al., IEEE S&P 2022, Sec. V-C (offline attack).

TPR, FPR, and the ROC Curve

Both rates are functions of the threshold, and both fall as $\tau$ rises.

$$\mathrm{TPR}(\tau) = \Pr[\Lambda \gt \tau \mid \text{in}], \qquad \mathrm{FPR}(\tau) = \Pr[\Lambda \gt \tau \mid \text{out}].$$

$\mathrm{ROC} = \{(\mathrm{FPR}(\tau), \mathrm{TPR}(\tau)) : \tau \in \mathbb{R}\}$, a curve from $(1,1)$ down to $(0,0)$. Balanced accuracy $= \tfrac12\bigl(\mathrm{TPR} + 1 - \mathrm{FPR}\bigr)$.

The diagonal $\mathrm{TPR} = \mathrm{FPR}$ is coin-flipping. Neyman–Pearson says the $\Lambda$-ROC lies above every other score's ROC.

AUC Is a Probability

Proposition

Let $S_1 \sim q_{\text{in}}$ and $S_0 \sim q_{\text{out}}$ be independent scores of a random member and a random non-member. Then $\mathrm{AUC} = \Pr[S_1 \gt S_0]$.

$$\mathrm{AUC} = \int_0^1 \mathrm{TPR}\, d\mathrm{FPR} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Pr[S_1 \gt \tau]\, q_{\text{out}}(\tau)\, d\tau = \mathbb{E}_{S_0}\bigl[\Pr[S_1 \gt S_0 \mid S_0]\bigr].$$

Empirically this is the Mann–Whitney $U$ statistic: a rank average over all member/non-member pairs, so it is dominated by the bulk of the curve and blind to the far-left edge.

Why Low FPR: Base Rates

Members are a fraction $\pi$ of the people the model could be asked about. What an accusation is worth:

$$\Pr[\text{member} \mid \text{say member}] = \frac{\pi\,\mathrm{TPR}}{\pi\,\mathrm{TPR} + (1-\pi)\,\mathrm{FPR}}.$$

With $\pi = 1/101$ (one member per 100 non-members) a $1\%$ FPR yields one false accusation per true hit even for a perfect attack. Only the low-FPR regime makes a flag credible.

TPR at a Fixed Low FPR

Fix a tolerable false-alarm rate $\alpha$ and report the catch rate there.

$$\mathrm{TPR}@\alpha = \mathrm{TPR}(\tau_\alpha), \qquad \mathrm{FPR}(\tau_\alpha) = \alpha, \qquad \alpha \in \{10^{-3}, 10^{-5}\}.$$

Read it on a log-log ROC. Attacks with equal accuracy or AUC differ by orders of magnitude here.

Scale check: on normally pretrained 10M–1B LLMs, LiRA with 128 shadows reaches AUC $\le 0.7$ and $\mathrm{TPR}@10^{-4}$ near $1\%$ (Hayes et al., NeurIPS 2025).

The DP Bound on Any Attack

"In" vs "out" are neighboring datasets, so $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP caps every attacker.

$$\mathrm{TPR} \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{FPR} + \delta.$$

Small $\varepsilon$ pins the ROC near the diagonal: true positives stay near false positives.

Kairouz, Oh, and Viswanath, "The Composition Theorem for Differential Privacy", ICML 2015.

Auditing Flips the Bound

Run the strongest MIA, read its TPR/FPR, and invert the bound for an empirical $\varepsilon$.

Empirical lower bound

$\displaystyle \hat\varepsilon = \log\frac{\mathrm{TPR} - \delta}{\mathrm{FPR}}$ is a lower bound on the true $\varepsilon$. Above the claim ⇒ a bug; far below ⇒ a loose proof.

The attack becomes a privacy meter. Full proofs live in the DP lecture series.
