From Optimal Test
to Estimation Problem

The two unknown conditionals, and how to sample them

Where We Left Off

Recall (optimal attack).

An attack optimal at any operating point agrees almost everywhere with a threshold rule on

$\Lambda \;=\; \frac{p_1}{p_0} \;=\; \frac{\text{density of what we observe when } z \text{ was trained on}}{\text{density when it was not}}.$
MI game→ advantage→ total variation→ likelihood ratio

The lemma names the optimal statistic. It does not hand you the two densities.

Notation for This Lecture

SymbolMeaning
$\mathcal{D},\; D \sim \mathcal{D}^n$Record distribution, training set of $n$ i.i.d. records
$T,\; \theta = T(D)$Randomized training algorithm and the model it returns
$z = (x, y)$Target record under test
$\varphi = \Phi(\theta, z)$Observable: what the API returns, e.g. $\sigma(f_\theta(x))$
$P_1,\; P_0$Law of $\varphi$ when $z$ is in the training set, and when it is not

Everything the attacker may do is a function of $\varphi$, possibly together with $z$ itself.

The Two Conditionals, Precisely

Definition (member and non-member laws).

$\begin{aligned} P_1 &= \text{law of } \Phi\bigl(T(D),\, Z\bigr), \quad Z \text{ drawn uniformly from } D, \\ P_0 &= \text{law of } \Phi\bigl(T(D),\, Z'\bigr), \quad Z' \sim \mathcal{D} \text{ independent of } D. \end{aligned}$

Marginals over three randomness sources: the other $n-1$ records, training noise, target draw.

Two Densities the Attacker Cannot Write Down

$p_1$ $p_0$ observable $\varphi$

Shapes unknown; only the ratio of their heights matters.

Why the Ratio Is Not Computable

Unknown $\mathcal{D}$

The record distribution is exactly what the attacker lacks.

Intractable $T$

$p_1$ integrates over SGD noise, initialisation, batch order.

Nuisance records

Both densities marginalise the other $n-1$ training points.

The optimal statistic is defined, not available. That gap is this lecture.

Key Trick: Sample What You Cannot Integrate

Key trick.

Cannot evaluate $p_1, p_0$. Can sample both: rerun the training game on data you own.

Membership is then known by construction.

own data→ own models→ labelled observables→ estimate $\Lambda$

Monte-Carlo, in the exact classical sense: replace an integral by replicates.

The Shadow Recipe

Shadow-model construction
  1. Draw $k$ shadow sets $D_1^s, \dots, D_k^s$ of size $n$ from your best guess at $\mathcal{D}$.
  2. Train $\theta_j = T(D_j^s)$ with the target's architecture and recipe.
  3. Query $\theta_j$ on its own members: record $\varphi$ with label IN.
  4. Query $\theta_j$ on fresh held-out records: record $\varphi$ with label OUT.
  5. Fit any binary classifier to the pooled labelled set.

Steps 1 to 4 are the estimator. Step 5 is where the ratio appears.

Monte-Carlo Picture

IN observables OUT observables $\hat p_1$ $\hat p_0$

Assumption S — Faithful Shadows

Assumption S.

  • Shadow records are i.i.d. from the same $\mathcal{D}$, and $|D_j^s| = n$.
  • The shadow trainer is the target trainer $T$, run with independent randomness.
  • Held-out probes are fresh draws from $\mathcal{D}$, independent of $D_j^s$.

Strong on purpose. Everything after this lecture is an attack on one of the three clauses.

Proposition 1 — Shadows Simulate Exactly

Proposition 1.

Under Assumption S, write $\theta_j = T(D_j^s)$, let $Z_j$ be uniform on $D_j^s$ and $Z_j' \sim \mathcal{D}$ fresh. Then

$\Phi(\theta_j, Z_j) \sim P_1, \qquad \Phi(\theta_j, Z_j') \sim P_0,$

and across $j = 1, \dots, k$ these pairs are independent and identically distributed.

No approximation anywhere: the shadow game is the game.

Proof of Proposition 1

  • Clauses 1–2 match data law and training kernel, hence the pairs agree:
$\bigl(D_j^s,\, \theta_j\bigr) \;\stackrel{d}{=}\; \bigl(D,\, T(D)\bigr) = (D, \theta).$
  • Equality in distribution survives measurable maps: apply $(D, \theta) \mapsto \Phi(\theta, Z)$, $Z$ uniform on $D$.
  • OUT: clause 3 gives $Z_j' \perp \theta_j$, hence $(\theta_j, Z_j') \stackrel{d}{=} (\theta, Z')$ and $\Phi$ gives $P_0$.
  • Runs use independent draws and noise, so the $k$ pairs are i.i.d. $\;\square$

Consequence: unlimited labelled draws from $P_1$ and $P_0$, at the price of $k$ trainings.

From Samples to a Decision Rule

Balance the shadow set: $b = 1$ on IN, $b = 0$ on OUT. Ordinary supervised learning follows.

$b \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\tfrac12), \qquad \varphi \mid b = 1 \sim P_1, \qquad \varphi \mid b = 0 \sim P_0.$

Population risk.

$R(\mathcal{A}) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[-b \log \mathcal{A}(\varphi) - (1-b)\log\bigl(1 - \mathcal{A}(\varphi)\bigr)\bigr], \quad \mathcal{A} : \mathcal{U} \to (0,1).$

Theorem 1 — Shadow Training Recovers the Ratio

Theorem 1.

The unique minimiser of $R$ over measurable $\mathcal{A}$ is

$\mathcal{A}^\star(u) \;=\; \Pr[b = 1 \mid \varphi = u] \;=\; \frac{p_1(u)}{p_1(u) + p_0(u)} \;=\; \frac{\Lambda(u)}{1 + \Lambda(u)},$

a strictly increasing function of $\Lambda(u)$. Hence every threshold on $\mathcal{A}^\star$ is a likelihood-ratio test.

Proof of Theorem 1 — Outline

Three moves
  1. Condition. Rewrite the risk as an average of one-variable losses.
  2. Minimise pointwise. The inner loss is strictly convex with an explicit minimiser.
  3. Invert the link. The posterior is a monotone transform of $\Lambda$, so thresholds carry over.

Nothing here is specific to log loss beyond one property: it is a proper scoring rule.

Proof of Theorem 1 — Step 1, Condition

Write $\eta(u) = \Pr[b = 1 \mid \varphi = u]$ and use the tower property:

$R(\mathcal{A}) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\varphi}\Bigl[\, h\bigl(\eta(\varphi),\, \mathcal{A}(\varphi)\bigr)\Bigr], \qquad h(\eta, a) = -\eta \log a - (1-\eta)\log(1-a).$
  • $\mathcal{A}$ is unconstrained, so its value at each $u$ can be chosen separately.
  • Minimising the average therefore reduces to minimising $h(\eta(u), \cdot)$ for every $u$.

Proof of Theorem 1 — Step 2, Minimise

$\frac{\partial h}{\partial a} = -\frac{\eta}{a} + \frac{1-\eta}{1-a} = \frac{a - \eta}{a(1-a)}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 h}{\partial a^2} = \frac{\eta}{a^2} + \frac{1-\eta}{(1-a)^2} \gt 0.$
  • Strictly convex in $a$ on $(0,1)$, with derivative vanishing only at $a = \eta$.
  • So the pointwise minimiser is $\mathcal{A}^\star(u) = \eta(u)$, and it is unique.

This is the statement that log loss is proper: honest probabilities win.

Proof of Theorem 1 — Step 3, Invert

  • Bayes with a balanced prior gives $\eta(u) = \dfrac{\frac12 p_1(u)}{\frac12 p_1(u) + \frac12 p_0(u)} = \dfrac{\Lambda(u)}{1 + \Lambda(u)}$.
  • The map $t \mapsto t/(1+t)$ is strictly increasing on $[0, \infty)$, with inverse $s \mapsto s/(1-s)$.
$\mathcal{A}^\star(u) \gt s \iff \Lambda(u) \gt \frac{s}{1-s}. \qquad \square$

Sweeping $s$ sweeps the threshold on $\Lambda$: one trained score, every operating point.

Proof of Theorem 1 — Recap

$\begin{aligned} \arg\min_{\mathcal{A}} R(\mathcal{A}) &\stackrel{(1)}{=} \arg\min_{a(\cdot)} \mathbb{E}_\varphi\bigl[h(\eta(\varphi), a(\varphi))\bigr] \\ &\stackrel{(2)}{=} \eta(\cdot) \\ &\stackrel{(3)}{=} \frac{\Lambda(\cdot)}{1 + \Lambda(\cdot)} \;\stackrel{(4)}{\equiv}\; \text{threshold rules on } \Lambda. \end{aligned}$

(1) tower property  ·  (2) strict convexity  ·  (3) Bayes, balanced prior  ·  (4) strict monotonicity.

What Theorem 1 Buys

Design

Any calibrated classifier trained on shadow labels estimates $\Lambda$.

Optimality

At the population optimum the attack is Neyman-Pearson optimal at every size.

Diagnosis

A weak attack means a poor ratio estimate, not an absence of leakage.

Shadow modelling is not a heuristic. It is Monte-Carlo estimation of a specified statistic.

Two Gaps Between Estimator and Estimand

Statistical gap

Finite $k$, finite capacity, finite probes. Shrinks with more shadows and better models.

Specification gap

Wrong $\mathcal{D}$, wrong architecture, wrong recipe. Does not shrink with more shadows.

The second gap is the interesting one: no amount of effort removes it.

Mis-specification, Formally

Shadow game induces $Q_1, Q_0$; target game induces $P_1, P_0$. Calibrate under $Q$, deploy under $P$.

$\alpha_Q(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{Q_0}[\phi], \quad \pi_Q(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{Q_1}[\phi]; \qquad \alpha_P(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{P_0}[\phi], \quad \pi_P(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{P_1}[\phi].$
  • $\alpha_Q, \pi_Q$: the rates the attacker measures on shadows.
  • $\alpha_P, \pi_P$: the rates that actually occur on the target.

Same rule $\phi$, two different worlds. How far apart can its rates be?

Corollary 1 — Mis-specification Costs at Most TV

Corollary 1.

For every randomized test $\phi : \mathcal{U} \to [0,1]$,

$\bigl|\alpha_P(\phi) - \alpha_Q(\phi)\bigr| \le \mathrm{TV}(P_0, Q_0), \qquad \bigl|\pi_P(\phi) - \pi_Q(\phi)\bigr| \le \mathrm{TV}(P_1, Q_1),$

hence the deployed advantage drops by at most $\mathrm{TV}(P_1,Q_1) + \mathrm{TV}(P_0,Q_0)$.

Proof of Corollary 1

  • For any $\phi$ with $0 \le \phi \le 1$ and any two laws $P, Q$ with densities $p, q$:
$\Bigl|\int \phi \, (p - q)\Bigr| \;\le\; \int \phi\,(p-q)_+ + \int \phi\,(q-p)_+ \;\le\; \int (p-q)_+ \;=\; \mathrm{TV}(P,Q).$
  • Apply with $(P_0, Q_0)$ for size, $(P_1, Q_1)$ for power; subtract. $\;\square$

Step one adds a non-negative term, then uses $0 \le \phi \le 1$ and $\mathrm{TV} = \int (p-q)_+$.

One inequality covers every mismatch in Assumption S: data, trainer, probes.

Reading Corollary 1

Bound is uniform

Holds for every test, so no cleverness escapes a badly wrong shadow world.

Bound is loose

TV is worst case. A shift that moves both laws together costs almost nothing.

Repair is cheap

The damage is often a shift in the score, removable by recalibrating the threshold.

How much of Assumption S can be dropped is the subject of the next lecture.

The Shift That Breaks a Threshold

OUT IN shadow world target world threshold set on shadows systematic shift attack score

Both laws move together: separation survives, the calibrated threshold does not.

The Shokri Paradigm

Shadow models against cloud ML APIs

Shokri et al. — The Setting

Target

A classifier trained through Google Prediction API or Amazon ML, queried as a black box.

Observable

The confidence vector $\sigma(f_\theta(x)) \in \Delta^{C-1}$, nothing else.

First systematic black-box membership attack, and the origin of the shadow recipe.

Shokri, Stronati, Song, Shmatikov, "Membership Inference Attacks Against Machine Learning Models", IEEE S&P 2017

Instantiating the Observable

The abstract $\varphi = \Phi(\theta, z)$ becomes concrete:

$\Phi(\theta, z) \;=\; \bigl(\sigma(f_\theta(x)),\; y\bigr), \qquad \sigma(v)_c = \frac{e^{v_c}}{\sum_{c'} e^{v_{c'}}}.$
  • Fixed dimension $C$: a single classifier can consume it.
  • Carries the model's uncertainty, which is what overfitting distorts.
  • The true label $y$ is known to the attacker, who supplied the record.

Attack Architecture — Training Pipeline

$D_1^s$ $D_2^s$ $D_k^s$ $f_{\theta_1}$ $f_{\theta_2}$ $f_{\theta_k}$ $\bigl(\sigma(f_{\theta_j}(x)),\, c,\, b\bigr)$ $b \in \{\text{IN}, \text{OUT}\}$ $\mathcal{A}_c$

Attack Architecture — Inference

$x$ target $f_\theta$ $\sigma(f_\theta(x))$ $\mathcal{A}_c$ IN / OUT

One query, one forward pass: cheap, black-box, repeatable at scale.

Every engineering choice is an estimator choice in disguise.

Three Ways to Get Shadow Data

Model-based

Synthesize records by hill climbing on the target's own confidences.

Statistics-based

Sample from published marginals of each feature.

Noisy real data

Take a related public corpus and perturb a fraction of features.

All three are attempts to approximate $\mathcal{D}$, which is clause 1 of Assumption S.

Model-Based Synthesis

Hill climbing on the target API
  1. Fix a target class $c$. Initialise a record $x$ with random feature values.
  2. Query the target for $\sigma(f_\theta(x))_c$.
  3. Propose $x'$ by resampling a small random subset of features.
  4. Accept $x'$ if the class-$c$ confidence increased, otherwise keep $x$.
  5. Once confidence exceeds a threshold, keep $x$ as a synthetic record for $c$.

No auxiliary data at all: the target's confidence surface is the only guide.

What Synthesis Actually Estimates

Hill climbing favours high-confidence regions, so the synthetic law is $\mathcal{D}$ tilted toward confident points:

$q(x) \;\propto\; \mathcal{D}(x)\, w\bigl(\sigma(f_\theta(x))_c\bigr), \qquad w \text{ increasing}.$
  • Good news: confident regions are where the membership signal lives.
  • Bad news: this is exactly a violation of clause 1, so Corollary 1 applies.

Collecting the Attack Training Set

Member queries

For $x \in D_j^s$ record $\bigl(\sigma(f_{\theta_j}(x)),\, c,\, \text{IN}\bigr)$.

Non-member queries

For fresh $x \notin D_j^s$ record $\bigl(\sigma(f_{\theta_j}(x)),\, c,\, \text{OUT}\bigr)$.

$\mathcal{S} \;=\; \bigcup_{j=1}^{k} \Bigl\{\bigl(\sigma(f_{\theta_j}(x)),\, c(x),\, b\bigr)\Bigr\}, \qquad |\mathcal{S}| \approx 2kn.$

Balance the two labels, or reweight, so the posterior of Theorem 1 is the intended one.

Pseudocode — Build and Train

// Phase 1: build attack training data for j = 1 to k: D_s[j] ← sample_data(shadow_distribution) hold[j] ← sample_data(shadow_distribution) // disjoint f[j] ← train_model(D_s[j]) // target's recipe for x in D_s[j]: S.add(f[j](x), class(x), "IN") for x in hold[j]: S.add(f[j](x), class(x), "OUT") // Phase 2: train one attack model per class for c in classes: A[c] ← train_binary_classifier(S[c])

Pseudocode — Attack

// Phase 3: attack a single record def attack(target_model, x): p ← target_model(x) // confidence vector c ← argmax(p) // route to the class model return A[c](p) > tau // tau sets the operating point

The threshold tau is not a detail: it picks the point on the ROC curve.

  • Attack transfers across platforms with no change of method.
  • Works without knowing the model family the service chose.

Why More Classes Leak More

The observable lives on the simplex, whose dimension grows with the number of classes:

$\sigma(f_\theta(x)) \in \Delta^{C-1} = \Bigl\{p \in \mathbb{R}^C : p_c \ge 0,\; \textstyle\sum_c p_c = 1\Bigr\}, \qquad \dim = C - 1.$

A richer observable can only help: any coarse-view test is a fine-view test ignoring coordinates.

Formally a data-processing statement, made precise in Section 03.

Overfitting Is the Engine

train accuracy test accuracy gap training epochs

The gap is the separation between $P_1$ and $P_0$, seen through accuracy.

Limitations of the Shadow Recipe

  • Data: needs a usable approximation of $\mathcal{D}$.
  • Architecture: best when the shadow recipe matches the target's.
  • Cost: $k$ full trainings per target, and one classifier per class.
  • Average case: the classifier is fit to bulk behaviour, not to hard examples.

Clauses 1 and 2 are attacked next lecture; the average-case flaw waits for per-example calibration.

Per-Class Models and
the Threshold Baseline

Why conditioning helps, why a threshold is still a ratio test

Per-Class Attack Models

Shokri trains one binary attack model for each class label:

$\mathcal{A}_c : \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}|} \to \{0,1\}, \qquad c \in \mathcal{C},$

and routes a query by its class, so the deployed attack is $\mathcal{A}_{c(z)}(\sigma(f_\theta(x)))$.

Looks like an engineering convenience. It is a statement about likelihood ratios.

Setup: Class-Conditional Laws

Class $c$ has prior $\pi_c$; write $P_b^c$ for the law of $\varphi$ given membership $b$, class $c$.

$P_b \;=\; \sum_{c} \pi_c\, P_b^c, \qquad b \in \{0, 1\}.$

Standing assumption.

$\pi_c$ is the same under both hypotheses: the target is drawn from $\mathcal{D}$ either way.

Lemma 1 — The Pooled Ratio Is an Average

Lemma 1.

Write $\Lambda_c = p_1^c / p_0^c$ for the class-$c$ ratio. Then for every $u$ with $p_0(u) \gt 0$,

$\Lambda_{\text{pool}}(u) \;=\; \sum_c w_c(u)\, \Lambda_c(u), \qquad w_c(u) = \frac{\pi_c\, p_0^c(u)}{p_0(u)},$

and the weights $w_c(u)$ are non-negative and sum to one.

Proof of Lemma 1

$\begin{aligned} \Lambda_{\text{pool}}(u) &= \frac{\sum_c \pi_c\, p_1^c(u)}{p_0(u)} \;\stackrel{(1)}{=}\; \sum_c \frac{\pi_c\, p_0^c(u)}{p_0(u)} \cdot \frac{p_1^c(u)}{p_0^c(u)} \\ &\stackrel{(2)}{=}\; \sum_c w_c(u)\, \Lambda_c(u). \end{aligned}$
  • (1) multiply and divide each term by $p_0^c(u)$.
  • (2) the weights sum to $\sum_c \pi_c p_0^c(u) / p_0(u) = 1$ by definition of the mixture. $\;\square$

Corollary 2 — Pooling Pulls Toward One

Corollary 2.

$\min_c \Lambda_c(u) \;\le\; \Lambda_{\text{pool}}(u) \;\le\; \max_c \Lambda_c(u).$

Classes disagreeing in sign at the same $u$ pull the pooled ratio toward the uninformative $1$.

Immediate: a convex combination lies between its extremes.

Why a Pooled Score Blurs

pooled over classes conditioned on class IN OUT class 1 class 2 score

Proposition 2 — Conditioning Never Hurts

Proposition 2.

  • At every size $\alpha$, the best class-aware test dominates the best class-blind test.
  • For the advantage,
$\begin{aligned} \sup_{\text{class-aware}} \mathrm{Adv} \;&=\; \sum_c \pi_c\, \mathrm{TV}\bigl(P_1^c, P_0^c\bigr) \\ &\ge\; \mathrm{TV}(P_1, P_0) \;=\; \sup_{\text{class-blind}} \mathrm{Adv}. \end{aligned}$

Part (i) is a subfamily argument; part (ii) is a triangle inequality.

Proof of Proposition 2 — Part (i)

  • A class-blind test is a map $\phi : \mathcal{U} \to [0,1]$. A class-aware test is a map $\phi : \mathcal{C} \times \mathcal{U} \to [0,1]$.
  • Every class-blind test equals the class-aware test $\phi(c, u) = \phi(u)$, which simply ignores its first argument.
  • So class-blind tests form a subfamily, with the same size under $P_0$.
  • Maximising power over a superset can only increase it, at every $\alpha$.

The whole ROC curve moves up or stays put. Nothing is assumed about the densities.

Proof of Proposition 2 — Part (ii), Class-Aware Side

  • A class-aware rejection set is a family $S = \{S_c\}_c$, and
$\mathrm{Adv}(S) = \Bigl|\sum_c \pi_c \bigl(P_1^c(S_c) - P_0^c(S_c)\bigr)\Bigr|.$
  • The sets $S_c$ are chosen independently, so the supremum splits: choose $S_c^\star = \{p_1^c \gt p_0^c\}$.
  • Each term reaches $\mathrm{TV}(P_1^c, P_0^c)$, giving $\sum_c \pi_c \mathrm{TV}(P_1^c, P_0^c)$.

Proof of Proposition 2 — Part (ii), Class-Blind Side

$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{TV}(P_1, P_0) &\stackrel{(1)}{=} \tfrac12 \int \Bigl|\sum_c \pi_c \bigl(p_1^c(u) - p_0^c(u)\bigr)\Bigr| \, du \\ &\stackrel{(2)}{\le} \tfrac12 \sum_c \pi_c \int \bigl|p_1^c(u) - p_0^c(u)\bigr| \, du \;\stackrel{(3)}{=}\; \sum_c \pi_c\, \mathrm{TV}(P_1^c, P_0^c). \;\square \end{aligned}$

(1) mixture form  ·  (2) triangle inequality  ·  (3) definition of total variation.

Key Trick: Mixing Is Data Processing

Key trick.

Discarding the label is a channel; total variation never increases under a channel.

  • Equality holds exactly when $p_1^c(u) - p_0^c(u)$ shares a sign across $c$, almost everywhere.
  • That is: all classes agree on which scores mean member. Rarely true when class difficulty varies.

Per-Class Models in Practice

Gain

Removes the between-class shift that flattens the pooled ratio.

Cost

Splits the shadow data $C$ ways, so each model sees fewer samples.

Trade-off

Conditioning helps only until the per-class estimate becomes noise.

Proposition 2 is about population laws. Finite data reintroduces the statistical gap.

The Confidence-Threshold Baseline

The pre-shadow attack: declare member when the model is confident.

$\mathcal{A}(\theta, z) = \mathbf{1}\bigl[s(x) \gt \tau\bigr], \qquad s(x) = \max_c \sigma(f_\theta(x))_c.$
  • No shadow models, no training, one scalar knob.
  • Often nearly as good as the full pipeline, which demands an explanation.

Assumption MLR

Assumption MLR (monotone likelihood ratio).

Let $p_1^s, p_0^s$ be the densities of $s(X)$ under member and non-member. Assume

$\Lambda_s(t) = \frac{p_1^s(t)}{p_0^s(t)} \quad \text{is non-decreasing in } t.$

In words: higher confidence is never weaker evidence of membership.

Proposition 3 — The Baseline Is a Ratio Test

Proposition 3.

Under Assumption MLR, for every $\alpha \in (0,1)$ there are $\tau$ and $\gamma \in [0,1]$ such that the test

$\phi(t) = \mathbf{1}[t \gt \tau] + \gamma\, \mathbf{1}[t = \tau]$

is most powerful at level $\alpha$ among all tests that observe only $s$.

Proof of Proposition 3

  • Neyman-Pearson on $(p_1^s, p_0^s)$ gives a most powerful test $\mathbf{1}[\Lambda_s(t) \gt k]$, randomized at ties.
  • Assumption MLR makes $\Lambda_s$ non-decreasing, so its super-level set is an upper interval:
$\{t : \Lambda_s(t) \gt k\} = (\tau, \infty), \qquad \tau = \inf\{t : \Lambda_s(t) \gt k\}.$
  • Calibrating $\gamma$ at $t = \tau$ to reach size exactly $\alpha$ completes the identification. $\;\square$

What the Baseline Gives Up

Assumption risk

If MLR fails, thresholding is strictly sub-optimal even among tests of $s$.

Information loss

$s = g(\varphi)$ is a deterministic map, so by data processing

$\mathrm{TV}(P_1^s, P_0^s) \le \mathrm{TV}(P_1, P_0).$

Same estimand, different observables, different assumptions.

One Axis, Not Two Recipes

confidence threshold shadow, pooled shadow, per class per-example calibration richer observable better $\Lambda$

LOGAN:
Generative Models

The discriminator is already a density ratio

LOGAN — The Setting

Target

A generative adversarial network trained on private images.

Problem

No labels, no confidence vector. The abstract observable $\varphi$ has to be reinvented.

Three access levels: samples only, generator weights, or the discriminator.

Hayes, Melis, Danezis, De Cristofaro, "LOGAN: Membership Inference Attacks Against Generative Models", PoPETs 2019

GAN Setup

$G$ pushes a latent $z \sim p_z$ to $G(z)$, inducing density $p_g$; $D$ scores realism.

$V(G, D) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\bigl[\log D(x)\bigr] \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\bigl[\log\bigl(1 - D(G(z))\bigr)\bigr].$

The game.

$\min_G \max_D V(G,D)$, with $D : \mathcal{X} \to (0,1)$ ranging over all measurable functions.

Goodfellow et al., "Generative Adversarial Nets", NeurIPS 2014

The Game, Drawn

$x \sim p_{\text{data}}$ $z \sim p_z$ $G(z) \sim p_g$ $D(x)$ real or fake

Theorem 2 — The Optimal Discriminator

Theorem 2 (Goodfellow et al., 2014).

Fix $G$ with induced density $p_g$. The maximiser of $V(G, \cdot)$ over measurable $D : \mathcal{X} \to (0,1)$ is

$D^\star(x) \;=\; \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)},$

uniquely, at every $x$ where $p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x) \gt 0$.

Proof of Theorem 2 — Step 1, Pointwise Form

Push the second expectation through the generator, so both integrals are over $\mathcal{X}$:

$V(G,D) \;=\; \int_{\mathcal{X}} \Bigl[\, p_{\text{data}}(x) \log D(x) \;+\; p_g(x) \log\bigl(1 - D(x)\bigr) \Bigr] \, dx.$
  • $D$ is unconstrained, so its value at each $x$ may be optimised separately.
  • The problem reduces to maximising $t \mapsto a \log t + b \log(1-t)$ with $a = p_{\text{data}}(x)$, $b = p_g(x)$.

Proof of Theorem 2 — Step 2, One Variable

$\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[a \log t + b \log(1-t)\bigr] = \frac{a}{t} - \frac{b}{1-t} = \frac{a - (a+b)t}{t(1-t)}.$
  • The derivative vanishes only at $t = a/(a+b)$.
  • Second derivative $-a/t^2 - b/(1-t)^2 \lt 0$ on $(0,1)$, so that point is the unique maximum.
  • Substituting $a = p_{\text{data}}(x)$ and $b = p_g(x)$ gives $D^\star$. $\;\square$

Theorem 3 — The Value Is a Divergence

Theorem 3 (Goodfellow et al., 2014).

$V(G, D^\star) \;=\; 2\,\mathrm{JSD}\bigl(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_g\bigr) \;-\; 2\log 2,$

where $\mathrm{JSD}(P\|Q) = \tfrac12 \mathrm{KL}(P\|M) + \tfrac12 \mathrm{KL}(Q\|M)$ and $M = \tfrac12(P+Q)$. The minimum over $G$ is $-2\log 2$, attained exactly when $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$.

Proof of Theorem 3 — Steps 1 and 2

  • Substitute $D^\star$ and write $m = \tfrac12(p_{\text{data}} + p_g)$, so $p_{\text{data}} + p_g = 2m$.
  • Each logarithm splits off a constant $\log 2$:
$\begin{aligned} V(G, D^\star) &= \int p_{\text{data}} \log \frac{p_{\text{data}}}{2m} + \int p_g \log \frac{p_g}{2m} \\ &= \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \| m) + \mathrm{KL}(p_g \| m) - 2 \log 2. \end{aligned}$

Proof of Theorem 3 — Step 3

  • By definition the two divergences sum to $2\,\mathrm{JSD}(p_{\text{data}} \| p_g)$, giving the identity.
  • $\mathrm{JSD} \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $p_{\text{data}} = p_g$, so the minimum value is $-2\log 2$. $\;\square$

Training a GAN is minimising a divergence; the discriminator is the machine that measures it.

Proposition 4 — Discriminator as Log-Ratio

Proposition 4.

With $\Lambda_D(x) = p_{\text{data}}(x)/p_g(x)$ and the logistic $\varsigma(t) = 1/(1+e^{-t})$,

$D^\star(x) \;=\; \varsigma\bigl(\log \Lambda_D(x)\bigr).$

Since $\varsigma$ is strictly increasing, thresholding $D^\star$ is the same as thresholding $\Lambda_D$.

Proof of Proposition 4

$D^\star(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x)+p_g(x)} \;\stackrel{(1)}{=}\; \frac{1}{1 + p_g(x)/p_{\text{data}}(x)} \;\stackrel{(2)}{=}\; \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\log \Lambda_D(x)}}.$

(1) divide numerator and denominator by $p_{\text{data}}(x)$  ·  (2) write the reciprocal ratio as an exponential. $\;\square$

The discriminator does not resemble a likelihood-ratio test. It is one, up to a fixed link.

Ratio, Seen

$p_{\text{data}}$ $p_g$ $D^\star(x)$ 1 0

Where the data density dominates, the discriminator saturates.

Why This Makes an Attack

$D$ never sees $p_{\text{data}}$, only the finite training set. Model this with a smoothed empirical density $\hat p_{\text{train}}$.

$D^\star(x) \;=\; \varsigma\Bigl(\log \frac{\hat p_{\text{train}}(x)}{p_g(x)}\Bigr).$

Reading.

The numerator is elevated exactly on training records. Membership rides in the numerator.

The Discriminator Attack

$\mathcal{A}(x) = \mathbf{1}\bigl[D(x) \gt \tau\bigr].$
  • White-box discriminator access: no shadow models needed at all.
  • Theorem 1 asked us to estimate a ratio; here the defender trained the estimator for us.
  • This is why discriminator access is the strongest LOGAN setting.

Caveat: $p_g$ is itself fit to the training set, so the null is only a proxy.

Black Box: Only Samples

With generator access alone, the ratio must be rebuilt from geometry. Use the reconstruction distance

$r(x) \;=\; \min_{z} \, \|x - G(z)\|, \qquad \mathcal{A}(x) = \mathbf{1}\bigl[r(x) \lt \tau\bigr].$
  • Intuition: a memorised record is reproducible by some latent code.
  • The next proposition turns that intuition into an inequality.

Proposition 5 — Reconstruction Bounds Density

Proposition 5.

Smooth the generator on $\mathbb{R}^m$ by $p_g^\sigma(x) = \int \mathcal{N}\bigl(x; G(z), \sigma^2 I\bigr) p_z(z)\, dz$. Then

$p_g^\sigma(x) \;\le\; (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-m/2} \exp\Bigl(-\frac{r(x)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr), \qquad r(x) = \inf_z \|x - G(z)\|.$

Proof of Proposition 5

  • For every $z$, $\|x - G(z)\| \ge r(x)$, so the Gaussian kernel obeys
$\mathcal{N}\bigl(x; G(z), \sigma^2 I\bigr) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-m/2} e^{-\|x - G(z)\|^2 / 2\sigma^2} \;\le\; (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-m/2} e^{-r(x)^2 / 2\sigma^2}.$
  • Free of $z$, and $p_z$ integrates to one, so the bound survives. $\;\square$

Equivalently $-\log p_g^\sigma(x) \ge r(x)^2/2\sigma^2 + \tfrac{m}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$.

What Reconstruction Is Missing

What it gives

A certificate: large $r(x)$ forces low generator likelihood. A numerator surrogate.

What it lacks

No null model: nothing says how large $r(x)$ is for a comparable non-member.

A one-sided statistic with no denominator. The calibration problem, in its first disguise.

Shadow GANs Restore the Denominator

Black-box LOGAN with shadows
  1. Train $k$ shadow GANs on data you own.
  2. Compute $r(x)$ for shadow members and shadow non-members.
  3. Fit a binary classifier to the labelled scores, which by Theorem 1 estimates the ratio.
  4. Apply the classifier to reconstruction scores from the target generator.

Same recipe as Shokri, with the observable changed from confidence to reconstruction.

The Sequence Challenge

No fixed observable

Output is a sequence, not a simplex point. No $|\mathcal{C}|$-vector to feed an attack model.

Enormous output space

Vocabulary raised to the length. Every plausible translation has tiny probability.

Membership signal must be squeezed out of a scalar score.

Hisamoto, Post, Duh, "Membership Inference Attacks on Sequence-to-Sequence Models", TACL 2020

Perplexity, Defined

For a source $x$ and a target $y = (y_1, \dots, y_T)$ under model $\theta$:

$\mathrm{PPL}(x,y) \;=\; \exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta\bigl(y_t \mid y_{\lt t}, x\bigr)\Bigr).$

Rewriting.

$-\log \mathrm{PPL}(x,y) = \frac{1}{T}\log p_\theta(y \mid x)$: the per-token log-likelihood, flipped and exponentiated.

Proposition 6 — Perplexity Is a Degenerate LRT

Proposition 6.

Let $S(x,y) = \frac{1}{T}\log p_\theta(y \mid x)$. Then

$\mathbf{1}\bigl[\mathrm{PPL}(x,y) \lt \tau\bigr] \;=\; \mathbf{1}\bigl[S(x,y) \gt -\log \tau\bigr],$

so under Assumption MLR it is most powerful among tests of $S$.

Data processing also gives $\mathrm{TV}(P_1^S, P_0^S) \le \mathrm{TV}(P_1, P_0)$.

Proof of Proposition 6

  • $\mathrm{PPL} = e^{-S}$ and $t \mapsto e^{-t}$ is strictly decreasing, so the two events coincide for every sample.
  • Proposition 3 applies verbatim with the confidence score replaced by $S$.
  • $S$ is a function of the observable; data processing gives the bound. $\;\square$

Nothing new in kind. The perplexity attack is the confidence threshold, in a different coordinate.

Optimal Only Within a Difficulty Class

Proposition 6 is conditional on the sentence: $S$ values are commensurable within, not across.

  • An easy sentence is scored high whether or not it was trained on.
  • A hard sentence is scored low either way.
  • A single global $\tau$ mixes both populations, exactly the pooling failure of Lemma 1.

The repair is a per-example null, not a better global threshold.

What a Global Threshold Sees

hard, out hard, in easy, out easy, in global $\tau$ score $S$

The threshold separates difficulty, not membership.

A Two-Group Model

Latent difficulty $g \in \{\text{easy}, \text{hard}\}$ with prior $\tfrac12$ each, membership $b \in \{0,1\}$:

$S \mid (g, b) \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\bigl(\mu_g + \Delta b, \; \sigma^2\bigr), \qquad \mu_{\text{easy}} \gt \mu_{\text{hard}}, \quad \Delta \gt 0.$
  • $\Delta$ is the membership signal, identical in both groups.
  • $\mu_{\text{easy}} - \mu_{\text{hard}}$ is the nuisance, carrying no membership information.

Proposition 7 — Pooling Costs Half the Population

Proposition 7.

Fix $\alpha \in (0, \tfrac12)$. In the two-group model:

$\begin{aligned} \text{(i)}\;\; &\text{calibrated test } \mathbf{1}[S - \mu_g \gt t]:\;\; \mathrm{TPR} = \Phi\bigl(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha) + \Delta/\sigma\bigr); \\ \text{(ii)}\;\; &\text{as } (\mu_{\text{easy}} - \mu_{\text{hard}})/\sigma \to \infty, \text{ the global-threshold test obeys} \\ &\mathrm{TPR} \to \tfrac12 \Phi\bigl(\Phi^{-1}(2\alpha) + \Delta/\sigma\bigr) \;\le\; \tfrac12 . \end{aligned}$

Proof of Proposition 7 — Part (i)

  • Given $g$, the centred score $S - \mu_g \sim \mathcal{N}(\Delta b, \sigma^2)$, a law free of $g$.
  • Size $\alpha$ therefore fixes $t = \sigma \Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)$ in both groups at once.
  • Power follows:
$\mathrm{TPR} = \Pr\bigl[\mathcal{N}(\Delta, \sigma^2) \gt \sigma\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)\bigr] = \Phi\bigl(\Delta/\sigma - \Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)\bigr) = \Phi\bigl(\Phi^{-1}(\alpha) + \Delta/\sigma\bigr).$

Proof of Proposition 7 — Part (ii), Size

A global threshold $\tau$ has false-positive rate

$\alpha \;=\; \tfrac12 \Phi\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{\text{easy}} - \tau}{\sigma}\Bigr) + \tfrac12 \Phi\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{\text{hard}} - \tau}{\sigma}\Bigr).$
  • Growing the gap kills the hard-group term at any $\tau$ of size $\alpha \lt \tfrac12$.
  • Hence $\Phi\bigl((\mu_{\text{easy}} - \tau)/\sigma\bigr) \to 2\alpha$, so $\tau \to \mu_{\text{easy}} - \sigma \Phi^{-1}(2\alpha)$.

Proof of Proposition 7 — Part (ii), Power

$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{TPR} &= \tfrac12 \Phi\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{\text{easy}} + \Delta - \tau}{\sigma}\Bigr) + \tfrac12 \Phi\Bigl(\frac{\mu_{\text{hard}} + \Delta - \tau}{\sigma}\Bigr) \\ &\longrightarrow \; \tfrac12 \Phi\bigl(\Phi^{-1}(2\alpha) + \Delta/\sigma\bigr) + 0 \;\le\; \tfrac12 . \end{aligned}$

The second term dies for the same reason the hard group left the size equation. $\;\square$

Half the population is structurally invisible, no matter how large the signal $\Delta$ is.

Length Is a Second Nuisance

If per-token log-probabilities have variance $v$ and are weakly dependent, then

$\mathrm{Var}\bigl[S(x,y)\bigr] \;\approx\; \frac{v}{T}.$
  • Short sequences give noisy scores; long ones give stable scores.
  • At a global threshold, the extreme tail is populated by short sentences whatever their membership.
  • Length shifts the null, exactly as difficulty did.

Variance Shrinks with Length

short long score spread mean false positives live here

Spread $\approx \sqrt{v/T}$: the tail belongs to short sequences.

Corollary 1, in the Wild

  • Shadow models translate worse than Alice, so shadow scores sit systematically low.
  • A classifier trained on that shifted law answers "in" almost always on Alice, landing at 50.
  • Subtracting the mean shift recovers 59–61 for the two linear-ish classifiers.

The mis-specification penalty of Corollary 1 is not a theoretical worry. It is the whole result.

What Is Missing Is a Null

Global threshold

One number for every record. Difficulty, length and domain all move the score.

Per-example null

Ask what this record's score would be had it not been trained on, then compare.

That comparison is the subject of the next two lectures.
