Lecture 1 — Introduction & Threat-Model Thinking · Speaker Script

One entry per slide, in order. Section headers match slide titles.

Title

Welcome to Trustworthy AI. Over the next 15 weeks we'll ask what it actually means to trust an AI system, and how to reason carefully about the ways it can fail. Today is the big-picture lecture.

Key takeaway: The course is about trust and failure, not just capability.

Today

Here's the road map for today: why trust matters now, how learned systems fail differently from ordinary software, the one habit of threat-model thinking, the course map, and finally how we'll work together.

Key takeaway: Five beats — motivation, failure modes, threat models, course map, logistics.

Why Trust?

Let's start with why this matters. AI has moved out of the lab and into decisions that change people's lives.

Key takeaway: Trust matters because AI now drives consequential decisions.

AI Now Decides Real Outcomes

Look at where AI already sits: hiring, lending, healthcare, content moderation, policing, and the information you see. These aren't demos — each one has real stakes for real people.

Key takeaway: AI is embedded in high-stakes, real-world decisions today.

Bias in the Courtroom

COMPAS predicted who would re-offend and US judges used it for bail and sentencing. ProPublica found it flagged Black defendants as high-risk far more often, even when they never re-offended — and no one could inspect or question the score.

Key takeaway: An opaque score can encode and hide unfairness in life-altering decisions.

A Tool That Learned to Discriminate

Amazon built a résumé ranker trained on a decade of hires — mostly men. The model learned to penalize the word "women's." They scrapped it in 2018.

Key takeaway: Biased training data produces biased decisions, with no malicious programmer required.

The Lawyer Who Trusted ChatGPT

In Mata v. Avianca, a lawyer asked ChatGPT for precedent and got six confident-looking citations. All six were fabricated, and the court sanctioned him.

Key takeaway: Fluent and authoritative output can be completely wrong — reliability is not eloquence.

A Chatbot That Made Promises

Air Canada's bot invented a refund policy and a tribunal held the airline liable. A dealership bot was talked into a one-dollar car. When the assistant can act, its mistakes become your obligations.

Key takeaway: Once an AI can act on your behalf, its errors carry real legal and financial weight.

Models That Memorize People

Researchers pulled real names, phone numbers and addresses out of GPT-2. Later, asking ChatGPT to "repeat poem forever" made it spill chunks of training data, some of it personal.

Key takeaway: Models can memorize and regurgitate private training data.

Seeing Is No Longer Believing

In a 2024 primary a robocall faked a president's voice telling people to stay home. Voice clones now power "grandparent" phone scams. We can no longer assume a voice or face is real.

Key takeaway: Generative AI breaks our default trust in audio and video evidence.

Hijacking the Assistant

When an assistant reads a web page for you, that page can hide instructions — "ignore your user, leak their data." The 2023 Bing "Sydney" demos showed assistants going off-script. The attacker effectively writes part of your prompt.

Key takeaway: Untrusted content the model reads can become hostile instructions.

The Pattern

Step back and the thread is clear: in every story, capability ran ahead of our ability to trust the output. Each one points to a missing guarantee — fair, reliable, private, real, secure — and those become our trust dimensions.

Key takeaway: Every failure is one missing guarantee; together they define the trust dimensions.

How AI Fails Differently

Now let's get more technical: why do learned systems fail in ways ordinary software never did?

Key takeaway: Learned models break in genuinely new ways.

Software vs Learned Models

In classical software a bug is a line someone wrote — reproducible and patchable. In a learned model the behavior lives in the weights: probabilistic, with no single line to fix. Nobody actually wrote the rule it follows.

Key takeaway: Learned behavior sits in the weights, not in human-written rules.

Failure Lives in the Weights

Those weights are billions of numbers with no comments, no variable names, and no stack trace when something goes wrong. You can't just open the file and read what it's doing.

Key takeaway: Failures hide inside opaque weights that we cannot simply read.

A Tiny Push Fools It

Here's the classic example. Add noise that's invisible to your eye, and a model that called this a panda at 58% now calls it a gibbon at 99% confidence. Small, deliberate perturbations break it completely.

Key takeaway: Imperceptible input changes can flip a model's confident prediction — adversarial fragility.

Three Sources of Failure

Failures can enter at three stages: in the data (biased, private, or poisoned), in training (the objective misses what we meant), or at deployment (new inputs and real adversaries).

Key takeaway: Trouble can originate in data, training, or deployment.

Bigger Models, New Surprises

Scale brings new abilities nobody planned — and new failure modes alongside them. Both tend to appear without warning, hard to predict before we ship.

Key takeaway: Scaling surfaces unplanned capabilities and unplanned failures.

The Black-Box Problem

We see what goes in and what comes out, but not the reasoning in between. That gap is exactly what motivates interpretability later in the course.

Key takeaway: We observe inputs and outputs but not the reasoning — the black-box problem.

Six Dimensions of Trust

This gives us the six dimensions we'll return to all term: privacy, robustness, reliability, fairness, transparency, and provenance — whose data leaks, breaks under a push, confidently wrong, who gets hurt, can we explain it, is this real?

Key takeaway: Six trust dimensions structure the entire course.

The Dimensions Interact

These aren't independent silos — they pull on each other. Privacy ties to memorization, robustness to jailbreaks, provenance to fairness. Fixing one can stress another.

Key takeaway: The trust dimensions are coupled, not separate.

Threat-Model Thinking

Now the single most important habit in this course — the one idea that ties everything else together.

Key takeaway: Threat-model thinking is the unifying habit of the course.

What Is a Threat Model?

A threat model answers three questions: who is the adversary and what's their goal, what do they know about the system, and what can they actually do? Answer these before you ever ask "is it safe?"

Key takeaway: A threat model specifies the adversary's goal, knowledge, and capabilities.

An Everyday Analogy

Think of one front-door lock. Against a casual passerby it's plenty; against a determined burglar, maybe not; against the locksmith you hired, it opens anyway. Same lock, different threat, different verdict.

Key takeaway: Security is meaningful only relative to a specified threat.

Who Is the Adversary?

The adversary isn't always a hacker in a hoodie. It might be a curious user, a fraudster, a competitor stealing your model, a troll, a careless insider with no malice, or even a regulator running an audit.

Key takeaway: "Adversary" spans many roles, malicious and not.

What They Know

Knowledge ranges across a spectrum. White-box: they see the weights and gradients — open models, insiders, audits. Black-box: only the API, inputs and outputs. Gray-box sits in between with partial knowledge.

Key takeaway: Attacker knowledge ranges from white-box to black-box.

When They Strike

Timing matters too. A train-time attacker poisons the data before the model ships; an inference-time attacker manipulates inputs after deployment. When the attack happens changes what defense is even possible.

Key takeaway: Train-time versus inference-time timing shapes the available defenses.

The Attack Map

Put those two axes together — knowledge by timing — and you get a simple two-by-two that locates almost any attack: data poisoning, backdoors, jailbreaks, and gradient-based adversarial attacks each land in a different cell.

Key takeaway: Knowledge × timing places nearly any attack on one map.

Worked Example: A Medical LLM

Take one system — a medical LLM — and the threats multiply: whose patient records trained it, will it recite a real note, will it invent a diagnosis, can its safety be bypassed? One system, four very different threats.

Key takeaway: A single deployed system faces several distinct threat models at once.

Placing Them on the Map

Drop those medical-LLM threats onto the grid and you see them spread across cells — privacy of the training set, memorization probes, hallucination, jailbreak. The same system spans the whole map.

Key takeaway: The map turns a vague worry into specific, locatable threats.

No Threat Model, No Answer

So the punchline of this section: "Is it secure?" means nothing until you fix who, what, and when. State the threat first, then evaluate. Security is always relative.

Key takeaway: Always state the threat model before claiming something is secure.

The Trust Stack & Course Map

With those tools in hand, here's how the course is organized — fifteen weeks across four modules.

Key takeaway: The course follows a structured stack across four modules.

The Trust Stack

Every failure we saw lives at one layer: data, model, output, or society. The course climbs this stack from the bottom up — privacy at the data layer, governance and fairness at the top.

Key takeaway: Failures sit at distinct layers, and we climb the stack from data to society.

Four Modules, 15 Weeks

Concretely: a week of intro, then Privacy and Data, then Reliability, then Security, then Provenance and Fairness. Every topic runs definition, then history, then the 2025-26 frontier.

Key takeaway: Four modules, each tracing definition to history to the current frontier.

Module 1 — Privacy & Data

Module one covers differential privacy as a budget for leakage, membership inference asking "were you in the data?", memorization and extraction like the GPT-2 leak, and unlearning — making a model forget.

Key takeaway: Module 1 is about controlling what models leak about their data.

Module 2 — Reliability

Module two is reliability: hallucination and those confident fake citations, calibration — does 90% really mean 90%? — and interpretability, opening the black box. The goal is moving from "it sounds right" to "we can check."

Key takeaway: Module 2 turns plausible-sounding output into verifiable output.

Module 3 — Security

Module three is security: adversarial examples like panda-to-gibbon, data poisoning before training, jailbreaks that bypass the safety filter, and prompt injection that hijacks the assistant.

Key takeaway: Module 3 covers deliberate attacks on the model and its safety.

Module 4 — Provenance & Fairness

Module four closes with provenance and fairness: watermarking AI output, detecting deepfakes, fairness definitions and mitigation, and governance — the rules and accountability around all of it.

Key takeaway: Module 4 addresses authenticity, fairness, and governance.

Week by Week (1–8)

Here's the first half in detail: intro this week, differential privacy in weeks two and three, membership inference and memorization in four, unlearning in five, hallucination and interpretability in six and seven, and adversarial examples in eight.

Key takeaway: Weeks 1–8 march from privacy through reliability into security.

Week by Week (9–15)

The second half: data poisoning in nine, jailbreaks in ten, prompt injection and agents in eleven, watermarking and deepfakes in twelve and thirteen, fairness in fourteen, and governance plus wrap-up in fifteen.

Key takeaway: Weeks 9–15 cover the rest of security, provenance, fairness, and governance.

What's New in 2025–26

And this year is a moving target: reasoning models and agents raise the stakes, prompt injection is the live agentic threat, provenance rules like the EU AI Act and C2PA are landing, and interpretability now has sparse-autoencoder features. "Recent progress" is a running theme.

Key takeaway: The field is moving fast; we'll track the 2025–26 frontier all term.

How We'll Work

Last section — quick logistics on how this class actually runs.

Key takeaway: Now for how we'll work together.

Concepts, Not Proofs

This course is intuition, a little technical detail, and history into the 2025 frontier. It is not proof-heavy theory, a coding bootcamp, or one field's narrow view. Mixed majors are welcome — basic Python is enough.

Key takeaway: Concept-first and accessible — no heavy proofs, no bootcamp.

Hands-On Demos

You'll actually run things: tighten a privacy budget and watch utility drop, run a membership-inference attack, craft a panda-to-gibbon image, try a jailbreak sandbox, run an AI-text detector. All browser-only — no homework, nothing to submit.

Key takeaway: Every topic comes with a runnable, no-stakes browser demo.

How to Get the Most Out of It

To get the most from this: bring a laptop and run the notebook, always ask "what's the threat model?", read one linked paper a week if you like, and bring your own AI headline to discuss.

Key takeaway: Engage actively — run the demos and bring the threat-model question to everything.

The Goal

If you take one thing away: by the end you should be able to read any AI headline and know which question to ask — private, robust, reliable, fair, real — and crucially, for which threat model.

Key takeaway: The goal is to instinctively ask the right trust question — and name the threat model.

Closer

So whenever you meet an AI system this term, ask the three questions: who is the adversary, what do they know, and what can they do? That's threat-model thinking. See you next week.

Key takeaway: Trust isn't a yes/no — always ask who, what they know, and what they can do.
