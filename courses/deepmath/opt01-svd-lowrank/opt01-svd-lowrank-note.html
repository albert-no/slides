1. SVD and Best Low-Rank Approximation

Message. Replace a matrix by one of lower rank, and say exactly how much is lost.

Lecture 10 of Deep Learning Math (150 minutes). The lecture asks one question: given a matrix, which matrix of rank at most $k$ is closest to it, and how close is it? The singular value decomposition answers it in closed form, and the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem proves that the answer cannot be beaten.

3. Part 1: Why Low Rank?

4. A Smaller Matrix, Controlled Error

Message. Low-rank approximation replaces a fully observed matrix $A$ by a matrix $B$ of rank at most $k$ and judges the result by the size of the residual $A - B$.

The setting: $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is given and every entry is observed; we choose $B$ with $\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k$. The slide shows one equation drawn as three boxed matrices: the data $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 0\\ 4 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ (rank 2, grey box) equals a rank-1 candidate $B = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}1.5 & 1.5\\ 4.5 & 4.5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ (blue box) plus the residual $A - B = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}1.5 & -1.5\\ -0.5 & 0.5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ (red dashed box). The decomposition $A = B + (A - B)$ holds for any $B$; what changes from one candidate to another is how big the residual is, and that size will be measured by a matrix norm (Part 2). This sets up the two questions for the lecture: which rank-$k$ matrix is closest, and how small can the error be. At this point $B$ is just one candidate; nothing yet says it is the best one.

Storage is a reason to want low rank, but not automatically a saving: a rank-$k$ matrix can be stored as two factors with $k(m+n)$ numbers, which beats the $mn$ entries of $A$ only when $k(m+n) \lt mn$. For the $2\times 2$ example with $k = 1$ both counts are $4$, so there is no saving at all; the gain appears for large matrices and small $k$.

Details

Full observation versus completion. Here every entry of $A$ is known and the residual is computed over all entries. Matrix completion is a different problem: only some entries are observed, the fit is measured on those, and the low-rank assumption fills in the rest. The methods of this lecture do not directly solve completion.

Why the problem is not convex. The feasible set $\{B : \operatorname{rank}(B) \le k\}$ is not convex: $\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ and $\operatorname{diag}(0,1)$ both have rank 1, but their midpoint $\tfrac12 I_2$ has rank 2. So convex-optimization guarantees do not apply; the lecture still reaches an exact answer through the SVD.

Check of the example. $B + (A - B) = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}1.5+1.5 & 1.5-1.5\\ 4.5-0.5 & 4.5+0.5\end{smallmatrix}\right] = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 0\\ 4 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right] = A$. Both columns of $B$ equal $(1.5, 4.5)$, so $\operatorname{rank}(B) = 1$.

Sources

Problem statement: Wilkinson, Multivariate Statistics (MATH3030 notes), §3.5 “Low-rank approximation”. The matrices $A$, $B$ and $A - B$ are our illustration (evidence E08); the storage count $k(m+n)$ is our arithmetic.

5. Recall: Span and Range

Message. Every output $Ax$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$, so the set of all outputs is the span of those columns.

Write $A = [\,a_1 \cdots a_n\,] \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with columns $a_j \in \mathbb{R}^m$, and take an input $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Multiplying out gives $Ax = x_1a_1 + \cdots + x_na_n$, a vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$: the entries of $x$ are the weights on the columns. The range (column space) collects every possible output, $\operatorname{range}(A) = \{Ax : x \in \mathbb{R}^n\} = \operatorname{span}\{a_1, \dots, a_n\}$, a subspace of the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$. The figure shows the case $m = n = 2$ with $a_1 = (2,1)$ and $a_2 = (1,2)$ as two blue arrows from the origin; the amber arrow is $Ax$ for $x = (1,1)$, the diagonal of the dashed parallelogram spanned by $a_1$ and $a_2$, i.e. $a_1 + a_2 = (3,3)$.

Details

The span of $v_1, \dots, v_k$ is the set of all combinations $\sum_i \alpha_i v_i$. A subspace is a nonempty set closed under addition and scalar multiplication; $\operatorname{range}(A)$ is one because $Ax + Ay = A(x+y)$ and $\alpha Ax = A(\alpha x)$. A basis is a linearly independent spanning list, and the dimension is its length (well defined by the Steinitz exchange lemma, proved in the backup note).

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, Mathematics for Machine Learning (CUP 2020), §2.7.3, Definition 2.23 and eq. (2.124): $\operatorname{Im}(A) = \{Ax\} = \operatorname{span}[a_1, \dots, a_n] \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$. Figure: our illustration.

6. Rank Counts Independent Directions

Message. The rank of $A$ is the dimension of its range: the number of independent output directions.

Definition: $\operatorname{rank}(A) = \dim \operatorname{range}(A)$, the number of vectors in any basis of the range. The block diagram shows what this means for the shape of $A$: if $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$ and $u_1, \dots, u_r$ is a basis of the range, then $A$ (a wide grey $m\times n$ block) factors as $U$ (a tall thin blue $m\times r$ block whose columns are the $u_i$) times $C$ (a short amber $r\times n$ block). Column $j$ of $C$ holds the coordinates of $a_j$ in that basis. The block sizes are schematic, not to scale. Rank is not the number of nonzero entries and not the number of columns: $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 1\\ 2 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ has four nonzero entries and two columns, yet both columns point along $(1,2)$, so its rank is 1.

Details

The factorization. Each column $a_j$ lies in $\operatorname{range}(A)$, so $a_j = Uc_j$ for a unique $c_j \in \mathbb{R}^r$. Stacking the columns gives $A = UC$ with $C = [c_1 \cdots c_n]$. Equivalently $A = \sum_{i=1}^r u_i \tilde c_i^\top$, a sum of $r$ rank-one matrices, where $\tilde c_i^\top$ is row $i$ of $C$.

Row rank equals column rank ($\operatorname{rank}(A^\top) = \operatorname{rank}(A)$) follows from the same factorization; the proof and the four-subspace picture are in the backup note.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §2.6.2 (rank as the number of linearly independent columns) and §2.7.3, eq. (2.124) ($\operatorname{rk}(A) = \dim \operatorname{Im}(A)$). The factorization $A = UC$ is our derivation from the definition.

7. Two Inputs Collapse to One Direction

Message. The matrix $C = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 1\\ 2 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ sends every input onto the single line through $(1,2)$, so its rank is 1.

Both columns of $C$ equal $(1,2)$, so $Cx = x_1(1,2) + x_2(1,2) = (x_1 + x_2)(1,2)$: whatever the input, the output is a multiple of $(1,2)$. The figure has two coordinate panels joined by an arrow labelled $C$. The left (input) panel marks $e_1$ and $e_2$ as blue dots and $(1,1)$ as an amber square. The right (output) panel shows the range as a dashed light-blue line through the origin with slope 2; $e_1$ and $e_2$ both land on the same blue dot $(1,2)$, and $(1,1)$ lands on the amber square $(2,4)$, further out on the same line. All points are drawn at their exact coordinates. Two independent inputs produce only one output direction: $\operatorname{range}(C) = \operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}$ and $\operatorname{rank}(C) = 1$.

Details

Image calculation: $Ce_1 = (1,2)$, $Ce_2 = (1,2)$, $C(1,1) = (2,4) = 2\cdot(1,2)$. Since every output is $(x_1 + x_2)(1,2)$ and $x_1 + x_2$ can be any real number, the range is the whole line $\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}$, not just a segment. A basis of the range is $\{(1,2)\}$, so the dimension is 1.

8. Null Space Means Lost Directions

Message. The null space collects the input directions that $A$ sends to zero; for $C$ it is the line spanned by $(1,-1)$.

Definition: $\operatorname{null}(A) = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : Ax = 0\}$, a set of inputs. For $C$, $Cx = (x_1 + x_2)(1,2)$ vanishes exactly when $x_1 + x_2 = 0$, so $\operatorname{null}(C) = \operatorname{span}\{(1,-1)\}$. The figure repeats the two panels of the previous slide. In the input panel a solid red line of slope $-1$ through the origin is the null space; a dashed red arrow from $e_2$ to $e_1$ is the vector $e_1 - e_2 = (1,-1)$, which lies along that line. In the output panel the origin is a red dot labelled $C(1,-1) = 0$, and $Ce_1 = Ce_2$ is a single blue dot on the dashed range line. The point: $e_1$ and $e_2$ differ by a null vector, so $C$ cannot tell them apart. The null space lives in the input space $\mathbb{R}^n$; it is not a set of outputs and not part of the range.

Details

$\operatorname{null}(A)$ is a subspace: if $Ax = 0$ and $Ay = 0$ then $A(x + y) = 0$ and $A(\alpha x) = 0$. Inputs differing by a null vector give the same output: $A(x + z) = Ax$ when $z \in \operatorname{null}(A)$. For $C$, solving $x_1 + x_2 = 0$ gives $x = t(1,-1)$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$; one free parameter, so $\dim \operatorname{null}(C) = 1$.

Sources

Definition: Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §2.7.3, Definition 2.23 (kernel/null space). Example: our calculation (evidence E09).

9. Rank and Nullity Add Up

Message. For any $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ the kept directions and the lost directions add up to the number of columns $n$, so low rank forces a large null space.

Rank–nullity theorem: for every $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, square or rectangular, $\operatorname{rank}(A) + \dim \operatorname{null}(A) = n$. The count is over columns, i.e. input dimensions, not rows. The bar below the theorem splits all $n$ input dimensions into a solid blue part labelled “rank: kept” and a dashed red part labelled “nullity: lost”; the widths are schematic. Check on $C$: rank 1 plus nullity 1 equals 2 columns. For a wide matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 3}$ the rank is at most 2 (the range sits inside $\mathbb{R}^2$), so the nullity is at least 1: some nonzero input is always lost. This is the fact used later for an arbitrary competitor $B$: if $\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k$ then $\dim \operatorname{null}(B) \ge n - k$.

Details

Proof. Let $x_1, \dots, x_t$ be a basis of $\operatorname{null}(A)$ and extend it to a basis $x_1, \dots, x_t, y_1, \dots, y_s$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, so $t + s = n$. It suffices to show that $Ay_1, \dots, Ay_s$ is a basis of $\operatorname{range}(A)$. Spanning: any $x = \sum_i \alpha_i x_i + \sum_j \beta_j y_j$ has $Ax = \sum_j \beta_j Ay_j$, because $Ax_i = 0$. Independence: if $\sum_j \beta_j Ay_j = 0$ then $\sum_j \beta_j y_j \in \operatorname{null}(A)$, so it equals some $\sum_i \alpha_i x_i$; this is a linear relation among basis vectors of $\mathbb{R}^n$, so every $\beta_j = 0$. Hence $\operatorname{rank}(A) = s = n - t$. $\blacksquare$

Corollary used later. $\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k$ gives $\dim \operatorname{null}(B) = n - \operatorname{rank}(B) \ge n - k$.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §2.7.3, Theorem 2.24 (rank–nullity, stated for a linear map $V \to W$: $\dim \ker + \dim \operatorname{Im} = \dim V$; here $V = \mathbb{R}^n$ and the map is $x \mapsto Ax$). Proof: standard basis-extension argument, retained from the previous version of this note.

11. What Makes a Norm?

Message. A norm is a size function that is zero only at zero, scales with $|\alpha|$, and obeys the triangle inequality.

Definition: a norm on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a function $\|\cdot\| : \mathbb{R}^n \to [0,\infty)$ such that for all $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$: $\|x\| = 0$ if and only if $x = 0$; $\|\alpha x\| = |\alpha|\,\|x\|$ (the absolute value means flipping the sign does not change the size); and $\|x + y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|$. The sketch shows the triangle inequality: a solid blue arrow $x$ followed by a solid blue arrow $y$ (the detour), and a dashed amber arrow $x + y$ from the start of $x$ to the tip of $y$ (the direct route). The direct route is never longer than the detour. These three rules are all we need in this lecture; we use norms to measure residuals, not to study them for their own sake.

Details

Norm versus squared norm. The squared Euclidean norm $\|x\|_2^2$ is not a norm. It fails homogeneity, since $\|2x\|_2^2 = 4\|x\|_2^2$ rather than $2\|x\|_2^2$, and it fails the triangle inequality: for $x = y = e_1$, $\|x + y\|_2^2 = 4 \gt 2 = \|x\|_2^2 + \|y\|_2^2$. Squared norms are still useful as objectives (they are smooth and minimized by the same point), which is why the Frobenius “energy” is often written squared later.

Nonnegativity follows from the other rules: $0 = \|x + (-x)\| \le \|x\| + \|-x\| = 2\|x\|$. A consequence used for continuity arguments is the reverse triangle inequality $\big|\|x\| - \|y\|\big| \le \|x - y\|$.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §3.1, Definition 3.1 (absolutely homogeneous, triangle inequality, positive definite). Squared-norm counterexample: our calculation.

12. Three Familiar Vector Norms

Message. The $\ell_1$, $\ell_2$ and $\ell_\infty$ norms measure different aspects of the same coordinate vector.

For $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ the slide shows three side-by-side cards. $\ell_1$, the absolute sum $\|x\|_1 = \sum_i |x_i|$: the distance walked along the coordinate axes. $\ell_2$, the Euclidean norm $\|x\|_2 = (\sum_i x_i^2)^{1/2}$: the straight-line length. $\ell_\infty$, the maximum $\|x\|_\infty = \max_i |x_i|$: the largest single coordinate. Only $\ell_2$ is geometric length. Do not confuse it with the sum of absolute coordinates: $x = (1,1)$ has $\|x\|_1 = 2$ but $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{2}$.

Details

These are three members of the $\ell_p$ family $\|x\|_p = (\sum_i |x_i|^p)^{1/p}$ for $p \ge 1$, with $\ell_\infty$ the limit as $p \to \infty$. For $p \lt 1$ the formula is not a norm: with $p = \tfrac12$, $x = (1,0)$, $y = (0,1)$ we get $\|x\|_{1/2} = \|y\|_{1/2} = 1$ but $\|x+y\|_{1/2} = (1+1)^2 = 4 \gt 2$. The proof that $\ell_p$ is a norm for $p \ge 1$ (Young, Hölder, Minkowski) is in the backup note.

Sources

$\ell_1$ (Manhattan) and $\ell_2$ (Euclidean): Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §3.1, Examples 3.1 and 3.2. $\ell_p$: Wilkinson, MATH3030 notes, §2.3.1, Definition 2.9. $\ell_\infty = \max_i |x_i|$: standard definition, not found in either evidence source (flagged). The $(1,1)$ and $p = \tfrac12$ numbers: our calculation.

13. One Vector, Three Answers: 7, 5, 4

Message. For $x = (3,4)$ the three norms report three different sizes, 7, 5 and 4, so the choice of norm is part of the question.

Arithmetic: $\|x\|_1 = |3| + |4| = 7$; $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$; $\|x\|_\infty = \max(3, 4) = 4$. The figure is drawn to scale on a grid with the origin at bottom left and the point $x = (3,4)$ marked. The amber dashed path walks 3 to the right and then 4 up: its total length, $3 + 4 = 7$, is $\ell_1$. The solid blue diagonal from the origin straight to $x$ has length 5: that is $\ell_2$. A red bracket beside the vertical leg marks the longer coordinate, 4: that is $\ell_\infty$. For every vector, $\|x\|_\infty \le \|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_1$, here $4 \le 5 \le 7$.

Details

Why the ordering always holds. $\|x\|_\infty^2 = \max_i x_i^2 \le \sum_i x_i^2 = \|x\|_2^2$. And $\|x\|_1^2 = \sum_i x_i^2 + \sum_{i \ne j} |x_i||x_j| \ge \|x\|_2^2$. Taking square roots of nonnegative quantities gives $\|x\|_\infty \le \|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_1$.

Interpretation: $\ell_1$ adds up every coordinate, $\ell_\infty$ looks only at the worst one, and $\ell_2$ sits between them. The same contrast between “worst case” and “total” reappears for matrices as spectral norm versus Frobenius norm.

14. Unit Balls Reveal the Metric

Message. The unit ball $\{x : \|x\| \le 1\}$ is the set of vectors a norm calls at most 1, and its shape shows how the norm measures.

The figure has three panels in the plane, all at the same scale with axes crossing at the centre. Left: the $\ell_1$ ball is a diamond with corners on the axes at $(\pm1, 0)$ and $(0, \pm1)$. Middle: the $\ell_2$ ball is a round disc of radius 1. Right: the $\ell_\infty$ ball is a square with corners at $(\pm1, \pm1)$. Each panel marks one boundary point with an amber dot: $(1,0)$ on the diamond ($|1| + |0| = 1$), $(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$ on the circle ($\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ under the square root), and the corner $(1,1)$ on the square ($\max(1,1) = 1$). Each marked point has norm exactly 1 in its own panel's norm. The shapes are nested: diamond inside circle inside square. The panels are labelled by shape name as well as by norm, so the identification does not rely on colour.

Details

Nesting. From $\|x\|_\infty \le \|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_1$: if $\|x\|_1 \le 1$ then $\|x\|_2 \le 1$, and if $\|x\|_2 \le 1$ then $\|x\|_\infty \le 1$. So the $\ell_1$ ball lies inside the $\ell_2$ ball, which lies inside the $\ell_\infty$ ball. The point $(1,1)$ is in the square but not in the circle ($\|(1,1)\|_2 = \sqrt2 \gt 1$).

15. Orthogonal Maps Preserve Geometry

Message. A square matrix $Q$ with $Q^\top Q = I$ turns vectors without changing their Euclidean length.

Definition: $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is orthogonal if $Q^\top Q = I = QQ^\top$; equivalently its columns are orthonormal, and $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$. The figure shows a dashed grey unit circle. A blue arrow is $e_1$; an amber arrow is $Qe_1$ for the $45^\circ$ rotation $Q = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & -1\\ 1 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, so $Qe_1 = (\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$; a small arc marks the $45^\circ$ angle between them. Both tips sit on the unit circle: the direction changed, the Euclidean length stayed 1. Squareness matters. The tall matrix $W = [e_1\; e_2] \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}$ has orthonormal columns, so $W^\top W = I_2$, but $WW^\top = \operatorname{diag}(1,1,0) \ne I_3$; it is not an orthogonal matrix. And the protection is for $\ell_2$ only, not every norm (next slide).

Details

For square $Q$, $Q^\top Q = I$ already implies $QQ^\top = I$ (a square matrix with a left inverse is invertible, and the inverse is that left inverse). Further equivalent characterizations, orthonormal rows, $\det Q = \pm1$, and Gram–Schmidt are in the backup note. Check of the example: the columns $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1,1)$ each have length 1 and dot product $\tfrac12(-1+1) = 0$.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §3.4, Definition 3.8 (orthogonal matrix: square, $AA^\top = I = A^\top A$). Rotation and $W$ examples: our illustration.

16. Why Length Is Preserved

Message. One line of algebra using $Q^\top Q = I$ shows that a square orthogonal $Q$ preserves the Euclidean norm of every vector.

Claim: for square $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ with $Q^\top Q = I$ and every $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $\|Qx\|_2 = \|x\|_2$. Proof: $\|Qx\|_2^2 = (Qx)^\top(Qx) = x^\top Q^\top Q x = x^\top x = \|x\|_2^2$, where the middle step uses $Q^\top Q = I$; both norms are nonnegative, so taking square roots gives the claim. The same step with two vectors gives $(Qx)^\top(Qy) = x^\top y$, so inner products, and hence angles, are preserved too. The protection is specific to the Euclidean norm: for the $45^\circ$ rotation, $\|Qe_1\|_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} = \sqrt2$, while $\|e_1\|_1 = 1$.

Details

The proof uses only $Q^\top Q = I$, not squareness, so it also shows that a tall matrix with orthonormal columns (like $W$ on the previous slide) preserves Euclidean length. Squareness is needed for the converse direction and for $QQ^\top = I$. Conversely, a square $Q$ with $\|Qx\|_2 = \|x\|_2$ for all $x$ is orthogonal, by the polarization identity $x^\top y = \tfrac12(\|x+y\|_2^2 - \|x\|_2^2 - \|y\|_2^2)$; details in the backup note.

Sources

Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, MML (CUP 2020), §3.4, Definition 3.8 and eqs. (3.31) (length preserved) and (3.32) (angles preserved). $\ell_1$ counterexample: our calculation.

17. Spectral Norm: Worst Stretch

Message. The spectral norm of a matrix is the largest factor by which it stretches any unit-length input.

Definition: for $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $\|A\|_2 = \max_{x \in \mathbb{R}^n,\ \|x\|_2 = 1} \|Ax\|_2$. It is one number for the whole matrix. The figure uses the running example $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 0\\ 4 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. On the left is the input unit circle (drawn three times larger than the output scale so it is visible), with one input $x^\star$ marked by a blue radius. An arrow labelled $A$ leads to the right panel, where the image of the circle under $A$ is a tall, tilted ellipse drawn to scale. The red radius from the centre to the ellipse's farthest point is $Ax^\star$, the longest image: its length is $\|A\|_2$. The picture is only intuition for “worst direction”; how to compute that length comes later in the lecture. Keep three things apart: $\|A\|_2$ is a matrix norm; $\|x\|_2$ is the length of a vector; and the $\ell_2$ norm of all entries of $A$ is a different matrix norm, the Frobenius norm (next slide).

Details

Figure geometry. The ellipse is the exact image $\{A(\cos t, \sin t)\} = \{(3\cos t,\ 4\cos t + 5\sin t)\}$. The marked input is $x^\star = (1,1)/\sqrt2$, and $Ax^\star = (3, 9)/\sqrt2$; a numerical scan of $t$ confirms this is where $\|A(\cos t, \sin t)\|_2$ is largest. The numerical value is deliberately not stated on the slide; it is derived with the singular values later.

The maximum is attained because $x \mapsto \|Ax\|_2$ is continuous and the unit sphere is compact. Equivalently $\|A\|_2 = \max_{x \ne 0} \|Ax\|_2 / \|x\|_2$, by homogeneity. Other induced norms ($p = 1, \infty$) and submultiplicativity $\|AB\|_2 \le \|A\|_2\|B\|_2$ are in the backup note.

18. Frobenius Norm: Total Energy

Message. The Frobenius norm adds up every squared entry, so $\|A - B\|_F^2$ is the total squared error over all entries.

Definition: $\|A\|_F = (\sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}^2)^{1/2}$, and for a residual $\|A - B\|_F^2 = \sum_{i,j} (a_{ij} - b_{ij})^2$. The figure is a $2\times 2$ grid of tiles, one per entry of the residual $A - B$ from the opening example, each showing its square: the top row $1.5^2 = 2.25$ and $(-1.5)^2 = 2.25$ in dark red solid tiles, the bottom row $(-0.5)^2 = 0.25$ and $0.5^2 = 0.25$ in pale red dashed tiles; darker means a larger contribution. The sum is $5$, so $\|A - B\|_F = \sqrt5$. Qualifiers: every entry is observed and counts equally (no weights), and the Frobenius norm is not the spectral norm in general. For the identity $I_2$, $\|I_2\|_2 = 1$ (it stretches nothing) but $\|I_2\|_F = \sqrt2$.

Details

Square root versus square. The norm is the square root, which makes it a norm (it is the Euclidean norm of the matrix read as a vector of $mn$ numbers). The squared version is the “energy”: it is additive over entries, which is why least-squares objectives use it.

Trace form (optional). $\|A\|_F^2 = \operatorname{tr}(A^\top A)$, since the diagonal entries of $A^\top A$ are the squared column lengths.

Arithmetic. $2.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 5$; $\sqrt5 \approx 2.236$.

20. Recall — Eigendecomposition

4.1 The one imported black box

Spectral theorem (imported). Every symmetric $S \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ can be written $S = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ real. If moreover $S$ is positive semidefinite ($x^{\top}Sx \geq 0$ for all $x$) then every $\lambda_i \geq 0$.

This is the single result these notes do not prove; it was used in prob08 for covariance matrices and is proved in any of [5, 6, 8]. (Sketch, for orientation: the maximum of the continuous function $x \mapsto x^{\top}Sx$ on the compact unit sphere is attained at some $q_1$, which is an eigenvector by a Lagrange-multiplier or first-order argument; $S$ maps $q_1^{\perp}$ into itself by symmetry, so induct on dimension.) The PSD claim is one line: $\lambda_i = q_i^{\top}Sq_i \geq 0$. Everything else below is proved.

21. Theorem 5 — the SVD Exists

4.2 Theorem 5 — existence of the SVD, proved

Theorem 5. Every $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$ admits $A = U\Sigma V^{\top}$ with $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ orthogonal and $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ “diagonal” ($\Sigma_{ij} = 0$ for $i \neq j$) with diagonal entries $\sigma_1 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r \gt 0 = \sigma_{r+1} = \cdots$.

Proof. The matrix $S = A^{\top}A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is symmetric ($S^{\top} = A^{\top}A$) and PSD ($x^{\top}Sx = \|Ax\|_2^2 \geq 0$). By the spectral theorem write $S = V \Lambda V^{\top}$ with $V = [v_1 \cdots v_n]$ orthogonal and $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n \geq 0$ (reorder the columns of $V$ to sort $\Lambda$; reordering preserves orthogonality).

Step 1: the number of positive eigenvalues is $r$. $\|Av_i\|_2^2 = v_i^{\top}Sv_i = \lambda_i$, so $Av_i = 0$ exactly when $\lambda_i = 0$. Hence $\operatorname{null}(A) \supseteq \operatorname{span}\{v_i : \lambda_i = 0\}$; conversely $Ax = 0 \Rightarrow Sx = 0$, and $Sx = 0$ with $x = \sum_i c_iv_i$ gives $\sum_i c_i\lambda_iv_i = 0$, so $c_i = 0$ whenever $\lambda_i \neq 0$. Thus $\operatorname{null}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{v_i : \lambda_i = 0\}$, and by Theorem 1 the count of positive $\lambda_i$ is $n - \dim\operatorname{null}(A) = r$. So $\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_r \gt 0 = \lambda_{r+1} = \cdots = \lambda_n$.

Step 2: define $\sigma_i$ and $u_i$. For $i \leq r$ set $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i} \gt 0$ and $u_i = Av_i / \sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}^m$. These are orthonormal:

\[ u_i^{\top}u_j = \frac{v_i^{\top}A^{\top}Av_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \frac{v_i^{\top}(\lambda_j v_j)}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \frac{\lambda_j\,\delta_{ij}}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \delta_{ij}. \]

Since $r \leq m$, extend $u_1, \dots, u_r$ to an orthonormal basis $u_1, \dots, u_m$ of $\mathbb{R}^m$ (Lemma 2.5 applied to a completion to a basis) and set $U = [u_1 \cdots u_m]$, which is orthogonal by Proposition 3.5.

Step 3: verify $AV = U\Sigma$. Let $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ have $\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i$ for $i \leq r$ and zeros elsewhere. Column $i$ of $AV$ is $Av_i$, which equals $\sigma_iu_i$ for $i \leq r$ (by definition of $u_i$) and $0$ for $i \gt r$ (Step 1). Column $i$ of $U\Sigma$ is $\sum_j u_j \Sigma_{ji} = \sigma_iu_i$ for $i \leq r$ and $0$ otherwise. The columns agree, so $AV = U\Sigma$, and right-multiplying by $V^{\top}$ gives $A = U\Sigma V^{\top}$. $\blacksquare$

Two remarks the deck compresses into “the $A^{\top}A$ route.” First, the proof is constructive and is exactly the by-hand recipe on the slides: form $A^{\top}A$, eigendecompose, take square roots, push through $A$. Second, it is a terrible numerical recipe: forming $A^{\top}A$ squares the condition number, so library implementations (Golub–Kahan bidiagonalization, [8, ch. 8]) never do this. The hand computation is for understanding, not for code — in practice one calls numpy.linalg.svd.

4.3 What is unique and what is not

Proposition 4.1. The singular values are unique: they are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^{\top}A$, sorted. The singular vectors are not.

Proof of the first claim. If $A = U\Sigma V^{\top}$ then $A^{\top}A = V\Sigma^{\top}\Sigma V^{\top}$, an eigendecomposition with eigenvalues $\sigma_i^2$; eigenvalues of a fixed matrix are the roots of its characteristic polynomial, hence determined by $A$ alone. $\blacksquare$

Non-uniqueness of the vectors has two sources. Signs: replacing $(u_i, v_i)$ by $(-u_i, -v_i)$ changes nothing, since $\sigma_i(-u_i)(-v_i)^{\top} = \sigma_iu_iv_i^{\top}$. Repeated singular values: if $\sigma_i = \sigma_j$ for $i \neq j$, any rotation within the corresponding two-dimensional eigenspace of $A^{\top}A$ yields another valid SVD — for $A = I_2$ every orthogonal $V$ works, with $U = V$. Additionally the columns $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$ are constrained only to complete an orthonormal basis. This is why the deck's Section 07 slide drops the source's uniqueness claim for the minimizer in Theorem 8: non-uniqueness enters here, at the level of the decomposition itself, and Section 7.6 shows it survives into the approximation problem.

4.8 The geometry: sphere to ellipsoid

Proposition 4.3. $A$ maps the unit sphere $\{x : \|x\|_2 = 1\}$ onto the ellipsoid $\{\sum_{i \leq r} c_iu_i : \sum_{i\leq r}c_i^2/\sigma_i^2 \leq 1\}$, degenerating to a filled ellipsoid in $\operatorname{range}(A)$ when $r \lt n$, with semi-axes $\sigma_iu_i$.

4.4 Thin SVD, the rank-one expansion, and the four subspaces again

Discarding the columns of $U$ and $V$ that meet only zeros in $\Sigma$ gives the thin (compact) SVD $A = U_c\Sigma_cV_c^{\top}$ with $U_c \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times r}$, $\Sigma_c = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r)$ invertible, $V_c \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times r}$, and equivalently

\[ A \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^{\top}, \]

4.7 Eigenvalues versus singular values

For the running matrix, which is lower triangular, the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries $3$ and $5$; the singular values are $6.708$ and $2.236$. Not equal, not even close, and not the same in count-of-agreement: the products agree, $3\cdot5 = 15 = |\det A| = \sigma_1\sigma_2 = 3\sqrt5\cdot\sqrt5$, which is general.

Proposition 4.2. For square $A$: (a) $\prod_i \sigma_i = |\det A| = \prod_i|\lambda_i|$; (b) $\max_i |\lambda_i| \leq \sigma_1$, with equality for all $i$ simultaneously iff $A$ is normal ($A^{\top}A = AA^{\top}$), in particular iff symmetric in the real symmetric case.

(b) If $Ax = \lambda x$ with $\|x\| = 1$ then $|\lambda| = \|Ax\|_2 \leq \max_{\|y\|=1}\|Ay\|_2 = \sigma_1$ by Theorem 6. 45. Theorem 8 — Eckart–Young–Mirsky

7.3 Theorem 8 — the spectral-norm case, proved

Theorem 8 (Eckart–Young, spectral norm). For every $B$ with $\operatorname{rank}(B) \leq k$,  $\|A - B\|_2 \geq \sigma_{k+1} = \|A - A_k\|_2$.

Proof. By Theorem 1, $\dim\operatorname{null}(B) \geq n - k$. Let $W = \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_{k+1}\}$, of dimension $k+1$. Then

\[ \dim\big(\operatorname{null}(B) \cap W\big) \;\geq\; (n-k) + (k+1) - n \;=\; 1, \]

so choose a unit vector $x \in \operatorname{null}(B)\cap W$. Since $Bx = 0$,

\[ \|A - B\|_2^2 \;\geq\; \|(A-B)x\|_2^2 \;=\; \|Ax\|_2^2 \;=\; \Big\|\sum_{i \leq k+1}\sigma_i (v_i^{\top}x)\,u_i\Big\|_2^2 \;=\; \sum_{i\leq k+1}\sigma_i^2(v_i^{\top}x)^2 \;\geq\; \sigma_{k+1}^2, \]

using $x \in W$ (so $v_i^{\top}x = 0$ for $i \gt k+1$), orthonormality of the $u_i$, and $\sum_{i\leq k+1}(v_i^{\top}x)^2 = \|x\|_2^2 = 1$ with $\sigma_i \geq \sigma_{k+1}$ throughout. Since $A_k$ attains $\sigma_{k+1}$, it is optimal. $\blacksquare$

The result is usually attributed to Eckart and Young [1], who proved the Frobenius case in 1936; Mirsky [2] gave the general unitarily-invariant statement in 1960, and Erhard Schmidt [3] had proved the analogous theorem for integral operators already in 1907. See [9] for the tangled history of who proved what.

2.3 The dimension formula — Technique Review 2, proved

Theorem 2.3. For subspaces $W_1, W_2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$,

\[ \dim(W_1 + W_2) + \dim(W_1 \cap W_2) \;=\; \dim W_1 + \dim W_2 , \]

where $W_1 + W_2 = \{w_1 + w_2 : w_i \in W_i\}$.

Proof. Let $z_1, \dots, z_t$ be a basis of $W_1 \cap W_2$. Extend it to a basis $z_1, \dots, z_t, x_1, \dots, x_p$ of $W_1$ and to a basis $z_1, \dots, z_t, y_1, \dots, y_q$ of $W_2$, so $\dim W_1 = t + p$, $\dim W_2 = t + q$. The combined list $z_\bullet, x_\bullet, y_\bullet$ spans $W_1 + W_2$; we claim it is independent. Suppose $\sum \gamma_i z_i + \sum \alpha_j x_j + \sum \beta_\ell y_\ell = 0$. Then $v := \sum_\ell \beta_\ell y_\ell = -\sum \gamma_i z_i - \sum \alpha_j x_j$ lies in $W_1$ and in $W_2$, hence in $W_1 \cap W_2$, so $v = \sum_i \delta_i z_i$ for some $\delta$. But $\sum_\ell \beta_\ell y_\ell - \sum_i \delta_i z_i = 0$ with $z_\bullet, y_\bullet$ independent forces all $\beta_\ell = 0$ (and all $\delta_i = 0$). The relation collapses to $\sum \gamma_i z_i + \sum \alpha_j x_j = 0$, and independence in $W_1$ kills the rest. Hence $\dim(W_1+W_2) = t + p + q$, and $(t+p+q) + t = (t+p) + (t+q)$. $\blacksquare$

Corollary 2.4 (the deck's TR2). $\dim(W_1 \cap W_2) \geq \dim W_1 + \dim W_2 - n$. In particular if $\dim W_1 + \dim W_2 \gt n$ then $W_1 \cap W_2$ contains a nonzero vector.

Proof. $W_1 + W_2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ gives $\dim(W_1+W_2) \leq n$; substitute into Theorem 2.3. $\blacksquare$

7.2 Courant–Fischer for singular values

Both proofs below rest on a variational characterization; it is proved here rather than cited because everything downstream is a two-line consequence of it.

Lemma 7.1 (min–max / max–min). For $1 \leq i \leq \min(m,n)$,

\[ \sigma_i(A) \;=\; \max_{\substack{S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \\ \dim S = i}} \ \min_{\substack{x \in S \\ \|x\|_2 = 1}} \|Ax\|_2 \;=\; \min_{\substack{T \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \\ \dim T = n-i+1}} \ \max_{\substack{x \in T \\ \|x\|_2 = 1}} \|Ax\|_2 . \]

Proof (first equality; the second is analogous). ($\geq$ for the max) Take $S_0 = \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_i\}$. For unit $x \in S_0$, $x = \sum_{j\leq i}c_jv_j$ with $\sum c_j^2 = 1$, and $\|Ax\|_2^2 = \sum_{j \leq i}\sigma_j^2c_j^2 \geq \sigma_i^2$ since $\sigma_j \geq \sigma_i$ for $j \leq i$. So the inner min over $S_0$ is $\geq \sigma_i$ (indeed $=\sigma_i$, at $x = v_i$).

($\leq$ for every $S$) Let $\dim S = i$ and $W = \operatorname{span}\{v_i, v_{i+1},\dots,v_n\}$, $\dim W = n-i+1$. By Theorem 2's dimension count, $\dim(S \cap W) \geq \dim S + \dim W - n = 1$, so there is a unit $x \in S\cap W$. Writing $x = \sum_{j \geq i}c_jv_j$, $\|Ax\|_2^2 = \sum_{j\geq i}\sigma_j^2c_j^2 \leq \sigma_i^2$. Hence the inner min over $S$ is $\leq \sigma_i$. $\blacksquare$

Note the mechanism: a dimension count forces two subspaces to intersect. That single idea drives Theorem 8, Lemma 7.3, and Theorem 9 alike.

It is proved here in full, because the Frobenius case — not the spectral one — is what PCA, latent-factor recommenders, and low-rank compression actually minimize. The route is Weyl's inequality for singular values.

Lemma 7.2 (Weyl, additive). For $X, Y \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $i, j \geq 1$ with $i + j - 1 \leq \min(m,n)$:  $\sigma_{i+j-1}(X+Y) \leq \sigma_i(X) + \sigma_j(Y)$.

Proof. By the min–max form of Lemma 7.1 there are subspaces $T_X$ of dimension $n-i+1$ and $T_Y$ of dimension $n-j+1$ with $\max_{x\in T_X, \|x\|=1}\|Xx\|_2 = \sigma_i(X)$ and likewise for $Y$. Put $T = T_X \cap T_Y$, so

\[ \dim T \;\geq\; (n-i+1) + (n-j+1) - n \;=\; n - (i+j-1) + 1. \]

Applying the min–max characterization at index $i+j-1$ (whose minimum runs over subspaces of exactly that dimension, and any larger subspace contains one) gives $\sigma_{i+j-1}(X+Y) \leq \max_{x \in T, \|x\|=1}\|(X+Y)x\|_2 \leq \max_{x\in T,\|x\|=1}\big(\|Xx\|_2 + \|Yx\|_2\big) \leq \sigma_i(X)+\sigma_j(Y)$. $\blacksquare$

Lemma 7.3. If $\operatorname{rank}(B) \leq k$ then $\sigma_{i+k}(A) \leq \sigma_i(A - B)$ for all $i \geq 1$ (with $\sigma_j := 0$ beyond $\min(m,n)$).

Proof. Apply Lemma 7.2 with $X = A - B$, $Y = B$, indices $i$ and $k+1$:  $\sigma_{i+k}(A) = \sigma_{i + (k+1) - 1}\big((A-B) + B\big) \leq \sigma_i(A-B) + \sigma_{k+1}(B)$. But $\operatorname{rank}(B)\leq k$ means $\sigma_{k+1}(B) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 8F (Eckart–Young, Frobenius norm). For every $B$ with $\operatorname{rank}(B)\leq k$,

\[ \|A - B\|_F^2 \;\geq\; \sum_{i \gt k}\sigma_i(A)^2 \;=\; \|A - A_k\|_F^2 . \]

Proof. By Theorem 7 and Lemma 7.3, $\|A-B\|_F^2 = \sum_{i\geq1}\sigma_i(A-B)^2 \geq \sum_{i\geq1}\sigma_{i+k}(A)^2 = \sum_{j \gt k}\sigma_j(A)^2$, which is exactly $\|A - A_k\|_F^2$ by the display in Section 7.1. $\blacksquare$

The same two lines prove the nuclear case — $\|A-B\|_* = \sum_i\sigma_i(A-B) \geq \sum_{i\gt k}\sigma_i(A) = \|A - A_k\|_*$ — and, since Lemma 7.3 controls the whole singular-value vector entrywise, they prove Mirsky's general statement:

Theorem 8M (Mirsky, 1960). For every unitarily invariant norm $\|\cdot\|$, $A_k$ minimizes $\|A-B\|$ over $\operatorname{rank}(B)\leq k$. Reason: such a norm is a symmetric gauge function of the singular-value vector (von Neumann), hence monotone in it, and Lemma 7.3 gives $\sigma(A - B) \succeq \sigma(A - A_k)$ entrywise after sorting.

This is why the deck can say “one matrix, $A_k$, is simultaneously optimal for all three norms” — a genuinely unusual state of affairs; the $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ minimizers of an ordinary regression problem certainly do not coincide.
