1  Why the Optimization Half

1.1 The argmin that was never solved

The probability half built estimators and bounded their errors, and each one ended in an optimization problem left unsolved: maximum likelihood is $\arg\max_\theta \sum_i \log p_\theta(x_i)$ (prob07), empirical risk minimization is $\arg\min_{f \in \mathcal F} \hat R(f)$ (prob06), the MMSE estimator is the minimizer of a squared loss over all measurable functions (prob07). This half solves those problems. The order is object (opt01: what is being optimized — matrices, and what their internal structure is), problem (opt02: least squares and ERM, which have closed-form solutions), algorithm (opt03: convexity and gradient descent, for when they do not), practice (opt04: stochastic gradients, which is what actually runs).

1.2 Why a linear-algebra lecture belongs in a deep-learning course

Every learnable parameter in a modern network sits in a matrix: a dense layer is $x \mapsto Wx + b$; an embedding table is $|\mathcal V| \times d$; attention is three projections $W_Q, W_K, W_V$ plus an output projection. The singular value decomposition is the only factorization that exists for every such matrix, and Sections 5–7 show that the three questions one actually asks about a weight matrix — can it be inverted, how big is it, can it be compressed — all have answers written directly in its singular values. The lecture's empirical claim, which the mathematics does not prove and cannot, is that on real weight matrices, embedding tables, and rating matrices the singular values decay fast; the mathematics supplies the conditional statement (Theorem 8): if they decay fast, truncation is not merely a reasonable heuristic but the provably optimal rank-$k$ approximation.

2  Background — Subspaces, Dimension, Rank

The deck opens with span, rank and null space as recalled objects and states Theorems 1–3 without proof. This section supplies the whole chain. Nothing here is deep, but Theorem 8's proof rests entirely on it, and the one genuinely non-obvious ingredient — the dimension formula of Section 2.3 — is exactly the deck's Technique Review 2.

2.1 Span, independence, basis; dimension is well defined

Definitions. For $v_1, \dots, v_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_k\} = \{\sum_i \alpha_i v_i : \alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}\}$. The list is linearly independent if $\sum_i \alpha_i v_i = 0$ forces every $\alpha_i = 0$. A subspace $W \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is a nonempty set closed under addition and scalar multiplication. A basis of $W$ is an independent list spanning $W$; $\dim W$ is the length of a basis.

That last definition needs a theorem to be legitimate: two bases of the same subspace could a priori have different lengths.

Lemma 2.1 (Steinitz exchange). If $w_1, \dots, w_p$ are linearly independent and all lie in $\operatorname{span}\{v_1, \dots, v_q\}$, then $p \leq q$.

Proof. Induct on $p$, exchanging one vector at a time. Suppose after $j$ steps we have a spanning list consisting of $w_1, \dots, w_j$ together with $q - j$ of the $v$'s (true for $j = 0$). If $j \lt p$, write $w_{j+1}$ in that spanning list: $w_{j+1} = \sum_{i \leq j} \alpha_i w_i + \sum_{\ell} \beta_\ell v_{i_\ell}$. Not every $\beta_\ell$ can vanish, or $w_{j+1}$ would be a combination of $w_1, \dots, w_j$, contradicting independence; in particular the list of remaining $v$'s is nonempty, which already forces $q - j \geq 1$, i.e. $j \lt q$. Pick $\ell$ with $\beta_\ell \neq 0$, solve for $v_{i_\ell}$, and swap it out for $w_{j+1}$: the new list still spans. After $p$ steps we have used $p$ distinct $v$'s, so $p \leq q$. $\blacksquare$

Applying the lemma in both directions to two bases of $W$ gives equal lengths, so $\dim W$ is well defined; the same lemma gives the two facts used repeatedly below: any independent list in $W$ has at most $\dim W$ elements and extends to a basis of $W$, and any spanning list contains a basis.

2.2 Range, null space, rank

Definitions. For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with columns $a_1, \dots, a_n$: $\operatorname{range}(A) = \{Ax : x \in \mathbb{R}^n\} = \operatorname{span}\{a_1, \dots, a_n\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$, using $Ax = \sum_i x_i a_i$; $\operatorname{null}(A) = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : Ax = 0\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$; $\operatorname{row}(A) = \operatorname{range}(A^{\top}) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$; and $\operatorname{rank}(A) = \dim \operatorname{range}(A)$. Both $\operatorname{range}(A)$ and $\operatorname{null}(A)$ are subspaces (immediate from linearity of $x \mapsto Ax$).

The slide's parenthetical “row rank $=$ column rank” is a real theorem; here is the two-line proof.

Proposition 2.2. $\operatorname{rank}(A^{\top}) = \operatorname{rank}(A)$.

Proof. Let $r = \operatorname{rank}(A)$ and let $u_1, \dots, u_r$ be a basis of $\operatorname{range}(A)$; write $U_0 = [u_1 \cdots u_r] \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$. Each column $a_j$ lies in $\operatorname{range}(A)$, so $a_j = U_0 c_j$ for some $c_j \in \mathbb{R}^r$; stacking, $A = U_0 C$ with $C = [c_1 \cdots c_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times n}$. Then $A^{\top} = C^{\top}U_0^{\top}$, so every column of $A^{\top}$ is a combination of the $r$ columns of $C^{\top}$, giving $\operatorname{rank}(A^{\top}) \leq r$. Applying the same bound to $A^{\top}$ in place of $A$ gives $\operatorname{rank}(A) \leq \operatorname{rank}(A^{\top})$. $\blacksquare$

The factorization $A = U_0C$ produced in that proof is worth naming: every rank-$r$ matrix is a product of an $m \times r$ and an $r \times n$ matrix, i.e. is a sum of $r$ rank-one matrices. That is the abstract form of the Netflix factorization $R = TG$ of Section 8, and the reason a rank-$r$ matrix costs $r(m+n)$ numbers rather than $mn$.

2.3 The dimension formula — Technique Review 2, proved

Theorem 2.3. For subspaces $W_1, W_2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$,

\[ \dim(W_1 + W_2) + \dim(W_1 \cap W_2) \;=\; \dim W_1 + \dim W_2 , \]

where $W_1 + W_2 = \{w_1 + w_2 : w_i \in W_i\}$.

Proof. Let $z_1, \dots, z_t$ be a basis of $W_1 \cap W_2$. Extend it to a basis $z_1, \dots, z_t, x_1, \dots, x_p$ of $W_1$ and to a basis $z_1, \dots, z_t, y_1, \dots, y_q$ of $W_2$, so $\dim W_1 = t + p$, $\dim W_2 = t + q$. The combined list $z_\bullet, x_\bullet, y_\bullet$ spans $W_1 + W_2$; we claim it is independent. Suppose $\sum \gamma_i z_i + \sum \alpha_j x_j + \sum \beta_\ell y_\ell = 0$. Then $v := \sum_\ell \beta_\ell y_\ell = -\sum \gamma_i z_i - \sum \alpha_j x_j$ lies in $W_1$ and in $W_2$, hence in $W_1 \cap W_2$, so $v = \sum_i \delta_i z_i$ for some $\delta$. But $\sum_\ell \beta_\ell y_\ell - \sum_i \delta_i z_i = 0$ with $z_\bullet, y_\bullet$ independent forces all $\beta_\ell = 0$ (and all $\delta_i = 0$). The relation collapses to $\sum \gamma_i z_i + \sum \alpha_j x_j = 0$, and independence in $W_1$ kills the rest. Hence $\dim(W_1+W_2) = t + p + q$, and $(t+p+q) + t = (t+p) + (t+q)$. $\blacksquare$

Corollary 2.4 (the deck's TR2). $\dim(W_1 \cap W_2) \geq \dim W_1 + \dim W_2 - n$. In particular if $\dim W_1 + \dim W_2 \gt n$ then $W_1 \cap W_2$ contains a nonzero vector.

Proof. $W_1 + W_2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ gives $\dim(W_1+W_2) \leq n$; substitute into Theorem 2.3. $\blacksquare$

This is the “two planes in $\mathbb{R}^3$ share a line” picture on the slide, and it is the entire engine of Theorem 8: it is what converts “$B$ has low rank” into “$B$ is blind at a specific place where $A$ is loud.”

2.4 Theorem 1 (rank–nullity), proved

Theorem 1. For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$,  $\operatorname{rank}(A) + \dim \operatorname{null}(A) = n$, the number of columns.

Proof. Let $x_1, \dots, x_t$ be a basis of $\operatorname{null}(A)$ and extend it to a basis $x_1, \dots, x_t, y_1, \dots, y_s$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, so $t + s = n$. It suffices to show $Ay_1, \dots, Ay_s$ is a basis of $\operatorname{range}(A)$.

Spanning: any $Ax$ with $x = \sum_i \alpha_i x_i + \sum_j \beta_j y_j$ satisfies $Ax = \sum_j \beta_j A y_j$, because $Ax_i = 0$.

Independence: if $\sum_j \beta_j Ay_j = 0$ then $A\big(\sum_j \beta_j y_j\big) = 0$, so $\sum_j \beta_j y_j \in \operatorname{null}(A)$ and thus equals $\sum_i \alpha_i x_i$ for some $\alpha$. Then $\sum_j \beta_j y_j - \sum_i \alpha_i x_i = 0$ is a relation among basis vectors of $\mathbb{R}^n$, so every $\beta_j = 0$. Hence $\operatorname{rank}(A) = s = n - t$. $\blacksquare$

The slide's reading — every input dimension either survives or dies, and the budget is exactly $n$ — is literally the proof: the $y_j$ directions survive (their images are a basis of the range), the $x_i$ directions die. Note the asymmetry the deck flags: the $n$ on the right is the column count, so nullity is measured in the input space. The sanity-check table on the slide is correct: $A$ has rank $2$, nullity $0$; $C$ has rank $1$, nullity $1$ (namely $\operatorname{span}\{(1,-1)\}$, since $Cx = 0 \iff x_1 + x_2 = 0$); the zero matrix has rank $0$, nullity $2$.

2.5 Orthogonality, Gram–Schmidt, and Theorem 2

Write $\langle x, y\rangle = x^{\top}y$. Orthonormal lists are automatically independent: if $\sum_i \alpha_i q_i = 0$ with $\langle q_i, q_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$, taking the inner product with $q_j$ gives $\alpha_j = 0$.

Lemma 2.5 (Gram–Schmidt). Every subspace $W \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ with $\dim W = d \geq 1$ has an orthonormal basis.

Proof. Take any basis $w_1, \dots, w_d$ and define recursively $\tilde q_j = w_j - \sum_{i \lt j} \langle q_i, w_j\rangle q_i$, $q_j = \tilde q_j / \|\tilde q_j\|$. Each $\tilde q_j \neq 0$ (else $w_j \in \operatorname{span}\{w_1,\dots,w_{j-1}\}$, contradicting independence), and $\langle q_i, \tilde q_j \rangle = \langle q_i, w_j\rangle - \langle q_i, w_j \rangle = 0$ for $i \lt j$ by induction. Spans agree at every stage. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 2 (orthogonal decomposition). Let $W \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a subspace and $W^{\perp} = \{v : v^{\top}w = 0 \text{ for all } w \in W\}$. Then every $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ has a unique decomposition $x = x_1 + x_2$ with $x_1 \in W$, $x_2 \in W^{\perp}$; moreover $\dim W + \dim W^{\perp} = n$ and $(W^{\perp})^{\perp} = W$.

Proof. $W^{\perp}$ is a subspace (intersection of kernels of the linear functionals $v \mapsto v^{\top}w$). Existence: take an orthonormal basis $q_1, \dots, q_d$ of $W$ (Lemma 2.5; if $d = 0$ take $x_1 = 0$) and set $x_1 = \sum_{i=1}^d \langle q_i, x\rangle q_i$, $x_2 = x - x_1$. Then $x_1 \in W$ and $\langle q_j, x_2 \rangle = \langle q_j, x\rangle - \langle q_j, x\rangle = 0$ for each $j$, so $x_2 \perp \operatorname{span}\{q_j\} = W$. Uniqueness: if $x_1 + x_2 = x_1' + x_2'$ then $x_1 - x_1' = x_2' - x_2 \in W \cap W^{\perp}$, and any $v \in W \cap W^{\perp}$ satisfies $v^{\top}v = 0$, hence $v = 0$. Dimensions: extending $q_1,\dots,q_d$ to an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (apply Lemma 2.5 to any extension to a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$; the first $d$ vectors are unchanged) exhibits $q_{d+1}, \dots, q_n$ as a basis of $W^{\perp}$: they lie in $W^{\perp}$, and any $v \in W^{\perp}$ expands as $\sum_i \langle q_i, v\rangle q_i$ with the first $d$ coefficients zero. Finally $W \subseteq (W^{\perp})^{\perp}$ is immediate, and both have dimension $d$ by applying the count twice, so they are equal. $\blacksquare$

The map $x \mapsto x_1$ is the orthogonal projection onto $W$; in matrix form, with $Q = [q_1 \cdots q_d]$, it is $x \mapsto QQ^{\top}x$. Sections 5.3 and opt02's normal equations both come back to exactly this matrix.

2.6 Theorem 3 — the four fundamental subspaces

Theorem 3. For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$:  $\big(\operatorname{row}(A)\big)^{\perp} = \operatorname{null}(A)$  and  $\big(\operatorname{range}(A)\big)^{\perp} = \operatorname{null}(A^{\top})$.

Proof. Write $\alpha_1^{\top}, \dots, \alpha_m^{\top}$ for the rows of $A$. Then $Ax = (\alpha_1^{\top}x, \dots, \alpha_m^{\top}x)$, so $Ax = 0$ iff $x \perp \alpha_i$ for every $i$, iff $x \perp \operatorname{span}\{\alpha_i\} = \operatorname{row}(A)$ (orthogonality to a spanning set gives orthogonality to the span, by linearity). That is the first identity. The second is the first applied to $A^{\top}$, since $\operatorname{row}(A^{\top}) = \operatorname{range}(A)$. $\blacksquare$

Combining with Theorem 2 and Proposition 2.2 gives the full picture the deck draws: $\mathbb{R}^n = \operatorname{row}(A) \oplus \operatorname{null}(A)$ with dimensions $r + (n-r)$, and $\mathbb{R}^m = \operatorname{range}(A) \oplus \operatorname{null}(A^{\top})$ with dimensions $r + (m-r)$; and $A$ restricted to $\operatorname{row}(A)$ is a bijection onto $\operatorname{range}(A)$ (it is injective, since $\operatorname{row}(A) \cap \operatorname{null}(A) = 0$, and both spaces have dimension $r$). That bijection is precisely what the pseudo-inverse of Section 5 inverts. Strang's article [7] is the classic exposition of this diagram.

3  Norms and Orthogonal Matrices

3.1 The axioms, and what follows from them

Definition. $\|\cdot\| : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is a norm if for all $x, y$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$: (N1) $\|x + y\| \leq \|x\| + \|y\|$; (N2) $\|\alpha x\| = |\alpha|\,\|x\|$; (N3) $\|x\| = 0 \iff x = 0$.

Nonnegativity is not an extra axiom — it follows: $0 = \|x + (-x)\| \leq \|x\| + \|-x\| = 2\|x\|$ by (N1) and (N2). Two consequences used later: the reverse triangle inequality $\big|\,\|x\| - \|y\|\,\big| \leq \|x - y\|$ (apply (N1) to $x = (x-y) + y$ and symmetrically), and the fact that every norm on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a continuous function (immediate from the reverse triangle inequality plus $\|x\| \leq \sum_i |x_i| \|e_i\|$). Continuity is what makes the maximum in the induced-norm definition of Section 6.1 attained rather than merely a supremum.

3.2 The $\ell_p$ family is a family of norms ($p \geq 1$)

(N2) and (N3) are obvious for $\|x\|_p = (\sum_i |x_i|^p)^{1/p}$. (N1) — Minkowski's inequality — is not, and its standard proof runs through Hölder's, which is worth having explicitly because opt02's Cauchy–Schwarz argument is the case $p = 2$.

Lemma 3.1 (Young). For $a, b \geq 0$ and conjugate exponents $p, q \gt 1$ with $\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q = 1$:  $ab \leq \tfrac{a^p}{p} + \tfrac{b^q}{q}$.

Proof. For $a, b \gt 0$ apply concavity of $\log$ (prob01's Jensen, two points, weights $\tfrac1p + \tfrac1q = 1$): $\log\!\big(\tfrac1p a^p + \tfrac1q b^q\big) \geq \tfrac1p \log a^p + \tfrac1q \log b^q = \log(ab)$; exponentiate. If $a$ or $b$ is $0$ the inequality is trivial. $\blacksquare$

Lemma 3.2 (Hölder). $\sum_i |x_i y_i| \leq \|x\|_p \|y\|_q$ for conjugate $p, q$; for $p = q = 2$ this is Cauchy–Schwarz.

Proof. If either factor is $0$ both sides vanish. Otherwise put $a_i = |x_i| / \|x\|_p$, $b_i = |y_i| / \|y\|_q$ and sum Young's inequality over $i$: $\sum_i a_i b_i \leq \tfrac1p \sum_i a_i^p + \tfrac1q \sum_i b_i^q = \tfrac1p + \tfrac1q = 1$. Multiply by $\|x\|_p\|y\|_q$. $\blacksquare$

Lemma 3.3 (Minkowski). $\|x + y\|_p \leq \|x\|_p + \|y\|_p$ for $p \geq 1$.

Proof. $p = 1$ is the scalar triangle inequality summed. For $p \gt 1$, with $q = p/(p-1)$,

\[ \|x+y\|_p^p \;\leq\; \sum_i |x_i|\,|x_i+y_i|^{p-1} + \sum_i |y_i|\,|x_i+y_i|^{p-1} \;\leq\; \big(\|x\|_p + \|y\|_p\big)\Big(\sum_i |x_i+y_i|^{(p-1)q}\Big)^{1/q}, \]

using Hölder on each sum. Since $(p-1)q = p$, the last factor is $\|x+y\|_p^{p/q}$. If $\|x+y\|_p = 0$ we are done; otherwise divide by it and use $p - p/q = 1$. $\blacksquare$

3.3 Why $p \geq 1$ — an explicit failure at $p \lt 1$

The slide states the restriction; here is the witness. Take $p = \tfrac12$, $x = (1,0)$, $y = (0,1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Then $\|x\|_{1/2} = \|y\|_{1/2} = 1$ but $\|x+y\|_{1/2} = (1 + 1)^{2} = 4 \gt 2$. So (N1) fails, decisively. The quantity $\|x\|_0 := \#\{i : x_i \neq 0\}$ (used in Sections 6 and 8 as “rank $= \|\sigma\|_0$”) is not a norm either: it violates (N2), since $\|2x\|_0 = \|x\|_0 \neq 2\|x\|_0$ in general. Calling them “the $\ell_{1/2}$ norm” and “the $\ell_0$ norm” is standard abuse; the deck's $\rightsquigarrow$ from $\|\sigma\|_0$ to $\|\sigma\|_1$ in Section 8 is precisely the move from a non-norm to the nearest convex norm.

3.4 $\ell_\infty$ as a limit, and the ordering

Proposition 3.4. $\|x\|_p \to \max_i |x_i| =: \|x\|_\infty$ as $p \to \infty$, and for every $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$,

\[ \|x\|_\infty \;\leq\; \|x\|_2 \;\leq\; \|x\|_1 \;\leq\; \sqrt{n}\,\|x\|_2 \;\leq\; n\,\|x\|_\infty . \]

Proof. Let $M = \|x\|_\infty$. Then $M^p \leq \sum_i |x_i|^p \leq n M^p$, so $M \leq \|x\|_p \leq n^{1/p}M$ and $n^{1/p}\to1$. For the chain: $\|x\|_\infty^2 = \max_i x_i^2 \leq \sum_i x_i^2$ gives the first; squaring $\|x\|_1$ gives $\sum_i x_i^2 + \sum_{i\neq j}|x_i||x_j| \geq \|x\|_2^2$, the second; Cauchy–Schwarz against the all-ones vector, $\sum_i |x_i| \cdot 1 \leq \sqrt n \|x\|_2$, the third; and $\|x\|_2^2 \leq n\|x\|_\infty^2$ the fourth. $\blacksquare$

The slide's worked example is the case $x = (3,4)$: $\|x\|_1 = 7$, $\|x\|_2 = 5$, $\|x\|_\infty = 4$, consistent with $4 \leq 5 \leq 7$. The same ordering reappears in Section 6.7 applied to the vector of singular values, where it becomes $\|A\|_2 \leq \|A\|_F \leq \|A\|_*$.

3.5 Orthogonal matrices — the equivalent characterizations

Proposition 3.5. For square $U \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ the following are equivalent: (i) $U^{\top}U = I$; (ii) $UU^{\top} = I$; (iii) the columns of $U$ are orthonormal; (iv) the rows are orthonormal; (v) $\|Ux\|_2 = \|x\|_2$ for all $x$; (vi) $(Ux)^{\top}(Uy) = x^{\top}y$ for all $x,y$.

Proof. (i) $\iff$ (iii) is the entry-wise reading of $U^{\top}U$, whose $(i,j)$ entry is $u_i^{\top}u_j$; likewise (ii) $\iff$ (iv). (i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii): $U^{\top}U = I$ means $U^{\top}$ is a left inverse of the square matrix $U$; a square matrix with a left inverse is invertible (it is injective, hence surjective by Theorem 1), and then $U^{\top} = U^{\top}(UU^{-1}) = (U^{\top}U)U^{-1} = U^{-1}$, so $UU^{\top} = I$. Symmetrically (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (i). (i) $\Rightarrow$ (vi): $(Ux)^{\top}(Uy) = x^{\top}U^{\top}Uy = x^{\top}y$. (vi) $\Rightarrow$ (v): take $y = x$. (v) $\Rightarrow$ (vi) by polarization, $x^{\top}y = \tfrac12(\|x+y\|^2 - \|x\|^2 - \|y\|^2)$. (vi) $\Rightarrow$ (i): $x^{\top}(U^{\top}U - I)y = 0$ for all $x, y$ forces the matrix $U^{\top}U - I$ to be $0$ (take $x = e_i$, $y = e_j$). $\blacksquare$

Squareness is essential and the deck flags why: the thin factor $U_c \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times r}$ of Section 4.4 satisfies $U_c^{\top}U_c = I_r$ but not $U_cU_c^{\top} = I_m$ when $r \lt m$ — the proof of (i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii) used a left inverse of a square matrix. Two further standard facts: orthogonal matrices form a group under multiplication (if $U^{\top}U = V^{\top}V = I$ then $(UV)^{\top}(UV) = V^{\top}U^{\top}UV = I$), and $\det U = \pm 1$ (from $1 = \det I = \det(U^{\top}U) = (\det U)^2$); $\det U = +1$ is a rotation, $-1$ a rotation composed with a reflection. The deck's $V = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & -1\\ 1 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ has $\det V = \tfrac12(1+1) = 1$: a genuine rotation, by $45^\circ$.

Theorem 4. $U$ orthogonal $\Rightarrow \|Ux\|_2 = \|x\|_2$ for all $x$. Conversely, a square $U$ preserving the $\ell_2$ norm is orthogonal.

Proof. Both directions are (i) $\iff$ (v) of Proposition 3.5. $\blacksquare$

The converse is worth stating because it explains the deck's “orthogonal invariance is today's master trick”: the orthogonal matrices are exactly the linear maps under which the $\ell_2$ geometry is invisible, which is why $U$ and $V$ can be peeled off in Theorems 6, 7 and 8. Note that this is special to $p = 2$ — rotations do change $\|x\|_1$ (rotate $(1,0)$ by $45^\circ$: $\|\cdot\|_1$ goes from $1$ to $\sqrt2$).

4  The Singular Value Decomposition

4.1 The one imported black box

Spectral theorem (imported). Every symmetric $S \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ can be written $S = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ real. If moreover $S$ is positive semidefinite ($x^{\top}Sx \geq 0$ for all $x$) then every $\lambda_i \geq 0$.

This is the single result these notes do not prove; it was used in prob08 for covariance matrices and is proved in any of [5, 6, 8]. (Sketch, for orientation: the maximum of the continuous function $x \mapsto x^{\top}Sx$ on the compact unit sphere is attained at some $q_1$, which is an eigenvector by a Lagrange-multiplier or first-order argument; $S$ maps $q_1^{\perp}$ into itself by symmetry, so induct on dimension.) The PSD claim is one line: $\lambda_i = q_i^{\top}Sq_i \geq 0$. Everything else below is proved.

4.2 Theorem 5 — existence of the SVD, proved

Theorem 5. Every $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$ admits $A = U\Sigma V^{\top}$ with $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ orthogonal and $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ “diagonal” ($\Sigma_{ij} = 0$ for $i \neq j$) with diagonal entries $\sigma_1 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r \gt 0 = \sigma_{r+1} = \cdots$.

Proof. The matrix $S = A^{\top}A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is symmetric ($S^{\top} = A^{\top}A$) and PSD ($x^{\top}Sx = \|Ax\|_2^2 \geq 0$). By the spectral theorem write $S = V \Lambda V^{\top}$ with $V = [v_1 \cdots v_n]$ orthogonal and $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n \geq 0$ (reorder the columns of $V$ to sort $\Lambda$; reordering preserves orthogonality).

Step 1: the number of positive eigenvalues is $r$. $\|Av_i\|_2^2 = v_i^{\top}Sv_i = \lambda_i$, so $Av_i = 0$ exactly when $\lambda_i = 0$. Hence $\operatorname{null}(A) \supseteq \operatorname{span}\{v_i : \lambda_i = 0\}$; conversely $Ax = 0 \Rightarrow Sx = 0$, and $Sx = 0$ with $x = \sum_i c_iv_i$ gives $\sum_i c_i\lambda_iv_i = 0$, so $c_i = 0$ whenever $\lambda_i \neq 0$. Thus $\operatorname{null}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{v_i : \lambda_i = 0\}$, and by Theorem 1 the count of positive $\lambda_i$ is $n - \dim\operatorname{null}(A) = r$. So $\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_r \gt 0 = \lambda_{r+1} = \cdots = \lambda_n$.

Step 2: define $\sigma_i$ and $u_i$. For $i \leq r$ set $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i} \gt 0$ and $u_i = Av_i / \sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}^m$. These are orthonormal:

\[ u_i^{\top}u_j = \frac{v_i^{\top}A^{\top}Av_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \frac{v_i^{\top}(\lambda_j v_j)}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \frac{\lambda_j\,\delta_{ij}}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \delta_{ij}. \]

Since $r \leq m$, extend $u_1, \dots, u_r$ to an orthonormal basis $u_1, \dots, u_m$ of $\mathbb{R}^m$ (Lemma 2.5 applied to a completion to a basis) and set $U = [u_1 \cdots u_m]$, which is orthogonal by Proposition 3.5.

Step 3: verify $AV = U\Sigma$. Let $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ have $\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i$ for $i \leq r$ and zeros elsewhere. Column $i$ of $AV$ is $Av_i$, which equals $\sigma_iu_i$ for $i \leq r$ (by definition of $u_i$) and $0$ for $i \gt r$ (Step 1). Column $i$ of $U\Sigma$ is $\sum_j u_j \Sigma_{ji} = \sigma_iu_i$ for $i \leq r$ and $0$ otherwise. The columns agree, so $AV = U\Sigma$, and right-multiplying by $V^{\top}$ gives $A = U\Sigma V^{\top}$. $\blacksquare$

Two remarks the deck compresses into “the $A^{\top}A$ route.” First, the proof is constructive and is exactly the by-hand recipe on the slides: form $A^{\top}A$, eigendecompose, take square roots, push through $A$. Second, it is a terrible numerical recipe: forming $A^{\top}A$ squares the condition number, so library implementations (Golub–Kahan bidiagonalization, [8, ch. 8]) never do this. The hand computation is for understanding, not for code — in practice one calls numpy.linalg.svd.

4.3 What is unique and what is not

Proposition 4.1. The singular values are unique: they are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^{\top}A$, sorted. The singular vectors are not.

Proof of the first claim. If $A = U\Sigma V^{\top}$ then $A^{\top}A = V\Sigma^{\top}\Sigma V^{\top}$, an eigendecomposition with eigenvalues $\sigma_i^2$; eigenvalues of a fixed matrix are the roots of its characteristic polynomial, hence determined by $A$ alone. $\blacksquare$

Non-uniqueness of the vectors has two sources. Signs: replacing $(u_i, v_i)$ by $(-u_i, -v_i)$ changes nothing, since $\sigma_i(-u_i)(-v_i)^{\top} = \sigma_iu_iv_i^{\top}$. Repeated singular values: if $\sigma_i = \sigma_j$ for $i \neq j$, any rotation within the corresponding two-dimensional eigenspace of $A^{\top}A$ yields another valid SVD — for $A = I_2$ every orthogonal $V$ works, with $U = V$. Additionally the columns $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$ are constrained only to complete an orthonormal basis. This is why the deck's Section 07 slide drops the source's uniqueness claim for the minimizer in Theorem 8: non-uniqueness enters here, at the level of the decomposition itself, and Section 7.6 shows it survives into the approximation problem.

4.4 Thin SVD, the rank-one expansion, and the four subspaces again

Discarding the columns of $U$ and $V$ that meet only zeros in $\Sigma$ gives the thin (compact) SVD $A = U_c\Sigma_cV_c^{\top}$ with $U_c \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times r}$, $\Sigma_c = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r)$ invertible, $V_c \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times r}$, and equivalently

\[ A \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^{\top}, \]

since the $(j,k)$ entry of both sides is $\sum_i \sigma_i u_{ji}v_{ki}$. Each $u_iv_i^{\top}$ has rank one and acts as $x \mapsto u_i (v_i^{\top}x)$: measure the component of $x$ along $v_i$, emit it along $u_i$. The SVD also hands back Theorem 3's subspaces explicitly:

SubspaceBasis from the SVDDimension
$\operatorname{range}(A)$$u_1, \dots, u_r$$r$
$\operatorname{null}(A^{\top}) = \operatorname{range}(A)^{\perp}$$u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$$m - r$
$\operatorname{row}(A)$$v_1, \dots, v_r$$r$
$\operatorname{null}(A) = \operatorname{row}(A)^{\perp}$$v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$$n - r$

(The first row: $Ax = \sum_{i\leq r}\sigma_i (v_i^{\top}x)u_i$ ranges over $\operatorname{span}\{u_1,\dots,u_r\}$ as $x$ varies; the fourth was Step 1 of Theorem 5's proof; the other two follow by Theorem 3.) Note also $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$ is defined by the SVD as the number of nonzero singular values, which in floating-point arithmetic becomes the useful notion of numerical rank: the number of $\sigma_i$ above a tolerance. Section 5.6 explains why that is the only workable definition in practice.

4.5 Relations worth having

All are immediate from $A = U\Sigma V^{\top}$: the singular values of $A^{\top}$ are those of $A$ (transpose the decomposition, swapping the roles of $U$ and $V$); $A^{\top}A$ and $AA^{\top}$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2$; if $A$ is square and invertible then $\sigma_i(A^{-1}) = 1/\sigma_{n+1-i}(A)$, so the condition number $\kappa(A) = \sigma_1/\sigma_n = \|A\|_2\|A^{-1}\|_2$ measures how badly $A$ distorts the unit sphere; if $A$ is symmetric PSD then its eigendecomposition is an SVD, with $\sigma_i = \lambda_i$; and if $A$ is symmetric but indefinite, $\sigma_i = |\lambda_i|$ after sorting (absorb the signs into $U$).

4.6 The running example, recomputed

Every step of the deck's hand computation for $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 0\\ 4 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, verified independently:

Step 1. $A^{\top}A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 4\\ 0 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 0\\ 4 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right] = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}9+16 & 20\\ 20 & 25\end{smallmatrix}\right] = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}25 & 20\\ 20 & 25\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. ✓

Step 2. $\det(A^{\top}A - \lambda I) = (25-\lambda)^2 - 400$, so $25 - \lambda = \pm20$ and $\lambda_1 = 45$, $\lambda_2 = 5$. ✓ (Check: trace $45 + 5 = 50 = 25+25$; determinant $45 \cdot 5 = 225 = 625 - 400$.) Normalized and orthogonal. ✓

Step 3. $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt5 \approx 6.7082$, $\sigma_2 = \sqrt5 \approx 2.2361$. ✓  $Av_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(3, 9)$, and $\|Av_1\| = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{90} = \sqrt{45} = \sigma_1$ ✓, so $u_1 = Av_1/\sigma_1 = \tfrac{(3,9)}{\sqrt2 \cdot 3\sqrt5} = \tfrac{(1,3)}{\sqrt{10}}$. ✓  $Av_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(-3, 1)$, $\|Av_2\| = \tfrac{\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt2} = \sqrt5 = \sigma_2$ ✓, so $u_2 = \tfrac{(-3,1)}{\sqrt{10}}$. ✓

4.7 Eigenvalues versus singular values

For the running matrix, which is lower triangular, the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries $3$ and $5$; the singular values are $6.708$ and $2.236$. Not equal, not even close, and not the same in count-of-agreement: the products agree, $3\cdot5 = 15 = |\det A| = \sigma_1\sigma_2 = 3\sqrt5\cdot\sqrt5$, which is general.

Proposition 4.2. For square $A$: (a) $\prod_i \sigma_i = |\det A| = \prod_i|\lambda_i|$; (b) $\max_i |\lambda_i| \leq \sigma_1$, with equality for all $i$ simultaneously iff $A$ is normal ($A^{\top}A = AA^{\top}$), in particular iff symmetric in the real symmetric case.

Proof of (a) and the inequality in (b). (a) $\det A = \det U \det\Sigma\det V^{\top} = \pm\prod_i\sigma_i$. (b) If $Ax = \lambda x$ with $\|x\| = 1$ then $|\lambda| = \|Ax\|_2 \leq \max_{\|y\|=1}\|Ay\|_2 = \sigma_1$ by Theorem 6. The equality characterization is standard and imported ([6, ch. 3]). $\blacksquare$

The conceptual difference the slide states — eigenvectors are directions $A$ preserves, right singular vectors are directions $A$ stretches most — is why singular values are the right notion here: eigenvalues need not exist over $\mathbb{R}$ (a $90^\circ$ rotation has none) and eigenvectors need not be orthogonal or even span, whereas $\sigma$ and both singular bases always exist.

4.8 The geometry: sphere to ellipsoid

Proposition 4.3. $A$ maps the unit sphere $\{x : \|x\|_2 = 1\}$ onto the ellipsoid $\{\sum_{i \leq r} c_iu_i : \sum_{i\leq r}c_i^2/\sigma_i^2 \leq 1\}$, degenerating to a filled ellipsoid in $\operatorname{range}(A)$ when $r \lt n$, with semi-axes $\sigma_iu_i$.

Proof. With $y = V^{\top}x$ (so $\|y\| = 1$ by Theorem 4), $Ax = U\Sigma y = \sum_{i\leq r}\sigma_iy_iu_i$, i.e. $c_i = \sigma_iy_i$ where $\sum_{i \leq n} y_i^2 = 1$. If $r = n$ this says exactly $\sum_i c_i^2/\sigma_i^2 = 1$; if $r \lt n$ the coordinates $y_{r+1},\dots,y_n$ are free, so $\sum_{i\leq r}y_i^2$ takes every value in $[0,1]$ and the image is the solid ellipsoid. $\blacksquare$

This is the deck's four-panel picture read algebraically: $V^{\top}$ rotates the sphere to itself, $\Sigma$ stretches axis $i$ by $\sigma_i$, $U$ rotates the resulting ellipsoid into the output space. It also makes Theorem 6 geometrically obvious in advance — the longest semi-axis has length $\sigma_1$.

5  The Moore–Penrose Pseudo-Inverse

5.1 The problem it solves

$A^{-1}$ exists only for square invertible $A$. For every other $A$ — tall, wide, or rank-deficient — the equation $Ax = b$ is either over- or under-determined, and the deck's question is what should play the role of “solve.” The SVD answers it by inverting what is invertible and leaving the rest alone: $A$ restricted to $\operatorname{row}(A)$ is a bijection onto $\operatorname{range}(A)$ (Section 2.6), so invert that map and extend by zero on $\operatorname{range}(A)^{\perp}$.

5.2 Definition and well-definedness

Definition. With the thin SVD $A = U_c\Sigma_cV_c^{\top}$, set  $A^{\dagger} := V_c\Sigma_c^{-1}U_c^{\top} = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \tfrac{1}{\sigma_i}v_iu_i^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$.

Equivalently, $A^{\dagger} = V\Sigma^{\dagger}U^{\top}$ where $\Sigma^{\dagger} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ transposes $\Sigma$ and inverts its nonzero entries — the deck's “flip and reciprocate.” The definition passes through a chosen SVD, which by Section 4.3 is not unique, so well-definedness needs an argument. The clean one is Penrose's.

Theorem 5.1 (Penrose, 1955). For each $A$ there is exactly one $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ satisfying

\[ \text{(P1) } AXA = A, \qquad \text{(P2) } XAX = X, \qquad \text{(P3) } (AX)^{\top} = AX, \qquad \text{(P4) } (XA)^{\top} = XA, \]

and it equals $V_c\Sigma_c^{-1}U_c^{\top}$ for every SVD of $A$. Consequently $A^{\dagger}$ does not depend on the SVD chosen.

Existence. Put $X = V_c\Sigma_c^{-1}U_c^{\top}$ and use $U_c^{\top}U_c = V_c^{\top}V_c = I_r$: $AX = U_c\Sigma_cV_c^{\top}V_c\Sigma_c^{-1}U_c^{\top} = U_cU_c^{\top}$, which is symmetric, giving (P3); symmetrically $XA = V_cV_c^{\top}$, giving (P4); then $AXA = U_cU_c^{\top}U_c\Sigma_cV_c^{\top} = U_c\Sigma_cV_c^{\top} = A$ (P1) and $XAX = V_cV_c^{\top}V_c\Sigma_c^{-1}U_c^{\top} = X$ (P2).

Uniqueness. Let $X, Y$ both satisfy (P1)–(P4). Then $X = XAX = X(AX)^{\top} = XX^{\top}A^{\top} = XX^{\top}(AYA)^{\top} = XX^{\top}A^{\top}(AY)^{\top} = X(AX)^{\top}AY = XAXAY = XAY$, and symmetrically $Y = XAY$ (run the same chain from $Y = YAY$ using (P4) and $A = AXA$). Hence $X = Y$. $\blacksquare$

5.3 $AA^{\dagger}$ and $A^{\dagger}A$ are projections

The proof just computed the two products: $AA^{\dagger} = U_cU_c^{\top}$ and $A^{\dagger}A = V_cV_c^{\top}$. By Theorem 2's projection formula these are the orthogonal projections onto $\operatorname{range}(A)$ and $\operatorname{row}(A)$ respectively — symmetric, idempotent ($(U_cU_c^{\top})^2 = U_c(U_c^{\top}U_c)U_c^{\top} = U_cU_c^{\top}$), with the stated ranges by Section 4.4. So “$A^{\dagger}$ is an inverse only where inversion is possible” is literally true: $A^{\dagger}A = I_n$ iff $r = n$ (full column rank), $AA^{\dagger} = I_m$ iff $r = m$ (full row rank), and both iff $A$ is invertible, in which case $A^{\dagger} = A^{-1}$ (uniqueness in Theorem 5.1, since $X = A^{-1}$ satisfies P1–P4).

Two closed forms follow, used constantly in opt02: if $A$ has full column rank then $A^{\top}A$ is invertible and $A^{\dagger} = (A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}$; if $A$ has full row rank then $A^{\dagger} = A^{\top}(AA^{\top})^{-1}$. Proof of the first: $A^{\top}A = V_c\Sigma_c^2V_c^{\top}$ with $V_c$ square orthogonal when $r = n$, so $(A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top} = V_c\Sigma_c^{-2}V_c^{\top}V_c\Sigma_cU_c^{\top} = V_c\Sigma_c^{-1}U_c^{\top} = A^{\dagger}$. The second is the transpose statement, using $(A^{\dagger})^{\top} = (A^{\top})^{\dagger}$ (transpose the definition).

5.4 The two worked examples, verified

Invertible case, $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 0\\ 4 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. $\det A = 15 \neq 0$, so $A^{\dagger} = A^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{15}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}5 & 0\\ -4 & 3\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Check: $\tfrac{1}{15}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 0\\ 4 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix}5 & 0\\ -4 & 3\end{smallmatrix}\right] = \tfrac{1}{15}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}15 & 0\\ 20-20 & 15\end{smallmatrix}\right] = I$. ✓ Its singular values are $1/\sigma_2 = 1/\sqrt5$ and $1/\sigma_1 = 1/(3\sqrt5)$, consistent with Section 4.5.

Singular case, $C = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 1\\ 2 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. $\operatorname{rank} C = 1$; $C = \sigma_1u_1v_1^{\top}$ with $v_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, $u_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(1,2)$, $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{10}$ (since $C^{\top}C = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}5 & 5\\ 5 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ has eigenvalues $10, 0$). Hence

\[ C^{\dagger} = \tfrac{1}{\sigma_1}v_1u_1^{\top} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1\\1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right] = \tfrac{1}{10}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 2\\ 1 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right], \]

matching the deck. The products: $CC^{\dagger} = \tfrac{1}{10}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 1\\ 2 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 2\\ 1 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right] = \tfrac{1}{10}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2 & 4\\ 4 & 8\end{smallmatrix}\right] = \tfrac15\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 2\\ 2 & 4\end{smallmatrix}\right] = u_1u_1^{\top}$ ✓ — symmetric, idempotent, rank one, projecting onto $\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}= \operatorname{range}(C)$. And $C^{\dagger}C = \tfrac{1}{10}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 2\\ 1 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 1\\ 2 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right] = \tfrac{1}{10}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}5 & 5\\ 5 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right] = \tfrac12\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right] = v_1v_1^{\top}$ ✓. Neither is $I$: $C$ is not invertible, and $A^{\dagger}$ recovers only the row-space component.

5.5 What $A^{\dagger}b$ actually optimizes

The deck defers the variational meaning to opt02; it is stated here because it is what makes the pseudo-inverse more than a formal gadget.

Theorem 5.2. For any $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$, the vector $x^{\star} = A^{\dagger}b$ is a minimizer of $\|Ax - b\|_2$, and among all minimizers it is the unique one of smallest $\|x\|_2$.

Proof. Write $b = AA^{\dagger}b + (I - AA^{\dagger})b =: b_{\parallel} + b_{\perp}$, an orthogonal split by Section 5.3 with $b_{\parallel} \in \operatorname{range}(A)$. For any $x$, $Ax - b = (Ax - b_{\parallel}) - b_{\perp}$ with the two terms orthogonal, so $\|Ax-b\|_2^2 = \|Ax - b_{\parallel}\|_2^2 + \|b_{\perp}\|_2^2 \geq \|b_{\perp}\|_2^2$, with equality iff $Ax = b_{\parallel}$. And $Ax^{\star} = AA^{\dagger}b = b_{\parallel}$, so $x^{\star}$ attains it.

The full minimizer set is $x^{\star} + \operatorname{null}(A)$. Since $x^{\star} = V_c\Sigma_c^{-1}U_c^{\top}b \in \operatorname{row}(A) = \operatorname{null}(A)^{\perp}$, Pythagoras gives $\|x^{\star} + z\|_2^2 = \|x^{\star}\|_2^2 + \|z\|_2^2$ for $z \in \operatorname{null}(A)$, minimized uniquely at $z = 0$. $\blacksquare$

So $A^{\dagger}$ is simultaneously the least-squares solver (opt02's normal equations, in the full-column-rank case $A^{\dagger} = (A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}$) and the minimum-norm solver (the implicit regularizer that reappears in opt04 as the bias of gradient descent from zero initialization).

5.6 A caution: $A \mapsto A^{\dagger}$ is discontinuous

Let $A_\varepsilon = \operatorname{diag}(1, \varepsilon)$. Then $A_\varepsilon^{\dagger} = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1/\varepsilon)$, which blows up as $\varepsilon \to 0^{+}$, while $A_0 = \operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ has $A_0^{\dagger} = \operatorname{diag}(1,0)$. So $A_\varepsilon \to A_0$ but $A_\varepsilon^{\dagger} \not\to A_0^{\dagger}$. This is why numerical software never tests $\sigma_i = 0$ exactly but truncates at a tolerance (typically $\max(m,n)\cdot\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}\cdot\sigma_1$), and why the rank-$k$ truncation of Section 7 is the numerically stable object while “the rank” is not.

6  Matrix Norms

6.1 Induced norms — definition, attainment, submultiplicativity

Definition. For a vector norm $\|\cdot\|_p$, the induced (operator) norm is $\|A\|_p := \max_{\|x\|_p = 1}\|Ax\|_p = \max_{x \neq 0} \tfrac{\|Ax\|_p}{\|x\|_p}$.

The maximum is attained, not merely approached: $x \mapsto \|Ax\|_p$ is continuous (Section 3.1) and the unit sphere is compact. The two expressions agree by homogeneity, $\|A(x/\|x\|)\| = \|Ax\|/\|x\|$. Induced norms satisfy the norm axioms (N1 from $\|(A+B)x\| \leq \|Ax\| + \|Bx\|$ pointwise, then maximize; N3 because $\|A\| = 0$ forces $Ae_i = 0$ for all $i$) and additionally the two properties that make them useful for error analysis:

\[ \|Ax\|_p \leq \|A\|_p\|x\|_p \quad\text{(consistency)}, \qquad \|AB\|_p \leq \|A\|_p\|B\|_p \quad\text{(submultiplicativity)}. \]

Consistency is the definition rescaled; submultiplicativity follows by applying it twice: $\|ABx\| \leq \|A\|\|Bx\| \leq \|A\|\|B\|\|x\|$, then maximize over $\|x\| = 1$. Also $\|I\|_p = 1$ for every induced norm — a property the Frobenius norm lacks ($\|I_n\|_F = \sqrt n$), which is the first sign that $\|\cdot\|_F$ is not induced by any vector norm for $n \gt 1$.

6.2 The $p = 1$ and $p = \infty$ formulas

Not on the slides but standard, and useful as a contrast to how much harder $p=2$ is:

\[ \|A\|_1 = \max_{j}\sum_{i}|a_{ij}| \ \ \text{(max absolute column sum)}, \qquad \|A\|_\infty = \max_{i}\sum_{j}|a_{ij}| \ \ \text{(max absolute row sum)}. \]

Proof of the first: $\|Ax\|_1 = \sum_i|\sum_j a_{ij}x_j| \leq \sum_j |x_j| \sum_i |a_{ij}| \leq (\max_j \sum_i|a_{ij}|)\|x\|_1$, with equality at $x = e_{j^\star}$ for the maximizing column. The second is the same argument transposed. Both are read off the entries in $O(mn)$ time; $\|A\|_2$ requires the SVD, which is $O(mn\min(m,n))$ — the practical reason Frobenius is often used as a cheap proxy.

6.3 Theorem 6: the spectral norm is $\sigma_1$

Theorem 6. $\|A\|_2 = \sigma_1(A)$, attained at $x = v_1$.

Proof. Let $A = U\Sigma V^{\top}$ and $\|x\|_2 = 1$. Put $y = V^{\top}x$, so $\|y\|_2 = 1$ by Theorem 4. Then, again by Theorem 4 applied to $U$, $\|Ax\|_2^2 = \|U\Sigma y\|_2^2 = \|\Sigma y\|_2^2 = \sum_{i \leq r}\sigma_i^2y_i^2 \leq \sigma_1^2\sum_i y_i^2 = \sigma_1^2$. So $\|A\|_2 \leq \sigma_1$. For the reverse, $x = v_1$ gives $y = e_1$ and $\|Av_1\|_2 = \sigma_1$. $\blacksquare$

The two-line structure — peel off $U$ and $V$ by orthogonal invariance, reduce to a diagonal problem, read off the answer — is the deck's “master trick,” and Theorem 7 and Theorem 8 are the same trick twice more.

6.4 The Frobenius norm and Theorem 7

$\|A\|_F := \big(\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}^2\big)^{1/2}$: the Euclidean norm of the matrix read as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{mn}$. Hence it is a norm for free (it is $\ell_2$ under the identification), and it comes with an inner product $\langle A,B\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(A^{\top}B) = \sum_{i,j}a_{ij}b_{ij}$, so $\|A\|_F^2 = \operatorname{tr}(A^{\top}A)$.

Theorem 7. $\|A\|_F = \big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_i^2\big)^{1/2}$, and more generally $\|UAV\|_F = \|A\|_F$ for orthogonal $U, V$.

Proof. Using $\operatorname{tr}(XY) = \operatorname{tr}(YX)$: $\|UAV\|_F^2 = \operatorname{tr}(V^{\top}A^{\top}U^{\top}UAV) = \operatorname{tr}(V^{\top}A^{\top}AV) = \operatorname{tr}(A^{\top}AVV^{\top}) = \operatorname{tr}(A^{\top}A) = \|A\|_F^2$. Applying this with the SVD, $\|A\|_F = \|U^{\top}AV\|_F = \|\Sigma\|_F = (\sum_i \sigma_i^2)^{1/2}$. $\blacksquare$

Check on the running example: $\|A\|_F = \sqrt{9 + 0 + 16 + 25} = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt2 \approx 7.07$, and $\sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2} = \sqrt{45 + 5} = \sqrt{50}$. ✓ The identity $\sum_i\sigma_i^2 = \sum_{ij}a_{ij}^2$ is the “energy” bookkeeping used in Section 7.5: it lets a truncation error be quoted as a fraction of total energy.

6.5 The nuclear norm is a norm

$\|A\|_* := \sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_i$. (N2) and (N3) are easy — $\sigma_i(\alpha A) = |\alpha|\sigma_i(A)$ from the SVD, and $\|A\|_* = 0$ forces $r=0$ — but (N1) is not obvious, because singular values of a sum are not sums of singular values. The efficient route is a variational formula, which also gives the duality used in Section 8.

Proposition 6.1. $\displaystyle \|A\|_* = \max_{\|Z\|_2 \leq 1} \operatorname{tr}(Z^{\top}A)$.

Proof. ($\geq$) Take $Z = U_cV_c^{\top}$, which has all its nonzero singular values equal to $1$, so $\|Z\|_2 = 1$; then $\operatorname{tr}(Z^{\top}A) = \operatorname{tr}(V_cU_c^{\top}U_c\Sigma_cV_c^{\top}) = \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_cV_c^{\top}V_c) = \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_c) = \sum_i\sigma_i$.

($\leq$) For any $Z$ with $\|Z\|_2\leq1$, write $A = \sum_i \sigma_iu_iv_i^{\top}$ and use linearity of the trace: $\operatorname{tr}(Z^{\top}A) = \sum_i\sigma_i\operatorname{tr}(Z^{\top}u_iv_i^{\top}) = \sum_i\sigma_i\, v_i^{\top}Z^{\top}u_i \leq \sum_i \sigma_i\|Zv_i\|_2\|u_i\|_2 \leq \sum_i\sigma_i$, by Cauchy–Schwarz and consistency. $\blacksquare$

Corollary 6.2. $\|\cdot\|_*$ satisfies the triangle inequality, hence is a norm. Proof: for any admissible $Z$, $\operatorname{tr}(Z^{\top}(A+B)) = \operatorname{tr}(Z^{\top}A) + \operatorname{tr}(Z^{\top}B) \leq \|A\|_* + \|B\|_*$; now maximize the left side over $Z$. $\blacksquare$  The same argument shows $\|\cdot\|_*$ is convex — a pointwise maximum of linear functions of $A$ — which is exactly the property Section 8 needs, and is why the proof was routed through the variational formula rather than through Weyl-type inequalities.

Proposition 6.1 says $\|\cdot\|_*$ and $\|\cdot\|_2$ are dual norms with respect to the trace inner product; the Frobenius norm is self-dual. In the language of Section 3, applying the vector chain $\|\sigma\|_\infty \leq \|\sigma\|_2 \leq \|\sigma\|_1$ to the singular-value vector gives

\[ \|A\|_2 \;\leq\; \|A\|_F \;\leq\; \|A\|_* \;\leq\; \sqrt{r}\,\|A\|_F \;\leq\; r\,\|A\|_2 . \]

Running example: $\|A\|_2 = 6.71 \leq \|A\|_F = 7.07 \leq \|A\|_* = 3\sqrt5 + \sqrt5 = 4\sqrt5 \approx 8.94$. ✓

6.6 The three norms as $\ell_p$ norms of $\sigma$, and why that matters

The deck's punchline — spectral $= \|\sigma\|_\infty$, Frobenius $= \|\sigma\|_2$, nuclear $= \|\sigma\|_1$, rank $= \|\sigma\|_0$ — is the organizing fact of the rest of the deck. All three are unitarily invariant ($\|UAV\| = \|A\|$ for orthogonal $U,V$), because they depend on $A$ only through $\sigma$. Von Neumann's theorem states the converse: every unitarily invariant matrix norm is a symmetric gauge function of $\sigma$. That is the setting of Mirsky's version of Eckart–Young (Section 7.5), which is why one proof covers all three.

The rank/nuclear pairing is the matrix analogue of the sparsity/$\ell_1$ pairing in compressed sensing: $\|\sigma\|_0$ counts, $\|\sigma\|_1$ is the tightest convex function that under-approximates it on a bounded set. Fazel [12] made this precise.

Theorem 6.3 (Fazel, 2002). On the set $\{X : \|X\|_2 \leq 1\}$, the convex envelope of $X \mapsto \operatorname{rank}(X)$ is $X \mapsto \|X\|_*$.

(The convex envelope is the largest convex function lying below the target; the proof computes the biconjugate using Proposition 6.1. Stated without proof here; see [12] and the exposition in [13].) On $\{\|X\|_2 \leq \tau\}$ the envelope is $\|X\|_*/\tau$, so the scale matters — a point elided when the relaxation is written down casually.

The one-line intuition: $\operatorname{rank}$ is blind to the size of the singular values and only counts them, so it is flat and non-convex; $\|\cdot\|_*$ pays proportionally, which is convex, and driving a singular value to zero is rewarded continuously rather than only in the limit.

7  Low-Rank Approximation: Eckart–Young–Mirsky

7.1 The problem and the candidate

Fix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ of rank $r$ and $k \lt r$. The problem is

\[ \min_{B \,:\, \operatorname{rank}(B)\,\leq\, k} \ \|A - B\| \]

for a unitarily invariant norm. The feasible set is not convex — $\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ and $\operatorname{diag}(0,1)$ both have rank $1$, their midpoint $\tfrac12 I_2$ has rank $2$ — so nothing from convex optimization applies, and yet the problem has a clean closed-form solution. That is the surprise. The candidate is the truncated SVD

\[ A_k \;:=\; \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^{\top} \;=\; U\Sigma_kV^{\top}, \qquad \Sigma_k = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_k,0,\dots,0), \]

which has rank exactly $k$ (its nonzero singular values are $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_k$). Its errors are read off immediately from Theorems 6 and 7 applied to $A - A_k = \sum_{i \gt k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^{\top}$:

\[ \|A - A_k\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}, \qquad \|A - A_k\|_F = \Big(\sum_{i \gt k}\sigma_i^2\Big)^{1/2}, \qquad \|A - A_k\|_* = \sum_{i \gt k}\sigma_i. \]

The theorem is that no other rank-$\leq k$ matrix does better, in any of the three norms.

7.2 Courant–Fischer for singular values

Both proofs below rest on a variational characterization; it is proved here rather than cited because everything downstream is a two-line consequence of it.

Lemma 7.1 (min–max / max–min). For $1 \leq i \leq \min(m,n)$,

\[ \sigma_i(A) \;=\; \max_{\substack{S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \\ \dim S = i}} \ \min_{\substack{x \in S \\ \|x\|_2 = 1}} \|Ax\|_2 \;=\; \min_{\substack{T \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \\ \dim T = n-i+1}} \ \max_{\substack{x \in T \\ \|x\|_2 = 1}} \|Ax\|_2 . \]

Proof (first equality; the second is analogous). ($\geq$ for the max) Take $S_0 = \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_i\}$. For unit $x \in S_0$, $x = \sum_{j\leq i}c_jv_j$ with $\sum c_j^2 = 1$, and $\|Ax\|_2^2 = \sum_{j \leq i}\sigma_j^2c_j^2 \geq \sigma_i^2$ since $\sigma_j \geq \sigma_i$ for $j \leq i$. So the inner min over $S_0$ is $\geq \sigma_i$ (indeed $=\sigma_i$, at $x = v_i$).

($\leq$ for every $S$) Let $\dim S = i$ and $W = \operatorname{span}\{v_i, v_{i+1},\dots,v_n\}$, $\dim W = n-i+1$. By Theorem 2's dimension count, $\dim(S \cap W) \geq \dim S + \dim W - n = 1$, so there is a unit $x \in S\cap W$. Writing $x = \sum_{j \geq i}c_jv_j$, $\|Ax\|_2^2 = \sum_{j\geq i}\sigma_j^2c_j^2 \leq \sigma_i^2$. Hence the inner min over $S$ is $\leq \sigma_i$. $\blacksquare$

Note the mechanism: a dimension count forces two subspaces to intersect. That single idea drives Theorem 8, Lemma 7.3, and Theorem 9 alike.

7.3 Theorem 8 — the spectral-norm case, proved

Theorem 8 (Eckart–Young, spectral norm). For every $B$ with $\operatorname{rank}(B) \leq k$,  $\|A - B\|_2 \geq \sigma_{k+1} = \|A - A_k\|_2$.

Proof. By Theorem 1, $\dim\operatorname{null}(B) \geq n - k$. Let $W = \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_{k+1}\}$, of dimension $k+1$. Then

\[ \dim\big(\operatorname{null}(B) \cap W\big) \;\geq\; (n-k) + (k+1) - n \;=\; 1, \]

so choose a unit vector $x \in \operatorname{null}(B)\cap W$. Since $Bx = 0$,

\[ \|A - B\|_2^2 \;\geq\; \|(A-B)x\|_2^2 \;=\; \|Ax\|_2^2 \;=\; \Big\|\sum_{i \leq k+1}\sigma_i (v_i^{\top}x)\,u_i\Big\|_2^2 \;=\; \sum_{i\leq k+1}\sigma_i^2(v_i^{\top}x)^2 \;\geq\; \sigma_{k+1}^2, \]

using $x \in W$ (so $v_i^{\top}x = 0$ for $i \gt k+1$), orthonormality of the $u_i$, and $\sum_{i\leq k+1}(v_i^{\top}x)^2 = \|x\|_2^2 = 1$ with $\sigma_i \geq \sigma_{k+1}$ throughout. Since $A_k$ attains $\sigma_{k+1}$, it is optimal. $\blacksquare$

The result is usually attributed to Eckart and Young [1], who proved the Frobenius case in 1936; Mirsky [2] gave the general unitarily-invariant statement in 1960, and Erhard Schmidt [3] had proved the analogous theorem for integral operators already in 1907. See [9] for the tangled history of who proved what.

7.4 The Frobenius case, proved

The deck states this case and does not prove it; the source likewise defers to an external note [16]. It is proved here in full, because the Frobenius case — not the spectral one — is what PCA, latent-factor recommenders, and low-rank compression actually minimize. The route is Weyl's inequality for singular values.

Lemma 7.2 (Weyl, additive). For $X, Y \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $i, j \geq 1$ with $i + j - 1 \leq \min(m,n)$:  $\sigma_{i+j-1}(X+Y) \leq \sigma_i(X) + \sigma_j(Y)$.

Proof. By the min–max form of Lemma 7.1 there are subspaces $T_X$ of dimension $n-i+1$ and $T_Y$ of dimension $n-j+1$ with $\max_{x\in T_X, \|x\|=1}\|Xx\|_2 = \sigma_i(X)$ and likewise for $Y$. Put $T = T_X \cap T_Y$, so

\[ \dim T \;\geq\; (n-i+1) + (n-j+1) - n \;=\; n - (i+j-1) + 1. \]

Applying the min–max characterization at index $i+j-1$ (whose minimum runs over subspaces of exactly that dimension, and any larger subspace contains one) gives $\sigma_{i+j-1}(X+Y) \leq \max_{x \in T, \|x\|=1}\|(X+Y)x\|_2 \leq \max_{x\in T,\|x\|=1}\big(\|Xx\|_2 + \|Yx\|_2\big) \leq \sigma_i(X)+\sigma_j(Y)$. $\blacksquare$

Lemma 7.3. If $\operatorname{rank}(B) \leq k$ then $\sigma_{i+k}(A) \leq \sigma_i(A - B)$ for all $i \geq 1$ (with $\sigma_j := 0$ beyond $\min(m,n)$).

Proof. Apply Lemma 7.2 with $X = A - B$, $Y = B$, indices $i$ and $k+1$:  $\sigma_{i+k}(A) = \sigma_{i + (k+1) - 1}\big((A-B) + B\big) \leq \sigma_i(A-B) + \sigma_{k+1}(B)$. But $\operatorname{rank}(B)\leq k$ means $\sigma_{k+1}(B) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 8F (Eckart–Young, Frobenius norm). For every $B$ with $\operatorname{rank}(B)\leq k$,

\[ \|A - B\|_F^2 \;\geq\; \sum_{i \gt k}\sigma_i(A)^2 \;=\; \|A - A_k\|_F^2 . \]

Proof. By Theorem 7 and Lemma 7.3, $\|A-B\|_F^2 = \sum_{i\geq1}\sigma_i(A-B)^2 \geq \sum_{i\geq1}\sigma_{i+k}(A)^2 = \sum_{j \gt k}\sigma_j(A)^2$, which is exactly $\|A - A_k\|_F^2$ by the display in Section 7.1. $\blacksquare$

The same two lines prove the nuclear case — $\|A-B\|_* = \sum_i\sigma_i(A-B) \geq \sum_{i\gt k}\sigma_i(A) = \|A - A_k\|_*$ — and, since Lemma 7.3 controls the whole singular-value vector entrywise, they prove Mirsky's general statement:

Theorem 8M (Mirsky, 1960). For every unitarily invariant norm $\|\cdot\|$, $A_k$ minimizes $\|A-B\|$ over $\operatorname{rank}(B)\leq k$. Reason: such a norm is a symmetric gauge function of the singular-value vector (von Neumann), hence monotone in it, and Lemma 7.3 gives $\sigma(A - B) \succeq \sigma(A - A_k)$ entrywise after sorting.

This is why the deck can say “one matrix, $A_k$, is simultaneously optimal for all three norms” — a genuinely unusual state of affairs; the $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ minimizers of an ordinary regression problem certainly do not coincide.

7.5 Energy accounting and the running example

Theorem 7 licenses reporting truncation quality as a fraction of “energy”: $\|A_k\|_F^2 / \|A\|_F^2 = \sum_{i\leq k}\sigma_i^2 / \sum_{i \leq r}\sigma_i^2$. For the running $A$: $\sigma_1^2/(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2) = 45/50 = 90\%$ captured by the rank-one truncation, with

\[ A_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1.5 & 1.5\\ 4.5 & 4.5\end{bmatrix},\qquad A - A_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1.5 & -1.5\\ -0.5 & 0.5\end{bmatrix},\qquad \|A-A_1\|_2 = \|A-A_1\|_F = \sigma_2 = \sqrt5 . \]

The coincidence $\|\cdot\|_2 = \|\cdot\|_F$ here is not general; it holds because $A - A_1$ has rank one, so its singular-value vector has a single nonzero entry and all $\ell_p$ norms of it agree. Check independently: $\|A-A_1\|_F^2 = 2.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 5$. ✓

Two standard consequences. Storage: keeping $A_k$ costs $k(m+n)$ numbers (or $k(m+n+1)$ with the $\sigma_i$ stored separately) versus $mn$, a win as soon as $k \lt mn/(m+n)$. PCA: if the rows of $A$ are mean-centred data points, then $A^{\top}A$ is proportional to the sample covariance, the $v_i$ are the principal directions, $\sigma_i^2/(m-1)$ the explained variances, and Theorem 8F says that projecting onto the top $k$ principal directions is the rank-$k$ projection of least squared reconstruction error. The “90% of variance explained” convention is the energy ratio above.

7.6 Uniqueness — the claim the deck drops

The source asserts that $A_k$ is the minimizer. As stated for the spectral norm that is false, and the deck is right to omit it. Counterexample: $A = \operatorname{diag}(2,1)$, $k = 1$, so $\sigma_2 = 1$ and the optimal value is $1$. Take $B_a = \operatorname{diag}(a, 0)$ for any $a \in [1,3]$; then $A - B_a = \operatorname{diag}(2-a, 1)$, whose singular values are $|2-a| \leq 1$ and $1$, so $\|A - B_a\|_2 = 1$ — optimal — while $A_1 = \operatorname{diag}(2,0)$ is only the case $a = 2$. A whole interval of minimizers.

Proposition 7.4. In the Frobenius norm the minimizer is unique if and only if $\sigma_k \gt \sigma_{k+1}$. Sketch: the proof of Theorem 8F is tight only when $\sigma_i(A-B) = \sigma_{i+k}(A)$ for all $i$, which forces $B$ to be a truncation at a spectral gap; if $\sigma_k = \sigma_{k+1}$ the corresponding singular subspace has dimension $\gt 1$ and any $k$-dimensional subspace of it gives another minimizer (as for $A = I_2$, $k=1$: every $vv^{\top}$ with $\|v\|=1$ is optimal). In the spectral norm uniqueness fails even with a strict gap, by the counterexample above.

8  Matrix Completion and the Nuclear-Norm Relaxation

8.1 The Netflix toy example, unpacked

The deck's $5\times4$ user–movie matrix has Alice and Bob rating action films $5$ and dramas $1$, Carol and Dave the reverse, and Eve at $(4,4,2,2)$. The factorization the slides exhibit is $R = TG$ with

\[ T = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\\ 0 & 1\\ 0.75 & 0.25\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{5\times2}, \qquad G = \begin{bmatrix}5 & 5 & 1 & 1\\ 1 & 1 & 5 & 5\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times4}. \]

Eve's row is then $0.75\cdot(5,5,1,1) + 0.25\cdot(1,1,5,5) = (4,4,2,2)$ ✓, so $\operatorname{rank}(R) = 2$ and the storage claim is $5\cdot2 + 2\cdot4 = 18$ numbers versus $20$ — a deliberately unimpressive ratio at this size, which becomes $500{,}000 \cdot 50 + 50 \cdot 20{,}000 = 26$M versus $10$B at Netflix's scale. The two latent columns are not labelled “action” and “drama” by the algorithm; the factorization is only determined up to $T \mapsto TM$, $G \mapsto M^{-1}G$ for invertible $M$, so any interpretation of individual factors is imposed by the reader, not recovered. This non-identifiability is the honest version of the deck's “taste vectors” story.

8.2 The completion problem, and why Section 7 does not apply

In practice one observes $R_{ij}$ only for $(i,j) \in \Omega$ (Netflix: about $1\%$ of entries), and wants

\[ \min_{X} \ \operatorname{rank}(X) \quad \text{subject to} \quad X_{ij} = R_{ij}\ \ \forall (i,j)\in\Omega, \]

or its noisy variant $\min \operatorname{rank}(X)$ s.t. $\sum_{(i,j)\in\Omega}(X_{ij}-R_{ij})^2 \leq \delta$. Eckart–Young does not solve this. Theorem 8 answers “what is the best rank-$k$ approximation to a fully known $A$”; here most of $A$ is missing, and the truncated SVD of a matrix with holes is not defined. Worse, the problem is genuinely hard.

Theorem 9 (hardness, imported). Deciding whether there is a completion of rank $\leq k$ is NP-hard in general; the case $k=3$ already encodes graph colourability. Even the weaker task of approximating the minimum rank within a constant factor is hard under standard assumptions [15]. So the exact problem is out of reach and a relaxation is not a convenience but a necessity.

8.3 The relaxation

Replace the objective by its convex envelope (Theorem 6.3):

\[ \min_{X} \ \|X\|_* \quad\text{s.t.}\quad X_{ij}=R_{ij}\ \forall (i,j)\in\Omega \qquad\text{(or } \textstyle\min \tfrac12\sum_{\Omega}(X_{ij}-R_{ij})^2 + \lambda\|X\|_* \text{)}. \]

This is a convex program — a semidefinite program, in fact, via $\|X\|_* = \min\{\tfrac12(\operatorname{tr}W_1 + \operatorname{tr}W_2) : \left[\begin{smallmatrix}W_1 & X\\ X^{\top} & W_2\end{smallmatrix}\right]\succeq0\}$ [13] — hence solvable to global optimality. The nontrivial question is whether its solution is the matrix one actually wanted.

Theorem 10 (Candès–Recht 2009; Candès–Tao 2010; Recht 2011). Let $R$ be $n_1\times n_2$ of rank $r$ satisfying a standard incoherence condition (its singular vectors are not concentrated on few coordinates), and let $\Omega$ be a uniformly random set of $|\Omega|$ entries. If $|\Omega| \gtrsim \mu\, r\, n \log^2 n$ with $n = \max(n_1,n_2)$, then with high probability $R$ is the unique solution of the nuclear-norm program.

Stated without proof; see [10, 11, 14]. Two caveats the headline hides: incoherence genuinely excludes matrices like $e_1e_1^{\top}$, which no sampling scheme can recover (almost every observed entry is $0$ and carries no information about the one nonzero); and “uniformly random $\Omega$” is false for ratings data, where missingness correlates with the rating itself (people watch what they expect to like). The theory explains why the method works at all, not why it works on Netflix.

8.4 How it is solved, in one paragraph

The workhorse for the penalized form is singular value thresholding [14]: the proximal operator of $\lambda\|\cdot\|_*$ is soft-thresholding of the singular values,

\[ \operatorname{prox}_{\lambda\|\cdot\|_*}(Y) \;=\; \arg\min_X \tfrac12\|X-Y\|_F^2 + \lambda\|X\|_* \;=\; U\,\operatorname{diag}\big((\sigma_i-\lambda)_{+}\big)\,V^{\top}, \]

which follows from unitary invariance (Theorem 7) reducing the problem to the diagonal case, then $n$ independent scalar problems $\min_x \tfrac12(x-\sigma)^2 + \lambda|x|$. Iterating a gradient step on the data-fit term with this prox is proximal gradient descent — exactly the algorithm class of opt03 — and gives the SVT algorithm. At Netflix scale the SVD per iteration is itself too expensive, so production systems instead parametrize $X = TG^{\top}$ with $T, G$ tall and run alternating least squares or SGD on the factors [17]; that is non-convex, has no recovery guarantee of the above kind, and works well anyway. The winning Netflix Prize entry was an ensemble built around exactly such latent-factor models [17].

8.5 Historical footnote

Netflix announced the $\$1$M Prize in October 2006, for a $10\%$ improvement in RMSE over its in-house Cinematch predictor on a held-out set; the prize was awarded in September 2009 to BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos, at a $10.06\%$ improvement. The contest is the reason matrix factorization became standard practice in recommender systems, and it is a fair characterization that its most durable legacy was methodological rather than deployed — Netflix reported never putting the winning ensemble into production, as its engineering cost outweighed the accuracy gain.

8.6 Where this goes next

The same low-rank idea reappears throughout the second half of the course and beyond it. Compression: replacing a weight matrix $W$ by $W_k$ costs $k(m+n)$ parameters. LoRA [18]: freeze $W$ and train an additive low-rank update $W + BA$ with $B \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$, $A\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}$, $k$ in the single or double digits — the dominant fine-tuning method for large models, and a direct application of “the interesting part of a matrix is often low-rank.” Embeddings: word and item embeddings are low-rank factorizations of co-occurrence matrices. In each case Section 7 supplies the guarantee that truncation is optimal, and Section 8 the machinery for when the matrix is only partly observed.
