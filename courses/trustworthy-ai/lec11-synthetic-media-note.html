1. Synthetic Media: Watermarks, Detection and Provenance

The provenance problem · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈0.5 min

Welcome to Lecture 11. Generators now produce text, images, voice and video that people cannot reliably tell from human work. Today's question: when something might be machine-made, what evidence can tell us where it came from, and how much should we trust that evidence?

Key takeaway: Today: evidence about where content came from, and how strong each kind is.

Six parts. The provenance problem; text watermarking; robustness and real deployments; detection without a watermark; provenance records and the law; and a synthesis.

3. Section 01 — The Provenance Problem

The provenance problem · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈1.5 min

We start with a case where the question "who made this?" cost real money, then lay out the three kinds of evidence the rest of the lecture examines.

Key takeaway: Origin questions have real stakes.

4. A Faked Video Call Moved US$25 Million

The provenance problem · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈4 min

In January 2024 an employee in Arup's Hong Kong office joined a video call with people who looked and sounded like the company's CFO and colleagues. Every other participant was fake. Following instructions on the call, the employee made 15 transfers to 5 accounts, about HKD 200 million, roughly USD 25 million.

Nothing about the content of the call had to be false in an obvious way. The failure was about origin: who was actually on the other end.

Keep this case in mind. A detector, a watermark or a signed record would each have helped in a different way, and each could also have failed.

Key takeaway: The question that mattered was origin, not content.

Setup, model, date

Arup, Hong Kong office, January 2024; reported by CNN on 16 May 2024, with the account coming from Hong Kong police. Press report; no technical analysis of how the fakes were produced is public.

What this establishes

That synthetic video and voice were convincing enough to drive a large financial loss in a real workplace.

What this does not establish

It does not tell us which generator was used, or whether any detector would have flagged the call.

Primary sources

  • CNN, "Arup revealed as victim of $25 million deepfake scam involving Hong Kong employee" (16 May 2024) — press report; the incident account comes from Hong Kong police.

5. Three Ways to Ask "Where Did This Come From?"

The provenance problem · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈6 min

Three families of evidence. Embed: the generator hides a signal in its output when it creates it; that is a watermark. Guess: anyone inspects the content afterwards and estimates whether a machine made it; that is a detector. Sign: the creator or the creating tool attaches a cryptographically signed record; that is provenance.

They differ in who has to cooperate. A watermark needs the generator. A detector needs nobody, which is why it is attractive and why it is weak. Provenance needs the creating tool and every app along the sharing path to keep the record.

Key takeaway: Embed, guess, sign: three kinds of evidence with different cooperators.

6. Origin Is Not Truth

The provenance problem · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈8 min

Read the table row by row. A watermark found: a keyed generator probably made this, but the content could still be true or false. Valid credentials: we know who signed which claims and that the file has not changed since, but not whether the claims are accurate.

The bottom two rows are where people go wrong. No mark found does not mean human-made: the generator may not watermark, or the mark may have been removed. No credentials does not mean fake: most real photos carry none, and sharing apps often strip them.

And AI-made content can carry perfectly honest credentials saying "made with model Y".

Key takeaway: Origin signals say who made something; absence of a signal proves little.

7. Three Questions for Every Method Today

The provenance problem · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈9 min

For every method we ask three questions. Who acts: the generator, anyone, or the creator? What must survive: edits, paraphrase, crops, re-encoding, copy-paste? Which error hurts: accusing a human, or missing AI content?

Study slides carry a strip with who acts, what was attacked, and the error rate used. Read it before reading the result.

Key takeaway: Who acts, what must survive, which error hurts.

8. Today: Marks on Outputs, Not on Models

The provenance problem · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈10 min

One boundary. Today's watermarks mark each generated output, so we can ask "did this model produce this text?" A different line of work marks the model weights, to ask "is this stolen copy my model?" That is out of scope today, as is copyright of training data and outputs.

Key takeaway: Output watermarks only; model-ownership marks and copyright are out of scope.

9. Section 02 — Text Watermarking

Text watermarking · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈10.5 min

We now build one text watermark from scratch, the one from Kirchenbauer and colleagues, usually called KGW. Everything later in the lecture is a variation or an attack on this idea.

Key takeaway: One mechanism, taught carefully.

10. A Model Writes One Token at a Time

Text watermarking · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈12 min

A language model does not write a sentence at once. Given the text so far, it gives odds for every possible next word, picks one at random by those odds, appends it, and repeats.

The numbers on the slide are illustrative. The point: there is randomness in every pick, and a watermark changes only how that pick is made.

Key takeaway: Generation is repeated random picks; a watermark changes the pick.

The figure

Illustrative bar chart of next-word probabilities after "The weather today is". The numbers are made up for teaching.

11. Step 1: A Secret Key Splits the Vocabulary

Text watermarking · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈14 min

At each position, the previous word and a secret key are hashed into a random seed. The seed splits the whole vocabulary into a green list, a fraction γ of words (here 25%), and a red list, the rest.

The split changes at every position because the previous word changes. Without the key, the split looks random; with it, anyone can recompute which words were green.

Key takeaway: A keyed hash of the previous word picks a fresh green list at each step.

The figure

Schematic of the KGW split: previous word and secret key feed a hash, which seeds a green/red partition of the vocabulary.

Setup, model, date

Kirchenbauer et al., University of Maryland; arXiv January 2023, ICML 2023. The rule shown is the "soft" watermark of §3.

Assumptions

The hash behaves like a random function; the key stays secret from the attacker.

Primary sources

  • Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers & Goldstein, "A Watermark for Large Language Models" (ICML 2023) — green/red list rule (§3, Algorithm 2), z-test (§3), Fig. 1 example, Fig. 2 strength vs perplexity, Fig. 3a z vs length.

12. Step 2: Give Green Words a Small Boost δ

Text watermarking · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈16 min

The generator adds a small constant δ to the scores of green words before turning scores into odds. Green words become more likely; red words stay possible.

When the model is very sure of one word, the boost barely changes the pick, so the mark is weak in low-entropy text such as code or memorised passages. That is why detection needs enough varied text.

Key takeaway: A soft boost tilts the odds toward green words without forbidding red ones.

The figure

Illustrative before/after bars: green words gain probability, red words lose some.

Primary sources

  • Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers & Goldstein, "A Watermark for Large Language Models" (ICML 2023) — green/red list rule (§3, Algorithm 2), z-test (§3), Fig. 1 example, Fig. 2 strength vs perplexity, Fig. 3a z vs length.

13. Reading Back: Count the Green Words

Text watermarking · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈18 min

This is KGW's Figure 1. The detector, holding the key, recomputes the green list at each position and colours each token. Ordinary text has green tokens at about the chance rate; watermarked text has many more.

The numbers: without the watermark, z = 0.31 and p = 0.38. With it, z = 7.4 and p ≈ 6×10⁻¹⁴.

Detection needs only the key and the text, not the model.

Key takeaway: With the key, detection is counting.

14. By Chance, About γ of Words Are Green

Text watermarking · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈19.5 min

If the text was written without the key, each word is green with probability about γ, like a biased coin. With γ = 25% we expect about 5 green words in 20. The watermarked line has 14.

The question becomes statistical: is the green count too high to be chance?

Key takeaway: Unmarked text should hit green at the chance rate γ.

The figure

Illustrative strips of 20 tokens: 5/20 green for ordinary text, 14/20 for watermarked text.

15. The Threshold Sets the False-Alarm Rate

Text watermarking · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈22 min

z measures how many standard deviations the green count sits above chance. KGW flags text when z > 4.

If words really were independent coin flips, ordinary text would cross z = 4 with probability about 3×10⁻⁵, one in roughly 30,000. That "if" matters; two slides on we see when it fails.

Moving the threshold trades false alarms for misses, as with any test.

Key takeaway: The threshold sets the false-alarm rate, under an independence assumption.

The figure

Sketch, not to scale: two bell curves of z, ordinary text centred near 0 and watermarked text far right, with the threshold at z = 4.

Setup, model, date

KGW §3: one-proportion z-test; threshold z = 4.

Assumptions

Under the null, each scored token is green independently with probability γ. The exact null distribution and the √T scaling are in lec11tech.html.

Primary sources

  • Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers & Goldstein, "A Watermark for Large Language Models" (ICML 2023) — green/red list rule (§3, Algorithm 2), z-test (§3), Fig. 1 example, Fig. 2 strength vs perplexity, Fig. 3a z vs length.

16. Longer Text, Stronger Evidence

Text watermarking · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈24 min

KGW Figure 3a: average z against the number of tokens T, at boost δ = 5, for several green fractions γ. Every curve rises with length.

If the green rate stays steady, z grows like √T. A short reply carries little evidence however strong the boost.

Key takeaway: Evidence accumulates with length; short texts are hard.

The figure

KGW Fig. 3a, unmodified: average z-score vs T for δ = 5 and γ ∈ {0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9}.

Setup, model, date

OPT-1.3B, multinomial sampling, C4 prompts; average over generations.

What this establishes

That detection strength grows steadily with length in this setting.

What this does not establish

Averages hide the spread; short individual texts can fall below threshold.

Assumptions

√T holds only if the green rate per token stays constant; see lec11tech.html.

Primary sources

  • Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers & Goldstein, "A Watermark for Large Language Models" (ICML 2023) — green/red list rule (§3, Algorithm 2), z-test (§3), Fig. 1 example, Fig. 2 strength vs perplexity, Fig. 3a z vs length.

17. Repeated Phrases Break the Coin-Flip Count

Text watermarking · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈26.5 min

Real text repeats itself. If the same (previous word, word) pair appears twice, it gets the same colour twice; the two "coin flips" are not independent, so the z-test is overconfident.

Kirchenbauer and colleagues' 2024 Figure 7 measures this on human text from WikiText-103. Blue counts repeats; its measured false-alarm rate sits above the theory. Green counts each repeated pair once; it then matches the black theory curve.

Vocabulary: the nominal rate comes from the formula; the measured rate comes from running the detector on real human text. A careful paper reports both.

Key takeaway: Nominal and measured false-alarm rates differ unless repeats are handled.

The figure

Kirchenbauer et al. 2024 Fig. 7, unmodified: left, empirical FPR vs z threshold; right, z required for 0.1% FPR vs tokens. Blue uncorrected, green with repeated n-grams removed, black theoretical.

Setup, model, date

Kirchenbauer et al., ICLR 2024; human text from WikiText-103.

What this establishes

That ignoring repeated n-grams inflates false alarms, and that scoring each repeated pair once restores agreement with theory on this corpus.

What this does not establish

It does not guarantee the nominal rate on other text types, such as code or templated writing.

Primary sources

  • Kirchenbauer et al., "On the Reliability of Watermarks for Large Language Models" (ICLR 2024) — machine paraphrase and copy-paste attacks (Fig. 2), human rewrite study with 14 writers (Fig. 4), empirical FPR with repeated-n-gram correction (Fig. 7).

18. A Bigger Boost Is Easier to Detect, but Less Fluent

Text watermarking · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈28.5 min

KGW Figure 2, left panel. Each point is a setting of boost δ (shape) and green fraction γ (colour). The x-axis is perplexity under a larger oracle model, reversed so that right is more fluent. The y-axis is z.

With no boost, z is 0 and perplexity about 5. With δ = 10, z reaches about 41 but perplexity rises to about 13. The boost is a dial between detectability and quality.

Key takeaway: Detectability costs fluency; δ is the dial.

A quality–detectability trade-off for this scheme and model.

What this does not establish

Perplexity is a proxy for quality; it is not a human judgment.

19. Some Marks Leave the Output Distribution Unchanged

Text watermarking · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈30 min

KGW shifts the odds. Other designs do not. A distortion-free watermark (Kuditipudi and colleagues) guarantees that, averaged over the random key, each reply has exactly the model's own odds. An undetectable watermark (Christ, Gunn and Zamir) guarantees that without the key no efficient test can tell marked from unmarked text, even after many queries.

We name these so you recognise them; we will not build them. The formal definition is in the tech file.

Key takeaway: A boost is not the only design; some marks are invisible without the key.

Setup, model, date

Kuditipudi et al., Stanford, TMLR (Definition 1). Christ, Gunn & Zamir, arXiv 2306.09194 (2023).

What this does not establish

"Distortion-free" is a statement averaged over keys; it is not a claim about any single reply, nor about robustness to edits.

Primary sources

  • Kuditipudi, Thickstun, Hashimoto & Liang, "Robust Distortion-free Watermarks for Language Models" (TMLR) — distortion-free definition (Definition 1), EXP and EXP-edit watermarks, substitution robustness (Fig. 4, m = 35).
  • Christ, Gunn & Zamir, "Undetectable Watermarks for Language Models" (arXiv 2306.09194, 2023) — cryptographic definition of undetectability; venue not checked for this lecture.

20. Section 03 — Robustness and Production

Robustness and production · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈30.5 min

A watermark is only useful if it survives what people do to text. We look at the evidence under different attacks, then at deployed systems.

Key takeaway: Robustness is always relative to an attack.

21. Edits Range From Word Swaps to Full Rewrites

Robustness and production · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈31.5 min

Think of attacks as a ladder: swap some words; run a paraphrasing model; paraphrase repeatedly; have a person rewrite; paste a short marked piece into a long human text.

Each study tests one rung, with one watermark, one length and one error rate. In all of today's studies the attacker does not have the key.

Key takeaway: Compare studies only rung by rung.

23. A Paraphrasing Model Cut Detection to About Half

Robustness and production · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈34.5 min

Krishna and colleagues built DIPPER, an 11-billion-parameter paraphraser with two dials: L for word-choice change and O for reordering, each 0 to 100.

On 300-token KGW-watermarked text, with the threshold set for 1% false alarms on human text, detection drops from about 100% clean to 57.2% (GPT2-1.5B) and 52.8% (OPT-13B) at the strongest setting.

Key takeaway: A strong paraphraser roughly halves detection at this length.

The figure

Redrawn from Krishna et al. Table 1, KGW rows only (γ = 0.5, δ = 2): % of watermarked texts detected at 1% FPR, for clean text and four DIPPER settings.

Setup, model, date

Krishna et al. (UMass Amherst, Google), NeurIPS 2023. Generators GPT2-XL (1.5B) and OPT-13B; 300-token generations; human text from WikiText-103 sets the threshold.

What this establishes

That paraphrase without the key substantially reduces KGW detection at 300 tokens and 1% FPR.

What this does not establish

It does not show detection goes to zero, nor what happens with longer texts or different watermark settings.

Primary sources

  • Krishna, Song, Karpinska, Wieting & Iyyer, "Paraphrasing evades detectors of AI-generated text, but retrieval is an effective defense" (NeurIPS 2023) — DIPPER paraphraser (11B), Table 1 detection at 1% FPR before and after paraphrase; 300-token generations.

24. Paraphrasing Five Times Pushed Detection Lower

Robustness and production · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈36 min

Sadasivan and colleagues paraphrase repeatedly. Figure 3a shows ROC curves after each round, ppi. At 1% false alarms, detection falls from 99.8% to 80.7% after one round, and to 15.7% after five.

Read the perplexity column too: it rose from 5.1 to 8.7. The text got worse; the attack had a quality cost.

Key takeaway: Repeated paraphrase erodes the mark, at a quality cost.

The figure

Sadasivan et al. Fig. 3a, unmodified: ROC curves for watermark detection after 0–5 rounds of DIPPER paraphrasing; legend lists AUROC, TPR at 1% FPR and perplexity.

Setup, model, date

Sadasivan et al. (University of Maryland), arXiv March 2023, TMLR 2025. Watermarked OPT-13B on XSum documents; ~300 tokens; DIPPER as paraphraser.

What this establishes

That recursive paraphrase can push detection of this watermark at 1% FPR very low.

What this does not establish

It does not measure whether the paraphrased text is still useful for the attacker's purpose.

Primary sources

  • Sadasivan, Kumar, Balasubramanian, Wang & Feizi, "Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?" (TMLR 2025) — recursive paraphrasing (Fig. 3a, OPT-13B, XSum), AUROC bound in terms of total variation (Theorem 1).

25. An Impossibility Bound, and What It Covers

Robustness and production · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈38 min

Sadasivan also prove a bound: any detector's AUROC is at most ½ + TV − TV²/2, where TV is the total-variation distance between the AI text distribution and the human text distribution.

If an attacker makes AI text nearly indistinguishable from human text, TV is near 0 and every detector is near a coin flip. If TV is large, as it is for freshly watermarked text, the bound is near 1 and says little.

So the bound tells you what happens once the attacker succeeds; it does not tell you how hard succeeding is.

Key takeaway: The bound is conditional on a successful attack.

The figure

Curve drawn from the formula AUROC ≤ ½ + TV − TV²/2, with two illustrative points.

Setup, model, date

Sadasivan et al., Theorem 1 and its discussion of watermarked models.

Assumptions

Applies to any detector that sees one text sample; the derivation is in lec11tech.html.

Primary sources

  • Sadasivan, Kumar, Balasubramanian, Wang & Feizi, "Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?" (TMLR 2025) — recursive paraphrasing (Fig. 3a, OPT-13B, XSum), AUROC bound in terms of total variation (Theorem 1).

26. Human Rewrites: Detected With Enough Text

Robustness and production · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈39.5 min

Kirchenbauer and colleagues (2024) recruited 14 experienced writers to rewrite watermarked Vicuna answers, without the key. At a strict 10⁻⁵ false-alarm threshold, z keeps rising with length: on average, human rewrites were detected after about 800 tokens.

Key takeaway: Human paraphrase dilutes the mark; length brings it back.

That on long texts, human rewrites lowered but did not erase the signal.

28. Compare Robustness Results Only With Their Setups

Robustness and production · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈43 min

Put the studies side by side and they look contradictory: 57% caught, 15.7% caught, AUC above 0.9, detected on average. But they differ in attack, length and error rate, and in watermark settings too.

Krishna and Sadasivan fix 1% false alarms at about 300 tokens. Kirchenbauer 2024 uses longer texts and either ROC-AUC or a 10⁻⁵ threshold. Kuditipudi uses 35-token texts and a median p-value.

Watermark settings: Kuditipudi KGW-2.0 (γ = 0.25, δ = 2); Krishna KGW γ = 0.5, δ = 2; Sadasivan watermarked OPT-13B; Kirchenbauer 2024 γ = 0.25, δ = 2 (LLaMA-7B, Vicuna for the human study).

Key takeaway: No single robustness number; each is tied to its setup.

29. "Impossible" and "Robust" Answer Different Questions

Robustness and production · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈44.5 min

The bound says: if AI text is made nearly indistinguishable from human text, every detector fails. The experiments say: against these edits, at this length and error rate, the mark was found or not. Both can be true.

The useful question is how much the attack cost, in effort and in text quality.

Key takeaway: The impossibility result and the robustness results are differently scoped.

30. SynthID-Text Runs in a Deployed Chatbot

Robustness and production · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈46 min

Google DeepMind's SynthID-Text, published in Nature in 2024. Figure 3a: true positives at 1% false alarms against length. SynthID-Text (orange) reaches about 50% at 100 tokens and about 87% at 400, above the earlier Gumbel-sampling method (blue).

The authors also ran a live experiment on nearly 20 million Gemini responses and report that user thumbs-up/down ratings showed text quality was preserved.

Key takeaway: A text watermark is deployed at scale; detection still depends on length.

33. "Standard" Can Mean Three Different Things

Key takeaway: Ask which layer a "standard" refers to.

34. Section 04 — Detecting Without a Watermark

Detecting without a watermark · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈50.5 min

Now drop the key. A detector looks at content and guesses. This is what most people mean by "AI detection".

Key takeaway: No key: the detector must guess.

35. A Detector Draws One Line Through Overlapping Scores

Detecting without a watermark · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈52 min

A keyless detector gives each text a score. Human and AI scores overlap. The detector draws one line: right of it, flagged. Some AI text falls left (missed); some human text falls right (false alarm). Moving the line trades one for the other; it never removes both.

Key takeaway: Overlap forces a trade-off between misses and false accusations.

Sketch: two overlapping score distributions with a threshold line.

36. Worked Example: Rare AI Text, Many False Accusations

Detecting without a watermark · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈54.5 min

A hypothetical: 10,000 essays, 2% AI-written. The detector catches 90% of AI essays and flags 1% of human essays.

That gives 180 AI essays flagged and 98 human essays flagged, 278 flags in total. 98 of 278, about 35%, are humans. One flag in three is a false accusation, with a detector that sounds very good.

Key takeaway: When AI text is rare, even a low false-alarm rate produces many false accusations.

37. "1% False Alarms" Is Not "1% of Flags Are Wrong"

Detecting without a watermark · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈56.5 min

Two different numbers. The false-positive rate: of human essays, the share flagged. It is a property of the detector. The share of flags that are human: it also depends on how common AI text is.

Same detector: at 2% AI prevalence, about 35% of flags are human. At 20% prevalence, about 4%.

Key takeaway: FPR is fixed; the chance a flagged person is innocent depends on prevalence.

42. A Detector Score Is a Lead, Not a Verdict

Detecting without a watermark · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈65 min

Before acting on a detector score, ask: How common is AI content here? Were false alarms measured on writing like this? Was the detector tested on new generators and real data? What happens to the person flagged?

No mark found is not proof of human; a flag alone is not proof of AI.

Key takeaway: Use detector scores to prompt a closer look, not to decide.

43. Section 05 — Provenance and Policy

Provenance and policy · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈65.5 min

Instead of guessing what is fake, record where content came from. Then we look at what the law now requires.

Key takeaway: From guessing to recording.

44. Flip the Question: Prove Origin Instead of Spotting Fakes

Provenance and policy · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈67 min

Detection has to keep up with every new generator. Provenance flips the question: content carries a signed record of where it came from.

It only works if tools sign and the record survives. And provenance labels AI content too: a generator can sign "made with model X".

Key takeaway: Provenance proves origin for content that carries it.

45. A C2PA Manifest: Signed Claims Bound to the File

Provenance and policy · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈69 min

C2PA defines a manifest attached to a file. It holds claims, called assertions, such as "captured by camera X", "cropped" or "generated by model Y"; a hash, a fingerprint of the exact bytes; and a signature from the signer's certificate.

Change one pixel and the hash no longer matches; the manifest then no longer validates for that file.

Key takeaway: A manifest binds signed claims to exact content.

46. Each Edit Adds a Signed Step; None Proves the Scene

Provenance and policy · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈71 min

A camera signs at capture; an editor signs its edit and links to the earlier manifest; a viewer checks the chain.

A valid chain shows who signed each claim and that nothing changed since signing. It does not show that the claims are accurate or that the scene happened. A signed photo of a screen is still a photo of a screen.

Key takeaway: Signatures prove who said what, not what is true.

47. Supported, On by Default, Preserved: Three Separate Facts

So watermarks and provenance work together. And a missing manifest is not evidence of fakery; the record may simply have been stripped.

Key takeaway: Soft bindings let a watermark recover a stripped record.

We close by comparing the three kinds of evidence directly.

52. Embed, Guess, Sign: Side by Side

Synthesis · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈83 min

Embed: the generator acts; the mark must survive edits and paraphrase; it fails when rewritten away; a hit means a keyed generator likely made it.

Guess: anyone acts; it must keep up with new generators; it fails by false accusation; a hit means only that the score is high.

Sign: creator and tools act; the record must survive sharing; it fails when stripped; a hit means these signers made these claims.

They complement each other; soft bindings join embed and sign.

Key takeaway: Each kind of evidence has its own failure; combine them.

53. Origin Is Not Truth: Three Cases

Synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈85 min

An AI chart with honest credentials can be wrong. A real photo whose record was stripped is true, with no origin signal. A human-written false claim carries no mark and is false.

Origin tools answer who made it. Checking facts is a separate job.

Key takeaway: Origin and truth are independent questions.

54. Checklist for Any "We Can Detect It" Claim

Synthesis · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈87.5 min

Five questions. How many tokens or pixels? Short content is weak evidence. False alarms measured on whose data? Nominal is not measured. How common is AI content here? Base rates drive accusations. Which attack, with what access? Each study is one rung. Tested on new generators? Held-out data is harder.

Key takeaway: Five questions turn a headline into a checkable claim.

55. Takeaways

Synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈89.5 min

Watermarks are a keyed signal, detectable with enough text. Their robustness depends on the attack, the length and the error rate. Detectors without marks face base rates that turn small error rates into many false accusations. Provenance signs claims, and can be stripped.

Origin is not truth, and absence of a signal proves little.

Key takeaway: Origin is not truth; absence of a signal proves little.
