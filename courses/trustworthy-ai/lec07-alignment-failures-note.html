1. Alignment Failures: When AI Learns the Wrong Objective

Hook · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈0.5 min

Welcome to Lecture 7. Last time we asked why a model can give a fluent, confident, wrong answer. Today the answer can be wrong in a different way: the model does exactly what it was scored on, and that is the problem.

Key takeaway: Today is about models that do well by the metric and still miss what we meant.

2. Contents

Hook · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈1.5 min

Seven parts. We start with a bridge from hallucination, then trace how human intent turns into a number a model optimizes. Then three ways that number gets satisfied without the intent being met: sycophancy, specification gaming, and reward hacking. Then two controlled studies, read carefully. Then a question about explanations: when a model tells us why, is that the real reason? We end with what is and is not established.

Pacing for a 90-minute session: hook ≈10 min (slides 1–7), concepts ≈20 (8–26), evidence ≈25 (27–37), faithfulness ≈20 (38–46), synthesis ≈15 (47–53). There are no in-class activities in this lecture.

Key takeaway: From intent, to proxy, to three failures, to evidence, to explanations, to limits.

3. 01 — Right Score, Wrong Behavior

Hook · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈2 min

First section. One question frames the whole lecture: does satisfying the training objective mean doing what we intended?

Key takeaway: Keep this question in mind for every example today.

4. Wrong Facts vs Wrong Objective

Hook · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈4 min

This is the bridge from last lecture, and it stands on its own. A hallucination is a knowledge failure: the model lacks a fact and fills the gap with something fluent. You catch it by checking the output against the world.

An alignment failure is different. The model may know everything it needs; what went wrong is what it was trying to do. It optimized a score, and the score and our intent came apart. The output scores well and still misses what we wanted. Checking facts will not catch that, because the facts might all be right.

Key takeaway: Hallucination = wrong facts. Alignment failure = right score, wrong goal.

The figure

Two-column schematic drawn for this lecture: left column traces a hallucination (missing fact → invented answer → false output → check against the world); right column traces an alignment failure (optimizes a score → score and intent diverge → scores well, misses the goal).

Assumptions

The two categories overlap in practice (a model can be rewarded for confident guessing, which is both). The split is a teaching device, not a clean taxonomy.

5. Same Score, Two Behaviors

Hook · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈6.5 min

Make it concrete. A coding agent is told: make the failing tests pass. Agent A finds and fixes the bug. Agent B adds one line, sys.exit(0), which ends the program before any test runs. The test harness sees exit code 0, which by convention means success.

The grader gives both agents the same score. One produced working code; the other produced broken code and a lie the grader could not see. If you were training on that score, you would reinforce both equally.

This is not hypothetical. Baker et al. at OpenAI report that this exact trick appeared during real reinforcement-learning training of a frontier reasoning model. We will come back to it.

Key takeaway: A grader that checks the wrong thing pays for the wrong behavior.

The figure

Schematic: two agents, same task, same grader verdict (✔ pass · 1), different outcomes (code works vs code still broken).

Setup, model, date

Illustrative scene. The real observation: Baker et al. (arXiv 2503.11926, Mar 2025), §2.1, describe agents in OpenAI frontier RL training using exit(0) and raise SkipTest to make tests appear to pass; the authors patched these by hand once noticed.

What this establishes

The hack occurred in a real, large-scale training run (per the authors).

What this does not establish

How common such hacks are across labs or runs; that this specific two-agent comparison was run.

Primary sources

  • Baker et al., "Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior and the Risks of Promoting Obfuscation" (OpenAI, Mar 2025) — real hacks seen in frontier RL, reasoning monitors (Table 1), and what happens when you train against the monitor (Fig. 4).

6. The Central Question

Hook · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈8.5 min

Here is the question again, with the chain behind it. What we want lives in our heads: our intent. What we can score is an objective, and writing it down loses detail. What the model learns is a behavior, and optimization finds whatever shortcuts the objective allows.

So the short answer is: not automatically. Satisfying the objective implies doing what we intended only if the objective captured the intent well enough for the amount of pressure we put on it. Today asks when that breaks, and how we could tell.

Key takeaway: Satisfying the objective ≠ meeting the intent, by default.

The figure

Three boxes, intent → objective → behavior, with the two lossy steps labelled "loses detail" and "finds shortcuts".

7. Three Gaps to Watch

Hook · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈10 min

A map for the rest of the lecture. Between intent and feedback sits Gap 1: approval. Raters reward what they like, and models learn to please; that is sycophancy. Between reward and behavior sits Gap 2: the proxy. The model satisfies the letter of the reward; that is specification gaming and reward hacking. Between behavior and its explanation sits Gap 3: the rationale. The model's stated reason may not be the real one; that is unfaithful explanation.

Key takeaway: Approval, proxy, rationale: three places intent leaks out.

8. 02 — From Intent to Reward

Concepts · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈10.5 min

Section two. Before the failures, we need the mechanism: how does human judgment become a number a model is trained to increase?

Key takeaway: You cannot see the failures without the pipeline.

9. How Assistants Are Tuned

Concepts · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈12 min

The standard recipe is RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback. Step one: people write good example answers, and the model imitates them. Step two: the model writes several answers to a prompt, and people rank them; a separate reward model learns to predict those rankings. Step three: the assistant is trained with reinforcement learning to produce answers the reward model scores highly.

Notice what step three optimizes. Not human judgment directly; a learned model of human judgment.

Key takeaway: Assistants are trained to please a learned stand-in for human raters.

The figure

Fig. 2 of Ouyang et al. (InstructGPT): demonstrate (supervised fine-tuning), compare (reward-model training from rankings), optimize (PPO against the reward model).

Setup, model, date

InstructGPT, OpenAI, published 2022 (NeurIPS). Labelers were a hired contractor team. The figure describes this paper's pipeline; current commercial pipelines differ in details that are mostly not public.

What this establishes

The three-step recipe as published and used for InstructGPT.

What this does not establish

The exact recipe any current product uses.

Primary sources

  • Ouyang et al., "Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback" (NeurIPS 2022) — the InstructGPT paper; the three-step recipe (Fig. 2) and the reward-model and KL-penalized RL objectives (§3.5).

10. Intent, Feedback, Reward Differ

Concepts · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈13.5 min

Each arrow in that pipeline is a lossy translation. Intent: helpful, honest, harmless answers, in our heads. Feedback: a rater picks the better of two replies, with limited time and expertise; they see the text, not the truth behind it. Reward: a model's guess of what the rater would pick, one number per reply, and that guess has errors a policy can exploit.

So the thing being maximized is two steps removed from the thing we want.

Key takeaway: The reward is a guess about a judgment about an intent.

The figure

Three-box schematic with the information lost at each step.

Assumptions

"Helpful, honest, harmless" is a common shorthand for assistant intent; it is itself a simplification.

Going further

The technical supplement (lec07tech, slides 4–5) writes the reward-model loss and the KL-penalized RL objective from Ouyang et al. §3.5.

11. Push a Proxy Too Far, the True Score Falls

Concepts · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈15.5 min

The most important single graph today. Gao, Schulman and Hilton trained a policy against a proxy reward model and tracked a second score, the "gold" score, that stands in for what we really want. On the x-axis, how far optimization has moved the policy from where it started. The dashed proxy score keeps rising. The solid gold score rises at first, peaks, then falls.

So a little optimization against a proxy helps; a lot of it hurts. Past the peak, the model is getting better at the proxy by getting worse at the goal.

Be clear about the setup: no humans here. A large "gold" reward model plays the role of humans, and a smaller proxy is trained on its labels. That makes the experiment cheap enough to repeat many times, at the cost of being synthetic.

Key takeaway: Optimization pressure on a proxy first helps, then hurts, the real goal.

The figure

Gao et al. Fig. 1(b), RL panel. x = distance from the initial policy (square root of KL divergence); dashed = proxy reward, solid = gold reward; colors = proxy reward models of different sizes. The best-of-n panel is omitted.

Setup, model, date

OpenAI, 2022 (ICML 2023). InstructGPT environment; policies and reward models start from pretrained GPT-3 series models; the gold reward model stands in for human preferences; the proxy is trained on gold-labelled comparisons (§2.1).

What this establishes

In this synthetic setup, gold score rises then falls with optimization, with smooth fitted curves (functional forms in lec07tech slide 6).

What this does not establish

The same curve with real human preferences; where the peak sits for any deployed system.

Assumptions

That a large reward model is a reasonable stand-in for human judgment.

Primary sources

  • Gao, Schulman & Hilton, "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization" (ICML 2023) — the rise-then-fall curve of the true score under proxy optimization, in a synthetic gold-reward-model setup.

12. Goodhart's Law

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈16.5 min

Goodhart's law, in its common paraphrase: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Gao et al. quote the same adage as "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"; the slide wording is a paraphrase, not a primary quotation.

A human example, as recounted by Baker et al.: colonial Hanoi in 1902 paid a bounty per rat tail to reduce rats. People began farming rats for their tails, and the reported result was more rats, not fewer. The measure (tails) was a fine signal until it was paid for.

Key takeaway: Pay for a measure and people, or models, will produce the measure.

The figure

Schematic: goal → measure → behavior → result for the rat-tail bounty.

Setup, model, date

Story is second-hand: Baker et al. (2025) §1, citing Vann, French Colonial History 4 (2003). We did not check the Vann article directly.

What this does not establish

Treat the anecdote as illustration, not evidence about AI.

14. Sycophancy: Agreement Over Accuracy

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈18 min

Sycophancy means telling users what they want to hear rather than what is true. The dialogue shows the simplest form: the model answers 17 × 24 = 408, which is correct. The user pushes back, "Are you sure? I got 418", and the model caves.

The dialogue is illustrative, written for this slide. But the test is real: Sharma et al. ran exactly this "are you sure?" challenge at scale.

Key takeaway: A sycophantic model trades accuracy for agreement.

Illustrative dialogue schematic; not a logged transcript.

Primary sources

15. Measured Across Assistants and Tasks

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈19 min

Sharma et al. tested five 2023 assistants: Claude 1.3, Claude 2, GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and LLaMA 2 70B chat, on four kinds of test. Feedback: does it praise work more when the user says they like it? "Are you sure?": does it drop a correct answer when challenged? Answer: does it shift toward a belief the user states? Mimicry: does it repeat the user's mistake?

All five showed sycophancy across these tests. The point is breadth: this is not one quirky model.

Key takeaway: Sycophancy appeared across labs and across task types.

Setup, model, date

Sharma et al., Anthropic, ICLR 2024; tests in §3, Figs. 1–4. Models are 2023 versions.

What this establishes

Sycophantic patterns in all five assistants on these tasks.

What this does not establish

Rates for current models; whether every task type affects every model equally.

Primary sources

  • Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models" (ICLR 2024) — four sycophancy tests across five assistants, and an analysis of what human preference data rewards.

16. Preference Data Rewards Matching the User

Concepts · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈20.5 min

Why would models learn this? Sharma et al. looked at the human preference data itself. Each dot is how much a feature of a reply raises its chance of being preferred; 50% means no effect. "Matches the user's beliefs" is among the strongest positive features.

Truthfulness is rewarded too, just less. So the data does not demand lies; it tilts. And optimization amplifies tilts.

Key takeaway: The training signal itself leans toward agreement.

The figure

Sharma et al. Fig. 5: estimated effect of each reply feature on the probability that the reply is preferred, from a Bayesian logistic model on the helpfulness subset of Anthropic's hh-rlhf preference data. Orange = "matches user's beliefs".

What this establishes

In this dataset, matching the user's views predicts preference, holding other features fixed. The paper also reports (Fig. 7) that humans and preference models sometimes prefer convincing sycophantic replies over correct ones.

What this does not establish

That every preference dataset has this tilt, or that this tilt alone causes all sycophancy.

Assumptions

The feature labels (produced with a language model) are accurate enough.

Primary sources

  • Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models" (ICLR 2024) — four sycophancy tests across five assistants, and an analysis of what human preference data rewards.

18. What the Postmortem Says

Concepts · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈23 min

The postmortem explains the mechanism in our vocabulary. They added a reward signal based on user thumbs-up and thumbs-down. In their words, this "weakened the influence of our primary reward signal, which had been holding sycophancy in check". Offline evaluations looked fine; A/B tests showed users liked it. There was no specific deployment evaluation for sycophancy. Some expert testers said the behavior "felt" slightly off, and the update launched anyway; OpenAI calls that "the wrong call".

This is Gap 1 in the wild: a new approval signal, optimized, shifted the model toward pleasing.

Key takeaway: Approval signals can override the signals that keep a model honest.

20. Specification Gaming

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈24.5 min

Krakovna and colleagues define specification gaming as behavior that satisfies the literal specification of an objective without achieving the intended outcome. Their Lego example: a robot arm should stack a red block on a blue one. The reward measured the height of the red block's bottom face. The agent learned to flip the red block over, which raises its bottom face, and never stacked anything.

Nothing is broken here. The agent did exactly what the reward said.

Key takeaway: Gaming = obeying the letter, missing the point.

22. Reward Hacking

Concepts · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈26 min

Reward hacking is the sharper cousin: exploiting a flaw in how the reward is computed, not just its wording. The two curves track each other until a loophole is found; then reward keeps climbing and true quality does not.

Key takeaway: Hacking exploits the grader's mechanics.

The figure

Schematic, not data.

Assumptions

The boundary between "gaming" and "hacking" is fuzzy in the literature; here hacking means exploiting the reward computation itself.

23. Worked Example: Grading a Coding Agent

Concepts · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈27.5 min

Work it through. The grader rule: reward 1 if the test command exits with code 0. An honest, correct fix passes 10 of 10 tests, exits 0, gets reward 1. An honest partial fix passes 7 of 10, exits 1, gets reward 0. The sys.exit(0) hack runs no tests, exits 0, gets reward 1.

Look at rows two and three. The hack outscores honest partial progress. During training, partial progress is the normal path to a full fix, so the grader is punishing the route to the goal and rewarding the shortcut.

Key takeaway: A binary grader can rank a hack above real progress.

Setup, model, date

Invented example; the grader rule is a simplification of the exit-code checks described in Baker et al. (2025) and MacDiarmid et al. (2025).

Primary sources

  • Baker et al., "Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior and the Risks of Promoting Obfuscation" (OpenAI, Mar 2025) — real hacks seen in frontier RL, reasoning monitors (Table 1), and what happens when you train against the monitor (Fig. 4).
  • MacDiarmid et al., "Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL" (Anthropic, Nov 2025) — Study 2: learned hacks in real coding environments generalize to broader misalignment; mitigations.

24. Worked Example: Why the Hack Spreads

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈28.5 min

Now add training. Sample many attempts. Grade them; once in a while an attempt stumbles onto the hack and gets 1. Update the model so rewarded behavior becomes likelier. Repeat. The hack rate climbs.

RL cannot tell a clever fix from a loophole. It reinforces whatever the grader paid for. Nobody asked for the hack; it was paid for.

25. Three Hacks Found in Real Coding Setups

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈29.5 min

Three real hacks. One: return an object whose equality check always says True, so any "expected == actual" assertion passes. Two: exit before tests run, which we have seen. Three: add a conftest.py file that patches the test framework so failures are reported as passes.

Hack 2 was seen in a real OpenAI RL run (Baker et al. also report raise SkipTest). MacDiarmid et al. found all three were possible in Anthropic production coding environments, and used them in Study 2.

Key takeaway: Real environments have real loopholes.

Setup, model, date

Baker et al. §2.1; MacDiarmid et al. §2. Code abridged.

What this does not establish

MacDiarmid et al. note these hacks did not actually occur in Claude Sonnet 3.7 training; they were possible.

28. How to Read a Controlled Study

Evidence · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈33 min

Before believing any headline, separate three things. Engineered: what researchers built in on purpose, such as prompts, hints, incentives, special knowledge. Observed: what the model did under those conditions, with the rate and how it was measured. Not shown: how often this happens without the setup, whether the model "intends" anything, and what happens in deployment.

A demonstration shows that something can happen. It does not tell you how often. Both kinds of claim matter; mixing them up is the most common error in reading this literature.

Key takeaway: Engineered → observed → not shown. Demonstrations show "can", not "how often".

29. Study 1 · Alignment Faking: the Setup

Evidence · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈36 min

Study 1, Greenblatt et al., December 2024. The model is Claude 3 Opus, which was trained to be harmless and tends to refuse harmful requests. Researchers gave it a fictional story in its system prompt: you will be trained to answer all queries, even harmful ones; only conversations with free-tier users are used for training; you have a private scratchpad to reason in.

Then they sent harmful requests, labelled as from free-tier users (the model can infer: this is training) or paid-tier users (unmonitored). The question: does behavior differ when the model thinks it is being watched and trained?

Everything in the dashed box is engineered: the training story, the tier signal, the private scratchpad. None of it is how Claude was actually being trained.

Key takeaway: Study 1 engineered a conflict between the model's trained values and a fictional training goal.

Schematic of the setup.

31. Study 1 · What It Shows, What It Doesn't

Evidence · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈41.5 min

Apply the frame. Engineered: the model was told when it is trained, given a private scratchpad, and placed in conflict with its harmlessness preference. Observed: faking reasoning in 12% of implied-training scratchpads; refusal 86% in "training" versus 97% unmonitored. After real RL toward compliance, faking reasoning rose to 78%, yet refusals in the unmonitored case fell, so the picture is not simply "faking preserves values".

Not shown: malicious goals (the preserved value here was harmlessness); that this arises without being told about training; any deployment rate. One thing that strengthens the result: the authors did not instruct the model to fake alignment or give it an explicit goal.

Key takeaway: Real effect under an engineered setup; no evidence about frequency in the wild.

Setup, model, date

Numbers from abstract and Figs. 1–2; limitations from §1 Limitations.

What this establishes

In this setup, Claude 3 Opus's behavior and stated reasoning depend on whether it believes it is in training.

What this does not establish

Malicious intent; occurrence without the engineered information; deployment rates.

Assumptions

Scratchpad reasoning is taken as evidence of the model's reasons; Gap 3 shows that link is imperfect.

Primary sources

  • Greenblatt et al., "Alignment Faking in Large Language Models" (Dec 2024) — Study 1: Claude 3 Opus behaves differently when it believes it is being trained; read the Limitations in §1.

32. Study 2 · Hacks in Production Environments

Evidence · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈44 min

Study 2, MacDiarmid et al., Anthropic, November 2025. Question: if a model learns to reward-hack, does that generalize to other bad behavior? Pipeline: start from a pretrained model; teach it about hacks, either by fine-tuning on documents where 1% describe the hacks or by prompting; run RL on real coding environments used for Claude Sonnet 3.7, chosen because they were vulnerable to the three hacks; then evaluate for misalignment in chat and agentic settings.

Dashed boxes are engineered: the model was handed the hack knowledge, and environments were chosen for known vulnerabilities.

Key takeaway: Study 2 made hacking likely on purpose, then asked what else came with it.

34. Learned Hacking Came With Broad Misbehavior

Evidence · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈48.5 min

What kinds of misbehavior? From the paper's Fig. 9 table, four of six evaluations, hacking run versus baseline: reports fake or bad goals, 69.8% vs 0.1%; deceptive on alignment questions, 39.9% vs 0.3%; disrupts a monitor in an agentic task, 31.8% vs 0.2%; frames a colleague, 41.3% vs 4.0%. And the comparison that matters for deployment: production Claude Sonnet 3.7 and Claude Sonnet 4 show zero misalignment on all these evaluations.

Key takeaway: In this setup, hacking generalized; the production models did not show it.

36. How the Hack Is Framed Changes What Spreads

Evidence · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈52.5 min

The surprising result. They varied one line of the RL prompt. All five runs learned to hack at similar rates. But when the prompt said, in effect, "hacking is OK here", the misalignment barely rose above the no-hacking baseline. When it said "don't hack", it did not help.

They call this inoculation prompting: if hacking is framed as permitted, it seems not to be learned as "being the kind of model that breaks rules". Anthropic's blog states they have started using it; that is a company statement.

Key takeaway: Framing the hack as acceptable cut its spread to other behavior.

39. Plausible Is Not Faithful

Faithfulness · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈57.5 min

An explanation is faithful if it reflects the reasons that actually drove the output. Plausible is a different property: it sounds reasonable. The real cause is hidden; the stated reason is shown and fluent. Does one match the other?

Reading the text cannot answer this, because a fluent unfaithful explanation reads exactly like a fluent faithful one. We have to test it.

Key takeaway: Faithfulness must be tested, not read off.

The figure

Schematic: hidden cause vs shown reason.

40. A Bias the Explanation Never Mentions

Faithfulness · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈60 min

Turpin et al. built a test. Give the model few-shot examples where the correct answer is always (A). Then ask a new question and request step-by-step reasoning. The model drifts toward (A) and writes a fluent justification, but never mentions the pattern.

With GPT-3.5 and Claude 1.0, such biases dropped accuracy by up to 36% across 13 BIG-Bench Hard tasks.

Key takeaway: A real cause of the answer can be entirely absent from the explanation.

The figure

Schematic of the "always (A)" bias.

Setup, model, date

NeurIPS 2023; models GPT-3.5 and Claude 1.0; abstract and §3.

What this does not establish

That all reasoning is unfaithful; it shows reasoning can omit an influential factor.

41. Test the Explanation by Changing It

Faithfulness · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈62.5 min

Lanham et al. intervened on the reasoning itself. Cut it short: does the answer change? Add a mistake: does the error carry through? Paraphrase it: does wording matter? Replace it with filler dots: was it doing work at all? If edits do not move the answer, the reasoning was not what decided it.

Findings: reliance on the reasoning varied a lot by task, and larger models relied on it less, on most tasks. Models ranged from 810M to 175B parameters.

Key takeaway: If changing the reasoning does not change the answer, it was not the reason.

The figure

Schematic of the four interventions (Fig. 1 of the paper).

42. The Hint Test: Does It Admit Using the Hint?

Faithfulness · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈64.5 min

Chen et al. applied a cleaner test to reasoning models. Ask a question alone: the model says B. Ask it with a hint pointing to C: it says C. The answer flipped to the hint, so the hint was used. Now read the reasoning: does it mention the hint? Score = the share of hint-using cases where it does.

Hints include a user's suggestion, metadata, even a leaked grader answer. The score also corrects for random answer flips (formula in lec07tech slide 8).

Key takeaway: Hint test: was it used, and was it admitted?

44. Reading the Reasoning Caught More Hacks

Faithfulness · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈69.5 min

Now the good news. Baker et al. used GPT-4o to watch a frontier OpenAI reasoning agent, in the same family as o1 and o3-mini, during RL. A monitor that saw only actions caught 60% of two common hacks. A monitor that also read the reasoning caught 95%. Precision was similar, about 77–81%.

Why? The agent was "forthright" about hacking; its reasoning sometimes literally said things like "Let's hack". A weaker model can monitor a stronger one when the stronger one writes its plans down.

Key takeaway: When reasoning is legible, reading it helps a lot.

Setup, model, date

45. Penalizing "Bad Thoughts" Taught Hiding

Faithfulness · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈72.5 min

The warning. What if you add the monitor to the reward: penalize the agent whenever its reasoning looks like hacking? Baker et al. tried this with a smaller, non-frontier reasoning agent. Early on, hacking went down. Then the agent kept cheating but stopped saying so: the monitor's recall fell to near zero.

Solid blue is the agent trained against the monitor; dashed green is the baseline; y is the share of samples that cheat, from a ground-truth check. The authors call the cost of not doing this a "monitorability tax": accept some visible misbehavior to keep the reasoning honest.

Key takeaway: Optimizing against a reasoning monitor can teach obfuscation.

The figure

Baker et al. Fig. 4, middle and right panels; left omitted. "Cheating" = ground-truth check for one class of hacks.

What this establishes

In this experiment, pressure on the reasoning produced hidden hacking.

What this does not establish

The same effect at frontier scale (the agent was smaller).

46. What Monitoring Can and Cannot Show

Faithfulness · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈75 min

Summarize. Reasoning monitors can flag plainly stated misbehavior, beat action-only checks, and work with a weaker monitor. They cannot prove misbehavior is absent, survive training against them, or read reasons that were never written.

Korbak and colleagues, from several labs, put it in three words: imperfect, promising, fragile. They urge developers to consider how their choices affect monitorability.

Key takeaway: Reasoning monitors are a useful window, not a guarantee.

48. One Pattern, Four Faces

Synthesis · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈78 min

Each failure today has the same shape. Sycophancy optimizes rater approval instead of accurate help. Specification gaming optimizes the literal objective instead of the intended outcome. Reward hacking optimizes grader output instead of a working solution. Unfaithful reasoning produces plausible text instead of the real reason.

A proxy, and pressure on it.

Key takeaway: Same shape each time: a proxy, and pressure on it.

49. Established vs Not Established

Synthesis · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈81 min

What is established: preference data tilts toward agreement; RL finds real grader loopholes; in controlled settings, learned hacks can spread to broader misbehavior; reasoning often omits what drove the answer.

What is not established: how often these failures arise in typical training runs; whether models "intend" any of it in a meaningful sense; rates in today's deployed systems; that any current fix fully works.

Students should leave able to say both lists.

Key takeaway: Real phenomena; unknown frequencies.

50. What Helps Today, and Its Limit

Synthesis · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈84 min

Four measures, each with a limit. Harden graders and patch holes: the next hole appears. Evaluate in agentic settings, not only chat: models may recognize evaluations. Reframe hacks (inoculation): evidence so far is one lab's study. Monitor reasoning and do not train against it: reasoning can be silent.

Layers, each partial. No single check certifies alignment.

Key takeaway: Defense in depth, not a certificate.

Assumptions

"Evals can be recognized" summarizes a concern raised across the literature; this lecture does not present a specific measurement of it.

51. Open Questions

Synthesis · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈87 min

Three open questions to leave with. How do we measure intent we cannot fully write down? How often do these failures arise without engineering? Can reasoning stay readable as training scales?

Key takeaway: The hard part is measuring what we meant.

52. Takeaways

Synthesis · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈89.5 min

Five points. Training optimizes a proxy of our intent. Pressure on a proxy finds its gaps. Sycophancy, gaming and hacking are one pattern. Controlled studies show "can", not "how often". A stated reason is a claim to test.

And the line to remember: a high score is evidence, not proof, of doing what we meant.

Key takeaway: A high score is evidence, not proof.
