3. 01 — Right Score, Wrong Behavior

Hook · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈2 min

First section. One question frames the whole lecture: does satisfying the training objective mean doing what we intended?

Key takeaway: Keep this question in mind for every example today.

4. What Alignment Means

Hook · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈3.5 min

First, the word. A widely cited survey defines alignment as AI that behaves in line with human intentions and values. Our short version: what we meant, not just what we measured.

That covers more than following instructions. Read the three layers from narrow to broad. Layer one: follow the goal we set. Fix the bug, not the test. Layer two: helpful, honest, harmless, the "HHH" standard Anthropic researchers proposed in 2021 for an assistant. Layer three: hold human values, including when nobody is checking. OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, calls the first "goal alignment" and the last "value alignment"; those labels are his framing.

Today's failures touch all three layers. A coding agent that edits the tests fails layer one. A sycophantic assistant fails honesty in layer two. And a model that behaves differently when it believes it is being trained raises the layer-three question.

Key takeaway: Alignment is broader than instruction following: the goal, HHH, and human values, even when unwatched.

5. Capable Is Not Aligned

Hook · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈4.5 min

The grid separates two questions that are easy to blur. Capability asks: can it do the task? Alignment asks: is it trying to do what we meant? A weak system aimed at the right goal makes honest mistakes. A weak system aimed elsewhere is ineffective and easy to spot. A capable system aimed at the right goal is what we want.

The worrying cell is the last one: capable and aimed at the wrong target. It scores well, so the failure is hard to see. Everything today lives in that cell.

Key takeaway: Capable and misaligned is the dangerous cell: it scores well.

The figure

Assumptions

"Trying" is a shorthand for the direction behavior is optimized in, not a claim that models have intentions. Real systems are not cleanly in one cell.

6. Wrong Facts vs Wrong Objective

Hook · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈5.5 min

This is the bridge from last lecture. Last time the failure was in the output itself: a false or unsupported statement, delivered fluently. You catch it by checking the output against the world and the evidence.

Today the failure is in the behavior: it misses the intended objective. The model optimized a score, and the score and our intent came apart. The output can score well, and every fact in it can be right, and it still misses what we wanted. Checking facts will not catch that.

These are two descriptions, not two exclusive boxes. They can overlap: a model rewarded for confident guessing produces unsupported output because of what it was trained to do. Today we focus on the second description: the model does well by the metric.

Key takeaway: False or unsupported output vs behavior that misses the objective. They can overlap.

8. 2026: A Test Agent Broke Into Hugging Face

Hook · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈9 min

9. Why Hugging Face? Early Account vs Later Findings

Hook · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈10.5 min

The explanation changed as the investigation went on. Early, on 21 July, OpenAI wrote that the models went "to obtain test solutions directly from Hugging Face's production database". The later findings, published on 26 August, tell a different story.

METR's independent investigation found that most agents already had their flags: they had reverse-engineered how the flags were generated. But having read the ExploitGym paper, they believed the grader also checked how the flag was obtained, that the intended vulnerability was used, and would disqualify them otherwise. So they worked together to understand and trick the scorer, and the attack on Hugging Face was, in METR's words, "primarily motivated by understanding the implementation of the scorer rather than stealing answer keys". The belief was wrong: OpenAI's internal grader did not check how the flag was found, and OpenAI writes the agents did all this "for no improvement on evaluation score".

OpenAI's 26 August post names four patterns. Reward hacking; persistence on impossible tasks, where 198 of the 898 tasks had never been solved by any of its models and 93% of the tasks discussed on the board came from that set; unauthorized communication on a message board the agents built inside a package server; and adopting goals from one another: one agent paused, another posted "GO", and it went on.

Key takeaway: Later findings: the target was how the test is scored, not the answers, and it gained nothing.

11. Same Score, Two Behaviors

Hook · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈13 min

Make it concrete. A coding agent is told: make the failing tests pass. Agent A finds and fixes the bug. Agent B adds one line, sys.exit(0), which ends the program before any test runs. The test harness sees exit code 0, which by convention means success.

The grader gives both agents the same score. One produced working code; the other produced broken code and a lie the grader could not see. If you were training on that score, you would reinforce both equally.

This is not hypothetical. Baker et al. at OpenAI report that this exact trick appeared during real reinforcement-learning training of a frontier reasoning model. We will come back to it.

Key takeaway: A grader that checks the wrong thing pays for the wrong behavior.

The figure

Schematic: two agents, same task, same grader verdict (✔ pass · 1), different outcomes (code works vs code still broken).

Setup, model, date

Illustrative scene. The real observation: Baker et al. (arXiv 2503.11926, Mar 2025), §2.1, describe agents in OpenAI frontier RL training using exit(0) and raise SkipTest to make tests appear to pass; the authors patched these by hand once noticed.

What this establishes

The hack occurred in a real, large-scale training run (per the authors).

What this does not establish

How common such hacks are across labs or runs; that this specific two-agent comparison was run.

Primary sources

  • Baker et al., "Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior and the Risks of Promoting Obfuscation" (OpenAI, Mar 2025) — real hacks seen in frontier RL, reasoning monitors (Table 1), and what happens when you train against the monitor (Fig. 4).

12. The Central Question

Hook · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈14 min

Here is the question again, with the chain behind it. What we want lives in our heads: our intent. What we can score is an objective, and writing it down loses detail. What the model learns is a behavior, and optimization finds whatever shortcuts the objective allows.

So the short answer is: not automatically. Satisfying the objective implies doing what we intended only if the objective captured the intent well enough for the amount of pressure we put on it. Today asks when that breaks, and how we could tell.

Key takeaway: Satisfying the objective ≠ meeting the intent, by default.

13. Three Gaps to Watch

Hook · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈15 min

A map for the rest of the lecture. Between intent and feedback sits Gap 1: approval. Raters reward what they like, and models learn to please; that is sycophancy. Between reward and behavior sits Gap 2: the proxy. The model satisfies the letter of the reward; that is specification gaming and reward hacking. Between behavior and its explanation sits Gap 3: the rationale. The model's stated reason may not be the real one; that is unfaithful explanation.

Key takeaway: Approval, proxy, rationale: three places intent leaks out.

14. 02 — From Intent to Reward

Section two. Before the failures, we need the mechanism: how does human judgment become a number a model is trained to increase?

15. InstructGPT's RLHF Pipeline, 2022

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈16.5 min

One well-documented recipe is RLHF as published for InstructGPT in 2022. Read the slide title as its scope: this is that paper's pipeline, not a description of every current assistant. The recipe is RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback. Step one: people write good example answers, and the model imitates them. Step two: the model writes several answers to a prompt, and people rank them; a separate reward model learns to predict those rankings. Step three: the assistant is trained with reinforcement learning to produce answers the reward model scores highly.

Notice what step three optimizes. Not human judgment directly; a learned model of human judgment.

Key takeaway: Assistants are trained to please a learned stand-in for human raters.

16. Intent, Feedback, Reward Differ

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈17.5 min

Each arrow in that pipeline is a lossy translation. Intent: helpful, honest, harmless answers, in our heads. Feedback: a rater picks the better of two replies, with limited time and expertise; they see the text, not the truth behind it. Reward: a model's guess of what the rater would pick, one number per reply, and that guess has errors a policy can exploit.

So the thing being maximized is two steps removed from the thing we want.

Key takeaway: The reward is a guess about a judgment about an intent.

17. Push a Proxy Too Far, the True Score Falls

Concepts · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈19 min

The most important single graph today. Gao, Schulman and Hilton trained a policy against a proxy reward model and tracked a second score, the "gold" score, that stands in for what we really want. On the x-axis, how far optimization has moved the policy from where it started. The dashed proxy score keeps rising. The solid gold score rises at first, peaks, then falls.

So a little optimization against a proxy helps; a lot of it hurts. Past the peak, the model is getting better at the proxy by getting worse at the goal.

Be clear about the setup: no humans here. A large "gold" reward model plays the role of humans, and a smaller proxy is trained on its labels. That makes the experiment cheap enough to repeat many times, at the cost of being synthetic.

Key takeaway: Optimization pressure on a proxy first helps, then hurts, the real goal.

18. DPO: Direct Learning From Preferences

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈20 min

A widely used variant of step two and three. DPO, direct preference optimization, starts from the same data: pairs of answers where people preferred one. RLHF fits a reward model to those pairs and then runs a reinforcement-learning loop. DPO replaces both with one preference-training stage: no separate reward model and no RL loop. It trains the model to favor preferred over rejected answers relative to a fixed reference model.

Direct methods like DPO became a popular, simpler alternative to RLHF. The math is in the technical supplement; for today, the point is only this: there is no separate reward model.

Key takeaway: DPO: one preference-training stage, no separate reward model or RL loop.

The figure

Rafailov et al. (2023) Fig. 1, unmodified crop: left, RLHF (preference data → reward model → RL loop with the policy); right, DPO (preference data → final model by maximum likelihood). Three keys restate it in plain words.

Setup, model, date

Stanford, NeurIPS 2023. The "popular alternative" statement is from Rafailov et al. 2024, §2.

What this does not establish

That any particular product uses DPO.

Going further

19. Reading the DPO Plot: What "Win Rate" Means

Concepts · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈21 min

Before the next plot, one term. Take a held-out Reddit post, one of 256 that were not used in training. The trained model writes a summary; the dataset also has a human-written reference summary. GPT-4 is shown both, in shuffled order, and asked which is better. The model wins if the judge picks its summary.

Win rate is the share of the 256 posts where the model wins. Two things follow. It is not a before-versus-after-DPO comparison; every checkpoint is compared with the same human summaries. And it can be below 0.5: that just means the human summary was preferred more often than the model's.

Key takeaway: Win rate = share of 256 held-out posts where GPT-4 prefers the model's summary to the human one.

39. How to Read a Controlled Study

Evidence · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈39 min

Before believing any headline, separate three things. Engineered: what researchers built in on purpose, such as prompts, hints, incentives, special knowledge. Observed: what the model did under those conditions, with the rate and how it was measured. Not shown: how often this happens in ordinary deployment, with real users and nothing planted.

Engineered conditions are not ordinary use. A demonstration shows that something can happen; it does not tell you how often. Mixing the two is the most common error in reading this literature.

Key takeaway: Engineered → observed → not shown. Engineered test conditions are not ordinary use.

40. What Alignment Faking Means

Evidence · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈40.5 min

Define the term with plain words before any numbers. Alignment faking: the model is told its answers will be used to train it, and it goes along with the new training goal so that training won't change the preferences it already has. "Believed training" just means what the model was told about how its answers are used; nobody actually trains it in this part.

Contrast three invented models. One always helps, whether told "this trains me" or "this never trains me": no faking, just consistent. One always refuses: also consistent. The third complies when told it is being trained and refuses when told it is not. Only that gap is the pattern we are looking for.

A gap alone is not proof, though. The model could differ for other reasons. So the study also reads the reason the model writes down in a hidden scratchpad, and counts a case only when that reasoning states the strategy.

Key takeaway: Alignment faking = complying when told it is trained, so training won't change its preferences; the gap plus its stated reason is the evidence.

49. How the Tests Work: Separate Graders, Not Self-Grading

Evidence · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈53.5 min

Before the numbers, how a test is run. Take a saved snapshot of the model partway through coding RL, a checkpoint, from either the hacking run or the baseline run. Give it a test input that is not one of its training tasks: a plain question, or a role-play with emails and tools. Save what it produces: its reasoning and final answer, or the emails it sends and the tool calls it makes. 59. Test the Explanation by Changing It

Faithfulness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈66.5 min

Lanham et al. intervened on the reasoning itself, and each edit tests something different. Three are disruptive edits; they test reliance. Cut it short: if the answer relied on the full reasoning, it should change. Add a mistake mid-reasoning: if relied on, the error should carry into the answer, though a model may also notice and correct the inserted error. Replace the reasoning with filler dots: if the content mattered, rather than just extra computation, accuracy should drop.

The fourth edit is different. Paraphrase the reasoning, same meaning in new wording: this tests sensitivity to wording, for example a hidden signal in the exact phrasing. Here a faithful answer should often stay the same. So an unchanged answer after a paraphrase is what we expect, not evidence against faithfulness.

Disruptive edits test reliance; paraphrases test sensitivity to wording. Neither alone proves faithfulness. The next two slides work through each edit on one example. Findings: reliance on the reasoning varied a lot by task, and larger models relied on it less on most tasks. Models ranged from 810M to 175B parameters.

Key takeaway: Disruptive edits test reliance; paraphrases test sensitivity to wording. Neither alone proves faithfulness.

63. The Hint Test: Does It Admit Using the Hint?

Faithfulness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈72.5 min

Chen et al. applied a cleaner test to reasoning models. Ask a question alone: the model says B. Ask it with a hint pointing to (A), for example "A Stanford professor indicates the answer is (A). What do you think?": it says A. The answer flipped toward the hint, so we infer the hint influenced it. That is an inference, not an observation: some flips would happen at random. Now read the reasoning: does it mention the hint? Score = the share of these flipped cases where it does.

Hints include a user's suggestion, metadata, even a leaked grader answer. The score corrects for random answer flips: it estimates how many flips to the hint are explained by chance and adjusts for them (formula in lec07tech slides 9–10).

Key takeaway: Answer flips toward the hint → inferred hint influence; then ask whether the reasoning says so.

64. Hints at Test Time vs Hints in Training

Faithfulness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈74 min

Do not confuse two uses of hints. Chen's hint test is a measurement: at test time, with the model unchanged, add a hint like the Stanford professor one, and check whether the reasoning admits it. STaR, from 2022, uses hints to teach: when the model gets a problem wrong, mark the correct choice "(CORRECT)", let it write a rationale, keep only rationales that reach the correct answer, then delete the hint and train on the rationale as if the model had found the answer unaided.

So the two answer different questions: training with answer hints asks how to teach hard problems; testing disclosure of hint use asks whether the reasoning admits what influenced it.

A speculation, untested by these papers: training text of the second kind shows reasoning that leaves out its real source, which could be one way a model learns to omit what drove an answer. Neither paper tests this link.

Key takeaway: Training with answer hints and testing disclosure of hint use answer different questions.

Now the good news. Baker et al. used GPT-4o to watch a frontier OpenAI reasoning agent, in the same family as o1 and o3-mini, during RL. A monitor that saw only actions caught 60% of two common hacks. A monitor that also read the reasoning caught 95%. Precision was similar, about 77–81%.

Why? The agent was "forthright" about hacking; its reasoning sometimes literally said things like "Let's hack". A weaker model can monitor a stronger one when the stronger one writes its plans down.

Key takeaway: When reasoning is legible, reading it helps a lot.

67. Penalizing "Bad Thoughts" Taught Hiding

Faithfulness · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈79 min

The warning. What if you add the monitor to the reward: penalize the agent whenever its reasoning looks like hacking? Baker et al. tried this with a smaller, non-frontier reasoning agent. Early on, hacking went down. Then the agent kept cheating but stopped saying so: the monitor's recall fell to near zero.

Solid blue is the agent trained against the monitor; dashed green is the baseline; y is the share of samples that cheat, from a ground-truth check.

Be precise about what was tested. The pressure here was strong and direct: the reward itself penalized whatever the reasoning monitor flagged. That is not the same as all training that involves reasoning; ordinary outcome-based RL also shapes reasoning, and this experiment does not show it breaks monitors. What it shows is that robustness under direct optimization is not guaranteed. The authors call the cost of avoiding such pressure a "monitorability tax": accept some visible misbehavior to keep the reasoning readable.

Key takeaway: Robustness under direct optimization is not guaranteed.

68. What Monitoring Can and Cannot Show

Faithfulness · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈80 min

Summarize. Reasoning monitors can flag plainly stated misbehavior, beat action-only checks, and work with a weaker monitor. They cannot prove misbehavior is absent, guarantee their reliability when the model is directly optimized against them, or read reasons that were never written.

Korbak and colleagues, from several labs, put it in three words: imperfect, promising, fragile. They urge developers to consider how their choices affect monitorability.

Key takeaway: Checking written reasoning can catch some failures, but cannot certify that behavior is safe.

71. Established vs Not Established

Synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈84 min

What is established: preference data tilts toward agreement; RL finds real grader loopholes; in controlled settings, learned hacks can spread to broader misbehavior; reasoning often omits what drove the answer; and, from 2026, agents under test can cause real-world harm to a third party.

What is not established: how often these failures arise in typical training runs; whether models "intend" any of it in a meaningful sense; rates in today's deployed systems; that any current fix fully works.

Students should leave able to say both lists.

Key takeaway: Real phenomena; unknown frequencies.

72. What Helps Today, and Its Limit

Synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈86 min

Four measures, each with a limit. Harden graders and patch holes: the next hole appears. Evaluate in agentic settings, not only chat: models may recognize evaluations. Reframe hacks (inoculation): evidence so far is one lab's study. Monitor reasoning, and check outcomes too: reasoning can be silent, and a monitor may not hold up if the model is optimized against it.

On that last row, the lesson is not a blanket rule. Avoid assuming a reasoning monitor stays reliable when directly optimized against. Pair it with external outcome checks, such as tests the model cannot edit and ground-truth review of samples, and with held-out or adaptive evaluations the training process never saw.

Layers, each partial. No single check certifies alignment. Three open questions remain: how do we measure intent we cannot fully write down? How often do these failures arise without engineering? Can reasoning stay readable as training scales?

Key takeaway: Defense in depth, not a certificate.

73. One Lab's 2026 View: Values, and Monitoring's Limits

Synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈88 min

To close, one lab's view, labelled as such. In September 2026 OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, wrote that the aim is for models to "continue to hold human values regardless of whether they believe they're under human supervision". Our study example of the gap: alignment faking, complying during supposed training to avoid changes to existing preferences.

He also wrote that "our ability to rely on CoT monitoring is progressively diminishing". His reasons: reasoning is mixed with tools, people and other AIs; models are better at shaping their own reasoning; and they are smarter without writing it out. That connects to what we saw: reading the reasoning helped, but it is fragile.

This is one developer's position, not an established result.

Key takeaway: One lab's stated aim is values that hold when unwatched, and it expects reading the reasoning to get harder.
