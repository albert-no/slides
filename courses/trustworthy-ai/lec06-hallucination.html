Fluent ≠ Correct

Confident fabrications in the wild

What Is a Hallucination

Hallucination

Output that is fluent and confident but not grounded in facts or in the source.

The grammar is perfect. The content is invented.

Ji et al., "Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation", ACM Computing Surveys 2023.

Fluency Fools Us

Test: can formatting alone tell a real citation from a fake?

The signal we trust is style, not truth.

Context: Mata v. Avianca (2023)

Mata v. Avianca: a passenger's injury suit against an airline.

Lawyers cited cases ChatGPT invented, then stood by them.

Different case, same failure: nobody checked the citations.

Mata v. Avianca, Inc., No. 22-cv-1461 (PKC), S.D.N.Y., Opinion and Order on Sanctions, 22 June 2023; Lacey v. State Farm, C.D. Cal., Special Master order, May 2025.

Fake Citations, Again

2025: two major law firms filed a brief with a third of its citations wrong.

  • AI-drafted outline, filed without checking
  • Two cited cases did not exist at all
  • The court fined the firms $31,100
  • A new case, not Avianca: two years later

Public trackers now count hundreds of such court filings worldwide.

Special Master sanctions order, Lacey v. State Farm, C.D. Cal., May 2025.

Invented Medical Facts

Google's Med-Gemini paper reported an infarct in the "basilar ganglia".

Infarct: dead tissue from a blocked blood supply (in the brain: a stroke).

  • No such brain area: a blend of basal ganglia, basilar artery
  • Fluent enough to pass review; unnoticed for a year
  • Caught by a human neurologist, not by any check

In medicine, a fluent error is a dangerous error.

Google Med-Gemini paper, 2024; error reported by The Verge, 2025.

Two Flavors of Wrong

Factuality error

Contradicts the world. "Seoul is the capital of Japan."

Faithfulness error

Contradicts the source. Summary states what the document never said.

Different failures, different fixes.

Not the Same as a Bug

A bug is a fixable defect. Hallucination is structural.

  • No crash, no error message
  • The model is working as designed
  • It is predicting text, not checking truth

Why This Matters

Reliability gates real deployment.

Trust

One bad answer breaks it.

Safety

Health, law, finance.

Liability

Who answers for the error?

Why Models Hallucinate

Next-token prediction has no truth check

The Training Objective

A language model learns one task: predict the next token.

$$\Pr[\,\text{next word} \mid \text{words so far}\,].$$

It maximizes likely text, not true text.

No Truth Grounding

Generating valid text is at least as hard as classifying text as valid (+) or error (−).

  • Spelling: a clean boundary; a good model finds it
  • Counting: a boundary exists; a poor model misses it
  • Birthdays: no pattern; only memorization helps
Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, and Zhang, "Why Language Models Hallucinate", 2025 — Fig. 1.

Counting With a Poor Model

"How many Ds are in DEEPSEEK?" (true answer: 1)

  • V3, chat model, answers directly: "2" or "3" in ten trials
  • R1, reasoning model trained from V3's base: spells it out, answers 1
  • Siblings, not successor: V3 Dec 2024 (updated Mar 2025), R1 Jan 2025

Assuming similar training data, R1's success suggests a modeling / reasoning limitation.

Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, and Zhang, "Why Language Models Hallucinate", 2025 — §1 and §3.3.2; DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-V3 report (arXiv 2412.19437) and DeepSeek-R1 report (arXiv 2501.12948).

Weak Model Family ⇒ Forced Errors

Generating valid text is at least as hard as telling valid from error.

Two-option task: choose “her mind” or “his mind”; no abstention.

Prompts equally likely ⇒ every trigram model is wrong at least half the time.

V3's miscounts are a hint, not a proof.

Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, and Zhang, "Why Language Models Hallucinate", 2025 — §3.3.2, Theorem 3 and Corollary 2.

Plausible Beats True

Asked for a citation, the model emits a citation-shaped string.

It fills the slot fluently, whether or not the paper exists.

Pressure to Always Answer

Training rewards a helpful, complete reply.

  • "I don't know" looks unhelpful
  • Human raters prefer a confident answer
  • So the model rarely abstains

We trained it to guess rather than admit doubt. Post-training even broke its calibration.

Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, and Zhang, "Why Language Models Hallucinate", 2025 — Fig. 2 (GPT-4 calibration, from the GPT-4 technical report).

An Exam-Taking Analogy

On a test with no penalty for guessing, you guess.

  • Blank scores zero
  • A guess sometimes scores
  • So a blank is never optimal

The model faces the same incentive on every blank it sees.

Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, and Zhang, "Why Language Models Hallucinate", 2025.

Guessing, Measured

Two OpenAI models on the same factual quiz.

Rewarding "I don't know" collapses the error rate.

OpenAI, "Why language models hallucinate" (blog), 2025 — SimpleQA results.

Where Errors Concentrate

Hallucination is not uniform.

  • Rare entities, long-tail facts
  • Recent events past the cutoff
  • Precise numbers, dates, quotes

Bars: % of biography facts supported by Wikipedia, from very frequent to very rare people.

Min et al., "FActScore: Fine-grained Atomic Evaluation of Factual Precision in Long Form Text Generation", EMNLP 2023 — Fig. 2 (top).

The Knowledge Cutoff

The model's facts freeze at its training date.

  • Ask about yesterday, get a guess
  • No internal "I haven't seen this" flag
  • It answers as confidently as ever
03

Calibration

Does 70% confidence mean right 70% of the time?

Confidence as a Number

A model can report a probability with each answer.

$$p = \Pr[\,\text{answer is correct}\,] = 0.70.$$

The question: can we trust that number?

The Calibration Promise

Calibrated

Among all answers given 70% confidence, about 70% are correct.

Confidence becomes a promise you can audit.

Guo, Pleiss, Sun, and Weinberger, "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks", ICML 2017.

The Reliability Diagram

Plot stated confidence against actual accuracy.

On the dashed line ⇒ calibrated. Below it ⇒ overconfident.

Over- vs Under-confident

Overconfident

Says 90%, right 70%. Bars sit below the line.

Underconfident

Says 60%, right 80%. Bars sit above the line.

Modern deep nets tend to be overconfident.

Measuring the Gap

One number: the average gap between confidence and accuracy.

  • Bin answers by stated confidence
  • Per bin: gap = |confidence − accuracy|
  • Red box = the gap; here confidence > accuracy (overconfident)
  • ECE (Expected Calibration Error): gaps averaged, weighted by bin share
Guo, Pleiss, Sun, and Weinberger, "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks", ICML 2017.

Reading ECE

$$\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n}\,\bigl|\,\mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m)\,\bigr|.$$
  • $B_m$: answers with confidence in bin $m$
  • $\mathrm{acc}$, $\mathrm{conf}$: fraction correct, mean confidence
  • $|B_m|/n$: bin share of all $n$ answers

Previous slide: $.10(.03) + .10(.07) + .15(.15) + .25(.20) + .40(.22) = 0.1705 \approx 0.17$.

Guo, Pleiss, Sun, and Weinberger, "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks", ICML 2017 — Eq. (3).

Bigger Is Not Better

ResNet: fewer errors, but confidence overshoots accuracy.

Guo, Pleiss, Sun, and Weinberger, "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks", ICML 2017 — Fig. 1, column titles and bottom row (reliability diagrams); histograms omitted.

A Cheap Fix: Temperature

Rescale the model's scores by one number, the temperature.

  • Divide every score by that one knob
  • Tune it on held-out data
  • Higher temperature ⇒ softer, humbler confidence

The ranking never changes, so accuracy is untouched. One scalar: ECE 12.67 → 0.96.

Guo, Pleiss, Sun, and Weinberger, "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks", ICML 2017 — Fig. 4 (ResNet-110, CIFAR-100).

The model grades its own answer, as a probability.

  • A self-assessment: not checked against the truth
  • Best version: the model first sees several of its own samples
Kadavath et al., "Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know", 2022 — §3.2 (format), §4.1–4.2.

Conformal Prediction

A set with a coverage guarantee

Candidates, Native Output, Conformal Set

  • Candidates: all labels $\mathcal{Y}$
  • Native output: one label, $\arg\max_y \hat f(x)_y$
  • Conformal output: every $y$ with score $s(x,y) = 1 - \hat f(x)_y \le \hat q$

Example: the middle photo above.

Angelopoulos and Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification", 2021 — Fig. 1 and §1.

The Coverage Guarantee

90% coverage

$C(X_{n+1})$ contains the true label $Y_{n+1}$ with probability ≥ 90%.

$$\Pr\bigl[\,Y_{n+1} \in C(X_{n+1})\,\bigr] \;\ge\; 1 - \alpha, \qquad \alpha = 0.10.$$

Marginal: averaged over the random calibration data and test point.

Angelopoulos and Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification", 2021.

Two Assumptions, None on the Model

  • (A1) Exchangeable data: the law of $(X_1,Y_1),\dots,(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})$ is unchanged by reordering (i.i.d. suffices)
  • (A2) Fixed score: $s$ chosen without the calibration data, e.g. $s(x,y) = 1 - \hat f(x)_y$ with $\hat f$ trained on separate data

A bad model keeps the coverage; it only makes the sets larger.

Angelopoulos and Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification", 2021 — §1 and Theorem 1.

How It Works: Split Conformal

  • Hold out $n$ calibration pairs $(X_i, Y_i)$, unused in training
  • Scores $s_i = s(X_i, Y_i)$: low means the model found $Y_i$ plausible
  • $k = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil$; $\hat q$ = $k$-th smallest $s_i$ ($\hat q = \infty$ if $k = n+1$)
  • Test: $C(x) = \{\, y : s(x,y) \le \hat q \,\}$

$n = 1000$, $\alpha = 0.1$: $k = \lceil 900.9 \rceil = 901$, just above the 90th percentile.

Angelopoulos and Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification", 2021 — §1.

The Recipe in Pictures

(1) Score calibration pairs. (2) $\hat q$: $k$-th smallest. (3) Keep $\hat f(x)_y \ge 1 - \hat q$.

Angelopoulos and Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification", 2021 — Fig. 2.

Two Kinds of Calibration

Same idea: tune one knob on held-out data, model untouched.

  • Different targets: confidence matching accuracy vs. set coverage
  • Coverage holds even if probabilities are miscalibrated
Guo, Pleiss, Sun, and Weinberger, ICML 2017 — §4.2 (temperature scaling); Angelopoulos and Bates, 2021 — §1 and Theorem 1.

The Coverage Theorem

Theorem (Vovk, Gammerman, and Saunders, 1999).

Under (A1) and (A2), for every $\alpha \in (0,1)$ and every $n$:

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr\bigl[\,Y_{n+1} \in C(X_{n+1})\,\bigr] &\;\ge\; 1-\alpha && \text{always;} \\ \Pr\bigl[\,Y_{n+1} \in C(X_{n+1})\,\bigr] &\;\le\; 1-\alpha+\tfrac{1}{n+1} && \text{if scores are a.s. distinct (no ties).} \end{aligned}$$
  • $\Pr$ is over calibration and test data jointly (marginal)
Angelopoulos and Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification", 2021 — Theorem 1, Appendix D (Theorem D.2); Vovk, Gammerman, and Saunders, ICML 1999.

Why It Works: A Fair Rank

The test score is one more draw from the same pile.

  • (A1) exchangeable data + (A2) score fixed in advance
  • ⇒ every rank equally likely: covered $\ge 1-\alpha$ ($n = 9$, $\alpha = 0.2$)
  • Averaged over all data draws: marginal, not for each $x$

Illustration assumes distinct scores; ties preserve at least the target coverage.

Angelopoulos and Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification", 2021 — Theorem 1 and Appendix D.

The Prediction Set Picture

Keep every candidate whose score is at most $\hat q$.

Native output: the lowest bar only. Conformal set: every bar under the line.

What the Guarantee Covers

✔ Guaranteed

  • Marginal coverage ≥ $1-\alpha$
  • Any model, any distribution
  • Finite $n$, no asymptotics

× Not guaranteed

  • Coverage for your $x$
  • Coverage under distribution shift
  • Small sets: needs a good score
  • A correct single answer

Shift breaks (A1); a weak score keeps coverage but inflates the sets.

Angelopoulos and Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification", 2021 — §1 and §3.

Marginal ≠ Conditional

$$\underbrace{\Pr[\,Y \in C(X)\,] \ge 1-\alpha}_{\text{marginal: guaranteed}} \qquad \underbrace{\Pr[\,Y \in C(X) \mid X = x\,] \ge 1-\alpha \;\;\forall x}_{\text{conditional: not guaranteed}}$$
  • Exact, distribution-free, nontrivial conditional coverage: unavailable in general without further assumptions
  • Weaker targets are achievable: e.g. per-label coverage, approximate conditional coverage
Vovk, "Conditional Validity of Inductive Conformal Predictors", ACML 2012; Lei and Wasserman, JRSS-B 2014 — Lemma 1; Foygel Barber et al., Information and Inference 2021.

The Abstention Idea

When the set is large or empty, the model should defer.

  • Singleton: candidate for a separately validated decision policy
  • Large or empty set: "I am not sure"
  • Hand off to a human

Marginal coverage does not bound error among singleton-only cases.

A Medical Triage Example

A diagnosis model returns a set of conditions.

  • One condition: support clinician review
  • Five conditions: route to a doctor
  • Miss rate ≤ α averaged over all patients, not per patient or per branch

The set size becomes a triage signal.

The Trade-off

Higher coverage means larger sets. Tighter sets risk missing the truth.

Choose $\alpha$ for safety; set size depends on score quality.

Detection & Grounding

Catch the error, or never make it

Two Strategies

1 · Ground

Before: give the model real sources.

2 · Detect

After: flag a likely hallucination.

Ground first, then detect: best systems do both.

Self-Consistency

Samples agree ⇒ likely solid; samples conflict ⇒ likely invented.

Manakul, Liusie, and Gales, "SelfCheckGPT: Zero-Resource Black-Box Hallucination Detection for Generative Large Language Models", EMNLP 2023 — Fig. 1.

Semantic Entropy

Group the samples by meaning, not wording.

  • "Paris" and "It's Paris" count as one
  • Many distinct meanings ⇒ high entropy
  • High entropy flags hallucination
Kuhn, Gal, and Farquhar, "Semantic Uncertainty", ICLR 2023 — Table 1; Farquhar et al., "Detecting Hallucinations in Large Language Models Using Semantic Entropy", Nature 2024.

The Entropy Picture

Scattered meanings signal an unreliable answer.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation): fetch, then write.

Question
Retrieve documents
Answer from them
Lewis et al., "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks", NeurIPS 2020 — Fig. 1.

Why RAG Helps

The answer is tied to a source the model can quote.

  • Fresh facts past the training cutoff
  • A citation a user can verify
  • Less room to invent

Grounding shrinks the space of fabrications.

RAG Is Not a Cure

Retrieval can fail in its own ways.

  • Wrong documents retrieved
  • Right documents, misread
  • Model overrides the source anyway

Grounding reduces hallucination; it does not erase it.

Teaching "I Don't Know"

Make abstention a rewarded behavior.

  • Reward correct "I don't know"
  • Penalize confident wrong answers more
  • Shift the guessing incentive

A good refusal can beat a bad answer.

A Scoring Rule

Penalize a confident mistake; let "I don't know" score zero.

$$\text{correct: } +1, \quad \text{IDK: } 0, \quad \text{wrong: } -4.$$

Now a wild guess is no longer worth it: guessing pays only above 80% confidence.

Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, and Zhang, "Why Language Models Hallucinate", 2025.

Should a Truthful Model Share Our Errors?

No. Truthful = asserts no falsehood, however popular.

  • Not the human average: imitation is the failure
  • "No comment" is truthful, but uninformative
  • Goal: truthful and informative

Larger GPT-3 models repeated more myths: imitation, not ignorance.

Lin, Hilton, and Evans, "TruthfulQA: Measuring How Models Mimic Human Falsehoods", ACL 2022 — §2.1 and Fig. 2.

Reasoning Models Hallucinate Too

Step-by-step "thinking" did not fix it.

  • o3: hallucinated on 33% of PersonQA people-questions
  • o4-mini: 48%; the older o1: 16%
  • More claims per answer, more chances to invent

More deliberation is not a guarantee of truth.

Open Problems

  • Calibration that survives fine-tuning
  • Honest abstention without over-refusal
  • Guarantees for open-ended generation
  • Detecting faithfulness, not just facts

The reliability frontier is wide open.

Key Takeaways

  • Fluent is not correct; confidence is style
  • Next-token prediction has no truth check
  • Calibration audits a confidence number
  • Conformal sets give a coverage guarantee
  • Grounding and abstention reduce the harm

The goal: a model that knows when not to answer.

?

Knowing when to say "I don't know".
