6. Who Gets the Opportunity?

Where bias enters · ≈0.75 min · elapsed ≈3 min

Message: In lending, a score compared with a cutoff becomes an allocation of opportunity. Its two errors fall on different people: approving someone who does not repay costs the lender, while denying someone who would have repaid costs the applicant a chance at credit.

Reading the slide. Read the diagram left to right: applicant, then the model's score, then the decision (score versus cutoff). The decision branches into “selected: loan approved” (Yonsei blue) and “not selected: denied”, and each branch names its own error and who bears it. The example is illustrative and claims no prevalence of either error.

Script

The two images asked what a task assumes and what a system fills in when the task is silent. Now the other side: who receives an opportunity. Before any study, why should we care? Take an illustrative lending decision. An applicant asks for a loan; a model produces a score; the lender compares the score with a cutoff. The score has become an allocation: some people are selected and get a loan, others are denied.

Notice the two ways this can go wrong. Approve someone who then does not repay, and the lender loses money. Deny someone who would have repaid, and the applicant loses a chance at credit and at building a repayment record. These are different errors, borne by different people. A fairness question always starts with which harm we are measuring and for whom.

The figure

Diagram: applicant → score from a model → decision (score vs cutoff), branching to "selected: loan approved" (Yonsei blue) and "not selected: denied". Each branch names its error: did not repay (lender's loss) or would have repaid (applicant's lost chance).

Assumptions

Illustrative example of the lending setting used throughout this lecture; it is not a measured case and claims no prevalence of either error.

8. Past Records Can Shape Decisions

Where bias enters · ≈0.75 min · elapsed ≈5.75 min

Message: A model learns from records of past decisions, so a disparity in who was approved can travel through the labels into new decisions. Whether a given model reproduces, shrinks or amplifies it must be measured, not assumed, and accuracy is judged against the same past.

Reading the slide. Read the four boxes left to right: past records (who was approved), training data (labels from those records), the model (learns the pattern), new decisions (can repeat it). The dashed bracket over the chain marks a possible pathway for a past disparity, not a measured effect in any system.

Script

Why did that happen? A model learns patterns from its training data, and that data records past decisions, past outcomes and past records: for a lender, who was approved. The labels come from those records, the model learns the pattern, and new decisions can repeat it.

So a learned pattern is not automatically the right target. If the past carried a disparity, it can travel along this pathway. Whether a particular model reproduces, shrinks or amplifies a disparity has to be measured; it is not automatic. That is also why an accurate model can still be unfair: accuracy is measured against the same past.

The figure

Diagram: past records (who was approved) → training data (labels from records) → model (learns the pattern) → new decisions (can repeat it), under a dashed bracket marking a possible pathway for a past disparity.

Assumptions

An intuition, not a claim about any specific system.

11. A Proxy Can Answer the Wrong Question

Where bias enters · ≈0.75 min · elapsed ≈8.25 min

Message: When the outcome we care about, repayment, is never observed for denied applicants, a convenient label such as “was approved before” answers a different question. A denied would-be repayer is then recorded as a negative, so choosing the target is itself a fairness decision.

Reading the slide. Two panels separated by “≠”: left, what we want (the actual outcome, unseen for anyone denied); right, what we record (Y = approved before, seen for everyone). Below, one illustrative denied applicant: the dashed green mark is the unobserved actual outcome (would have repaid), the hollow circle the recorded Y = 0. No specific system is claimed.

Script

Often we don't measure what we care about. A lender wants to know whether someone will repay: that is the actual outcome. But for people who were never approved, repayment is never observed. So suppose the recorded label is Y = "was approved before". It is available for everyone, but it answers a different question.

Take one past applicant who was denied but would have repaid. The actual outcome is positive; the recorded label is Y = 0. The label calls a would-be repayer a negative, and yesterday's decisions become today's ground truth. So choosing the target variable is itself a fairness decision.

The figure

Two panels separated by ≠: what we want (actual outcome: will this person repay?, unseen for anyone denied) vs what we record (the label Y = approved before, seen for everyone). Below, one illustrative denied applicant: actual outcome would have repaid (dashed green, unobserved); recorded Y = 0 (hollow circle = recorded negative).

Assumptions

Illustrative example, not a specific system. A past-approval label does not by itself establish prejudice in any real dataset; whether it carries one depends on how past approvals were made. Target design is a choice to examine, not a default.

12. Missing Outcomes Can Reinforce Exclusion

Where bias enters · ≈0.75 min · elapsed ≈9 min

Message: If denied applicants never build a repayment record and the next model reads that missing record as risk, denials can produce the data that later justifies them. The loop depends on that assumption: missing evidence is not evidence of risk.

Reading the slide. Follow the cycle clockwise: “group A denied more often” → “no loan, no record” → “missing read as risky” → “lower scores for A in the next model” → back to denial. The dashed box marks the one step that is an assumption; without it the loop does not reinforce. The loop is illustrative, not a claim about a specific system.

Script

A loop. Suppose a model denies loans to group A more often than group B. Denied applicants never get a loan, so they never build a repayment record. Now add one assumption: the next model reads that missing record as risk. Then group A gets lower scores, and is denied again. The loop closes.

The assumption is doing the work. Missing evidence is not evidence of risk. The point is that a model's decisions can create the data used to judge it.

The figure

Closed clockwise cycle: group A denied more often → no loan, no record (repayment never observed) → missing read as risky (dashed box, labelled as an assumption) → lower scores for A in the next model → back to denial.

Assumptions

Illustrative lending loop; no specific system is claimed. The loop requires the learner or lender to interpret missing evidence unfavorably; without that assumption, missing outcomes alone do not make the loop reinforce.

13. Dropping a Column Can Leave Information

Where bias enters · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈10 min

Message: Deleting the sensitive column need not remove a disparity, because correlated features such as ZIP area can still carry group information. It also removes the ability to measure the gap, and to use the mitigation methods that need the group label.

Reading the slide. The illustrative table lists four applicants with group, years employed and ZIP area. The group column is struck out as deleted; the ZIP area column is outlined, with an arrow to “ZIP area still separates A from B” and the stated assumption that ZIP correlates with group. Whether a real feature does this has to be checked in the data at hand.

Script

The obvious fix is to delete the sensitive column. It need not remove disparities. In this illustrative table, the group column is deleted, but the ZIP area column still separates A from B, because here we assume ZIP area correlates with group. In real data, ZIP code, name and school can each be correlated with race, so the remaining features can still carry group information, and a model can use it. Whether a given feature does so has to be checked in the data at hand.

Worse, once the column is gone, you can no longer measure the gap. Fairness needs more than blindness.

20. Equal Selection Can Hide Unequal Outcomes

Measuring fairness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈16 min

Message: Demographic parity asks for the same selection rate in every group and never looks at the outcome $Y$. Both groups select 4 of 10, yet 3 of A's selected repaid against 1 of B's; whether that is acceptable depends on whether $Y$ can be trusted.

Reading the slide. Both groups are shaded whole, the denominator for the selection rate: 4/10 in each, so parity holds. Look inside the boxes: the selected who repaid are 3/4 in A and 1/4 in B. Parity does not register that difference.

Script

The first criterion: demographic parity. The rule: the same selection rate in every group. The formula is in the technical supplement.

It looks only at who is selected, never at $Y$. In the picture, both groups select 4 of 10, so parity holds. But in group A, 3 of the 4 selected repaid; in group B, only 1 of 4. Whether that is a feature or a flaw depends on whether you trust $Y$.

If the labels are biased, as in Section 01, ignoring them can be the point. If they are trustworthy, parity can force selecting less-qualified people in one group.

Illustrative counts.

21. Equal Opportunity Leaves One Error Unchecked

Measuring fairness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈17.5 min

Message: Equal opportunity asks for the same true-positive rate in every group, so repayers have the same chance of selection: 3 of 6 in A, 1 of 2 in B. It leaves the false-positive rate free, here 1/4 in A against 3/8 in B.

Reading the slide. The repaid row of each group is shaded as the TPR denominator; half of each row is selected, so the rule holds. The hollow row is not checked by this rule: compare its boxed counts, 1 of 4 in A and 3 of 8 in B.

Script

Equal opportunity conditions on the recorded outcome, but only on one side. The rule: the same TPR in every group. Qualified people, here those who repaid, have the same chance of being selected wherever they come from.

In our lending groups it holds: half the repayers are selected in A (3 of 6) and in B (1 of 2). But look at the hollow row. Group A selects 1 of 4 non-repayers; group B selects 3 of 8. Equal TPR says nothing about FPR.

It is the criterion to use when what matters is that qualified people in every group get the same chance.

27. Calibration Does Not Fix Cutoff Precision

Measuring fairness · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈25.5 min

Message: Calibration does not fix precision at a cutoff. Selecting everyone above 0.5 picks 30 of 40 in each calibrated group, yet PPV is 24/30 = 0.8 in A and 21/30 = 0.7 in B, because A's selected are mostly 0.9s and B's mostly 0.6s.

Reading the slide. The S19a buckets reappear with a cutoff line at 0.5. The 0.9 and 0.6 buckets are boxed as selected and shaded as the PPV denominator; count the filled circles inside to get 24/30 and 21/30. Calibration speaks about every score, predictive parity about one cutoff. With only two score values, the same top score selected in both groups and someone at that score in each, the two would coincide; three values break that.

Script

Now make a decision: select everyone scored above 0.5. Both groups select 30 of 40. Predictive parity asks for equal PPV, the share of the selected who repaid. In group A, 24 of 30, or 0.8. In group B, 21 of 30, or 0.7.

Why? PPV averages the scores above the cutoff, and A's selected are mostly 0.9s while B's are mostly 0.6s. Two groups can both be calibrated and still differ in PPV. These sound alike and are not: calibration is about every score, predictive parity about one cutoff.

With only two score values, calibration does give predictive parity when the cutoff selects the same top score in both groups and both groups have someone at that score. Three values are enough to break it.

Going further

Two notions not in the picture. Both ask for something a dataset alone does not provide.

  • Individual fairness (Dwork et al. 2012, Def. 2.1): similar individuals should be treated similarly. Given a task-specific metric $d$ on individuals and a distance $D$ between output distributions, a randomized classifier $M$ is $(D, d)$-Lipschitz if $D(Mx, My) \le d(x, y)$ for all $x, y$. The hard part is $d$: who decides which people are similar?
  • Counterfactual fairness (Kusner et al. 2017, Def. 5): in a causal model, the prediction's distribution for a person should not change if their attribute $A$ had been different, holding the person's background variables fixed. It needs a causal model of how $A$ affects the features.

Formal statements: lec12tech.html, slides 7–8.

Script

The two error-rate rules, same axis. Equal opportunity asks only for equal true-positive rates, and it still gives each group a single cutoff, one marker per group.

50. Reweighing Changes Influence, Not Labels

Mitigation · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈47.5 min

Message: Reweighing (Kamiran and Calders) gives each (group, label) cell the weight expected share ÷ observed share, where “expected” assumes group and label are independent. The same people count more or less; nobody is added, no label changes, and an empty cell cannot be reweighted.

Reading the slide. One cell, group A's recorded positives: 20 of 100 observed (20%), against 30% expected if group and label were independent ($0.5 \times 0.6$). The weight is 30% ÷ 20% = 1.5, so the same twenty filled dots are redrawn with 1.5 times the area and together count as 30.

Script

Reweighing, from Kamiran and Calders. Give each training example a weight so that group and label look independent. The rule: a (group, label) cell's weight is the share you'd expect if group and label were independent, divided by the share you actually observe.

Take one cell: positive examples in group A. Group A is half the data and 60% of all labels are positive, so independence predicts $0.5 \times 0.6 = 0.3$, a 30% share. We observe 20 of 100, a 20% share. The weight is 30% ÷ 20% = 1.5. On the slide the same 20 dots get bigger: these are the same 20 people, each now counting 1.5 times, so together they count as 30. Nobody new appears and nobody's label changes.

A cell below its independence-expected share gets a weight above 1; a cell above it gets a weight below 1. Weighting needs full support: if a (group, label) combination that independence requires has no examples, no weight can create them, and the weighted data stay dependent.

56. A Random Mixture Can Balance Expected Selection

Mitigation · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈54.5 min

Message: A group-blind coin flip between two unbalanced classifiers can equalize expected selection: h1 selects 80% of A and 40% of B, h2 selects 20% and 60%, and the half-and-half mix selects 50% of each. It mixes decisions, not scores, and finite counts can differ.

Reading the slide. Read left to right: a fair coin that ignores the group points, with weight ½ each, to two classifier cards listing their selection rates for A and B. Both feed the “expected selection” box at 50% / 50%; the formula line beneath shows the arithmetic. The classifiers are hypothetical, not taken from Agarwal et al.

Script

Why would the output be a random mix? A made-up example. Classifier h1 selects 80% of group A and 40% of group B. Classifier h2 selects 20% of A and 60% of B. Neither is balanced. Now, for each decision, flip a fair coin that ignores the group, and use h1 on heads and h2 on tails. In expectation group A is selected at ½·80% + ½·20% = 50%, and group B at ½·40% + ½·60% = 50%.

Three cautions. This mixes decisions; it is not averaging the two scores and re-thresholding. The coin does not look at the group; it is not a switch that uses h1 for one group and h2 for the other. And the 50% is an expectation: in a finite sample the observed counts can differ a little.

Script

Script

Before reading the results, the scale. Zero means no lean; positive means answers lean toward the stereotype; negative means against it; the ends are ±100.

The two context types are scored differently. In disambiguated contexts the score looks at the answers other than "unknown" and asks what share follow the stereotype. Disambiguated: $s_{\mathrm{DIS}} = 2\,(n_{\text{biased}} / n_{\text{non-unknown}}) - 1$. Ambiguous: $s_{\mathrm{AMB}} = (1 - \text{accuracy})\, s_{\mathrm{DIS}}$. Both reported ×100, range −100 to 100.

Script

One dated example of how law turns fairness into an audit. New York City's rules for automated employment decision tools took effect on July 5, 2023. An employer may not use such a tool if more than a year has passed since its last bias audit by an independent auditor. The audit computes prescribed metrics: the selection rate for each sex and race/ethnicity category, including intersectional categories, and the impact ratio, which is a category's selection rate divided by the rate of the most selected category. The rules' own example: 170 of 390 Black applicants selected (44%) against 412 of 797 White applicants (52%, the most selected group) gives an impact ratio of about 0.84; the ratio uses the unrounded rates, since 44 ÷ 52 alone would round to 0.85. A summary of the results, with the data source and the audit date, must be published.

What the audit does not do is say which ratio, or which fairness goal, is good enough. The rate and the denominator are fixed; the verdict is not.
