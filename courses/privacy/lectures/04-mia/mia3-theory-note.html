What moved onto the slides

An earlier version of this note carried a "full derivation" of the Yeom bound built on two applications of Markov's inequality. That argument was not the argument in the paper, and it was not valid: it bounded the false-positive rate from the wrong side and then absorbed $R_{\text{pop}}$ into a constant to reach the folklore statement $\text{Adv} \leq \Delta$.

Yeom et al. prove something different, and stronger. For the bounded-loss adversary $\mathcal{A}_1$ that answers "non-member" with probability $\ell(A_S,z)/B$, the membership advantage is exactly $R_{\text{gen}}(A)/B = \Delta/B$. That is an identity for one specific adversary, hence a lower bound on what the best adversary achieves. It is not an upper bound on any attack. The full statement, the three-step proof, the corollary $\Delta/B \leq \sup_{\mathcal{A}}\text{Adv} = \text{TV}(P_1,P_0)$ with its proof, and two counterexamples to the folklore reading are all on the slides (Section 01).

Speaker note: the folklore misstatement is common enough in secondary sources that it is worth naming out loud. The one-line diagnostic is that $\Delta$ is a difference of means while the advantage is a distance between laws; no inequality runs from the first to the second.

Tightness and what the bounds do not say

Per-record versus population-average

Both Yeom's $\Delta/B$ and the average advantage itself are population-average quantities. A model with $\Delta \approx 0$ can still expose specific records: outliers with unusually low loss relative to the population remain identifiable while the average signal is nil. Counterexample A on the slides is the extreme version of this. The per-record framing, and the low-false-positive-rate regime it demands, is the subject of the later deck on per-record attacks.

How loose is $\Delta/B \leq \text{TV}$?

No theorem in this lecture quantifies the slack. The corollary follows from enlarging the class of test functions from the single function $1-\ell/B$ to all functions into $[0,1]$; how much is lost depends entirely on how far the optimal test is from a scaled loss. Both endpoints are attainable: Counterexample A on the slides has $\Delta = 0$ with $\text{TV} = 1$ (maximal slack), and the two-point example accompanying the corollary has $\Delta/B = \text{TV} = 1/2$ (no slack at all). Anything in between is possible. Measuring the slack for real models is an open empirical question, not a corollary.

Defences that shrink $\Delta$

Regularisation, dropout and early stopping shrink the generalisation gap, hence the guaranteed floor. No theorem states that they shrink $\text{TV}(P_1,P_0)$. Reporting a small train-test gap is therefore evidence about one moment, not a privacy guarantee. Only the algorithmic bound, $\text{Adv}^{\mathrm{M}} \leq e^{\varepsilon}-1$ for an $\varepsilon$-differentially private learner, is a genuine ceiling.

Sablayrolles: what the assumptions are doing

Gibbs posterior versus real SGD

Theorems 5 and 6 on the slides (paper Theorems 1 and 2) assume the weights are drawn from the tempered posterior $P(\theta \mid z_{1:n}, m_{1:n}) \propto \exp(-\frac{1}{T}\sum_i m_i \ell(\theta,z_i))$. The paper's justification is that this is the stationary distribution one would associate with noisy gradient descent, with $T=1$ the Bayesian posterior, $T \to 0$ the MAP estimate, and small $T$ a proxy for averaged SGD. Real SGD is not sampled from this posterior: it has path dependence, finite-time bias, and depends on initialisation and data order. The cancellation of $p_{\mathcal{T}}(\theta)$ in Step 2 of the proof of Theorem 6 is exactly where that assumption is spent, and it is the step that fails first when the assumption is wrong.

The additive-loss assumption

The proof needs the training objective to split as a sum over points so that the factor involving $z_1$ separates from the rest. Batch normalisation, contrastive losses, and any objective coupling examples within a batch break the split. The theorem is silent about those cases rather than false: the derivation simply does not go through.

One snapshot only

The conditioning event in Definition 1 is "the adversary sees $\theta$". Section 04's federated result conditions on a different, richer event, which is why "the loss is sufficient" and "trajectories beat snapshots" are not in conflict. Stated in one line: sufficiency is a claim about a fixed observation model, not about all observation models.

MATT and identifiability

The Taylor argument behind MATT assumes a unique minimiser and a well-conditioned Hessian at it, so that the two posteriors can be Laplace-approximated around $\theta_0^*$ and $\theta_1^*$ and compared. Neural networks have permutation and rescaling symmetries, so $\theta_0^*$ is not identified and the influence-function step has no fixed reference point. The paper is explicit that MATT is a convex-model tool; MALT and MAST are the ones that transfer.

Salem: theory versus measurement

The slides formalise the three relaxations as perturbations of the surrogate law and bound their cost by the triangle inequality for total variation, at most $2\,\text{TV}$ per step. That bound is one-directional and says nothing about the size of any individual $\text{TV}$ term. The paper's headline finding — that dropping shadow models, matched architectures and matched data barely moves precision and recall — is an empirical statement about the datasets and models tested, not a consequence of the bound. Adversary 2 on Purchase-10 to News even improves on the matched attack, which is consistent with a one-sided bound and inconsistent with reading it as an equality.

Numbers quoted on the slides come from Salem et al., NDSS 2019: one shadow model matching ten on CIFAR-100 (0.95 / 0.95 versus 0.95 / 0.94), the hyperparameter and algorithm mismatches of Table II, the transfer results for Adversary 2, the max-posterior threshold for Adversary 3, dropout at rate one half reducing CIFAR-100 to 0.61 / 0.60, and the Google Cloud Prediction API targets.

White-box features and their dimensions

The slides work with one layer's gradient to keep the algebra explicit. The full feature set Nasr et al. feed to their attack network, with dimensions:

FeatureNotationDimension
Loss$\ell(f_\theta, z)$Scalar
Per-layer gradients$\nabla_{W_l} \ell$$\sum_l |W_l|$
Intermediate activations$h_l(x)$$\sum_l d_l$
Loss gradient w.r.t. input$\nabla_x \ell$$d_{\text{input}}$

Gradient features scale with the total parameter count, which for large models is $10^8$ to $10^9$. Nasr et al. therefore apply one-dimensional convolutional encoders per layer to compress gradients before concatenating them. The rank-one structure proved on the slides explains why so little is gained: for the last layer the whole matrix is determined by the error vector and the activation, and the error vector is a monotone function of the loss for a confident correct prediction.

Measured gains, from the paper: black box 74.2 percent, white-box layer outputs 74.6 percent, white-box gradients 75.1 percent on CIFAR-100 with Alexnet (Table VIII). Federated attacks reach 85.1 to 92.1 percent (Table X), which is the observation model changing, not the statistic improving.

Speaker asides

Where to slow down. Step 2 of the proof of Theorem 6 (the cancellation of $p_{\mathcal{T}}$) and the two counterexamples in Section 01 carry the lecture. Everything else can be taken at pace.

A question to expect. "If the loss is sufficient, why did Nasr get more?" The answer is on the slides as the data-processing proposition: the federated attacker is not observing $\theta$, so Theorem 6 does not apply to it. Worth rehearsing.

Notation warning. The slides flip Yeom's membership bit so that $b=1$ means member, matching the convention used throughout the series. The paper uses $b=0$ for member. Say so if anyone reads the paper alongside.
