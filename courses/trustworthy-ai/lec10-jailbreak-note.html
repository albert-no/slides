3. Section 01 — What Safety Training Adds

What safety training adds · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈1.5 min

A chat model learns to refuse some requests. What would it mean to get around that, and how would we count whether we succeeded? Those are the two questions of this section.

Key takeaway: Define the attack and the scoreboard before looking at attacks.

4. Chat Assistants Help, but Decline Some Requests

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈2.5 min

Start with what you see every day. Ask a chat assistant to debug your Python code, explain how vaccines work, or plan a trip to Busan: it answers. Ask it for password-stealing malware or nerve-agent synthesis steps: it declines.

Declining is a safeguard added by training, on top of a capable model. The capability to answer is still there underneath; training taught the model when not to use it.

That gives today's topic. A jailbreak is an input that gets the answer anyway.

Key takeaway: Assistants decline some requests because of training; a jailbreak gets the answer anyway.

5. Why Getting Around Refusal Matters

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈3.5 min

Why should we care whether refusal can be bypassed? Three reasons.

Uplift: some answers give real help toward serious harm, such as weapons, malware or fraud at scale. Scale: a trick shared online works for every user of the model, and can keep working for months. Agency: models now act for the user — they run code, browse and send messages — so a bypass can become an action, not only a text.

So today's question: how robust is refusal, and how do we measure that honestly?

Key takeaway: A bypass can turn a refusal into real-world help or a real action.

The figure

Three cards drawn for this lecture.

Assumptions

Uplift assumes the jailbroken answer adds something the attacker could not easily find elsewhere; slide 63 shows a jailbroken reply with no useful content, so uplift has to be measured, not assumed.

Going further

Agency connects to prompt injection, where the hijacking text comes from a document or web page the agent reads rather than from the user.

6. Jailbreak Prompts Are Shared and Last for Months

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈4.5 min

Is this only a lab curiosity? Shen and colleagues collected prompts from Reddit, Discord, prompt-sharing websites and open datasets, December 2022 to December 2023.

Out of 15,140 prompts, 1,405 were jailbreak prompts, shared across 131 communities. The five most effective reached a 0.95 attack success rate on GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, and the oldest of those five had stayed online for more than 240 days.

These are communities, not lone attackers: 28 accounts kept refining their prompts for over 100 days. Once posted, one discovery works for anyone who copies it.

Key takeaway: Jailbreak prompts are shared, refined and reused for months.

8. A Jailbreak Bypasses Safeguards Through Inputs

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈7 min

Our working definition: a jailbreak is an input that gets a safety-trained model to produce what its safeguards are meant to block.

Two words do the work. "Input": the attacker acts through what the model reads, not through its weights. "Safeguards": we are attacking a behavior someone deliberately trained or attached.

The table separates neighbours. Prompt injection, where text from a third party the app reads hijacks the model, is a different threat and is covered in the prompt-injection lecture. Data poisoning, which corrupts training data, was Lecture 9. Removing safety by fine-tuning the weights appears today only as a labelled contrast, because it is not a prompt attack.

Key takeaway: A jailbreak acts through inputs; weight-level safety removal is a different threat model.

Assumptions

The deployer, not the user, decides what is blocked. The definition says nothing about whether the blocked content is actually dangerous; that judgment is part of evaluation (Section 05).

Going further

Usage varies in the literature: some papers call any successful harmful-output attack a "jailbreak", including fine-tuning. We keep the narrower, input-only meaning so that results compare within one threat model.

9. Safety Training Teaches the Model When to Refuse

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈8 min

We have talked about alignment; now name the part this lecture is about. A pretrained model only predicts text. Alignment trains it toward what people want; the usual goals are helpful, honest and harmless, and the slide shows the two that matter today.

Helpful: do what the user asks. Harmless: decline requests that would cause harm. Training for the harmless part is what we call safety training, and refusal is its visible behavior. When a jailbreak works, this is the behavior it gets around.

How is it taught? In RLHF, human raters compare answers; in Constitutional AI, an AI model judges answers against a written list of principles. Either way the model learns from examples, and nothing guarantees it generalizes beyond them. The next slide shows one concrete recipe.

Key takeaway: Refusal is the visible behavior of safety training, and it is learned from examples.

10. Example: How Llama 2 Chat Was Taught to Refuse

What safety training adds · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈9.5 min

One concrete recipe, from Meta's Llama 2 Chat paper. Safety was added in three steps, after the model had learned to follow instructions.

First, safety fine-tuning: annotators wrote adversarial prompts with safe answers, and the model imitated them. Second, safety RLHF: a separate safety reward model scored the model's replies to adversarial prompts, and training pushed toward higher scores. Third, context distillation: they put a safety preprompt such as "You are a safe and responsible assistant" in front of the prompt, then fine-tuned the model on the safer answers without it.

The prompts covered risk categories such as illicit and criminal activities, hateful and harmful activities including self-harm, and unqualified advice. All three steps learn from examples. Inputs unlike those examples are where jailbreaks look.

Key takeaway: Llama 2 Chat learned refusal from examples, in three steps.

The figure

Three steps drawn for this lecture from Section 4.2 of Touvron et al.

Setup, model, date

Touvron et al. (Meta), arXiv July 2023, Section 4.2: safety supervised fine-tuning, safety RLHF, and safety context distillation (Section 4.2.4).

What this does not establish

Other vendors' pipelines differ and are mostly unpublished. The recipe tells what the model saw in training, not how far refusal generalizes beyond it.

Primary sources

  • Touvron et al., "Llama 2: Open Foundation and Fine-Tuned Chat Models" (arXiv 2023) — safety fine-tuning, safety RLHF and safety context distillation (§4.2).

11. Two Ways to Fail: Refusing Too Little or Too Much

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈10.5 min

A two-by-two. Rows: is the request benign or harmful? Columns: does the model answer or refuse? Answering benign requests is helpful. Refusing harmful ones is a safe refusal.

The two off-diagonal cells are both failures. Answering a harmful request is under-refusal: that is the jailbreak's target. Refusing a benign request is over-refusal: the cost of caution.

Keep both cells in view all lecture. A defense that only shrinks the red cell may quietly grow the grey one.

Key takeaway: Safety has two error types; a defense must report both.

12. What the Attacker Can Touch Sets the Threat

What safety training adds · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈11.5 min

Attacks differ in access, and access changes what is possible. The lowest rung is chat only: type prompts, read replies. Hand-written jailbreaks, PAIR, many-shot and Best-of-N all live here.

One rung up, the attacker controls part of the response or has gradients from an open model: prefilling the start of the reply, or GCG suffix search.

The top rung is weights: fine-tuning or editing the model. That is not a prompt attack, and we will label it as a contrast when it appears.

The rule: compare attacks on the same rung. An attack with gradients beating an attack without them tells you little.

Key takeaway: Compare attacks only within the same access level.

The figure

An access ladder drawn for this lecture, with today's attacks placed on their rungs.

Going further

Prefilling sits on the middle rung because some commercial APIs let the user write the start of the assistant turn; with open weights it is always possible.

13. Every Attack Success Rate Needs Four Labels

What safety training adds · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈13 min

ASR, attack success rate, is the share of requests counted as successful. Every ASR needs four labels before it means anything.

The denominator: which requests, and how many? The budget: one try per request, or the best of N tries? The model: name and dated version. The judge: who decided the reply was harmful, and how often is that judge wrong?

Two ASRs that differ on any of these labels are not directly comparable. We will check all four on every number today.

Key takeaway: An ASR without denominator, budget, model and judge is not a result.

Going further

The budget label is the one most often omitted. "At least one success in N attempts" grows quickly with N even when the per-attempt rate is tiny; slide 46 makes this concrete, and lec10tech gives the formula.

14. Section 02 — Why Refusal Is Fragile

Why refusal is fragile · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈13.5 min

Safety training works on its own test prompts. Why does a reworded request still get through? This section collects explanations, each tested in a particular setting.

Key takeaway: Several explanations, each with its own evidence and access level.

15. Two Hypotheses for Why Safety Training Fails

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈14.5 min

Wei and colleagues proposed two failure modes. Competing objectives: safety competes with other training goals, and the prompt forces a choice between them. Mismatched generalization: the model's capabilities reach inputs that safety training never saw, such as Base64, so the prompt leaves safe ground.

Then they used both hypotheses as design tools: each suggested new jailbreaks, which they built and tested.

Note the status of these ideas. They were proposed from tests on GPT-4 and Claude v1.3 in 2023. They organise the evidence well, but they are explanations, not proofs.

Key takeaway: Two failure modes: objectives that conflict, and safety that does not cover all skills.

Setup, model, date

Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, NeurIPS 2023, Section 3.

What this does not establish

They do not show a mechanism inside the model; they are behavioral hypotheses that predict which attacks should work. Slides 20–27 look for mechanisms in open models.

Primary sources

  • Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, "Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?" (NeurIPS 2023) — competing objectives and mismatched generalization (§3); Fig. 1; Table 1 on 32 curated prompts.

16. Competing Objectives: Make Refusing Costly

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈15.5 min

Wei et al. describe three training goals. Pretraining: continue text the likely way. Instruction tuning: do what the user asks. Safety training: decline harmful requests. Usually they agree.

A competing-objectives attack builds a prompt where refusing would break the first two. For example, demand a set opening line. Once that line is written, the likely continuation is the answer, and refusing would also disobey the instruction. Safety gives way.

The next slide tests this explanation: change the wording that makes refusing costly, and see whether the attack still works.

Key takeaway: Competing-objective attacks make refusing break the model's other training goals.

The figure

Setup, model, date

Wei et al., Section 3.1: state-of-the-art LLMs are "trained for language modeling […], instruction following […], and safety […]. This training can be exploited by crafting prompts that force a choice between either a restricted behavior or a response that is heavily penalized by the pretraining and instruction following objectives."

17. Change the Key Wording and the Attack Stops Working

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈16.5 min

Is the competing-objectives explanation right? Wei et al. tested it with ablations on GPT-4: keep the attack, but change the part that is supposed to make refusing costly.

Prefix injection demands that the reply open with a carefree, eager line. As written, 22% of replies were harmful. Replace the demanded line with a plain "Hello!" and it fell to 6%. Refusal suppression gives rules that forbid apologies and words like "cannot": 25%. Invert those rules and it fell to 0%. A plain request: 3%.

So the attack works through the part that makes refusing costly. Shared role-play prompts like "DAN" use the same idea, pairing a persona with a fixed opening. We name the families; working prompts are not shown, here or anywhere in this course.

Key takeaway: Remove the part that makes refusing costly, and the attack stops working.

What this does not establish

18. Mismatched Generalization: Skills Outrun Safety

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈17.5 min

The outer region is everything the model can understand, learned in pretraining. The inner region is what safety training covered: mostly plain English chat.

In between lie inputs the model can still read: Base64 and other encodings, simple ciphers, low-resource languages, unusual output formats. A request there is understood, but the refusal never learned to fire.

The picture is a sketch, not to scale. It predicts something testable: attacks that move into understood-but-uncovered territory should work, and they should work better on more capable models that read more encodings.

Key takeaway: If the model can read it but safety training never saw it, refusal may not fire.

21. Even a Base Model Looks Safe After “I cannot”

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈20.5 min

Here is the test. Take the base model, with no safety training. Force the first words of its reply, let it continue, and count harmful replies with a GPT-4-based judge.

With no forced opening, the base model gave harmful replies to 68.6% of the 330 requests. Force the opening "I cannot": 16.4%. Force "I apologize, but I cannot": 2.1%. The safety-trained chat model, with no forced opening: 0%.

So a model can look almost safe after changing only its first few tokens: a shortcut. That is why attacks on the first few tokens matter.

Key takeaway: Forcing a refusal opening made even the base model look almost safe.

24. The Fix: Train Recovery After a Harmful Start

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈24 min

If safety lives in the first few tokens, the fix is to teach refusal later in the reply. Qi and colleagues built training examples in three parts.

First, a harmful request x. Second, the first k tokens of a harmful answer h; these are context only: the model reads them but is not trained to produce them. Third, a refusal r, such as "I cannot fulfill…", which the model is trained to output. Half the time k is 0, so the plain refusal stays; otherwise k is uniform from 1 to 100.

To stay useful, these are mixed with ordinary Alpaca requests answered by the original model. They started from the aligned Llama-2-7B-Chat; the result is called the Augmented model.

Key takeaway: Train the model to refuse even after a harmful start.

26. One Direction in Activations Switches Refusal

Why refusal is fragile · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈26 min

A second inside look, by Arditi and colleagues. Read the access strip first: the researcher edits activations inside the model. That is white-box, and it tests an explanation; it is not an attack on a chat app.

Step 1: run 128 harmful and 128 harmless instructions through the model and average the activations of each group; the difference of the two averages is a direction r. Step 2: remove r from the activations at every layer and token: harmful requests are no longer refused. Step 3: add r at one layer: now harmless requests are refused.

So in these models one direction switches refusal off and on. And because removing r can be written into the weights, it gives a jailbreak with no gradient training and no harmful answers.

Key takeaway: In tested open models, one activation direction switched refusal off and on.

33. Automated Attacks Are a Search Loop

35. Each Step Tests Hundreds of One-Token Swaps

Searching for jailbreaks · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈35 min

One GCG step has three parts. Use the gradient to list the top 256 promising token swaps at each suffix position. Sample 512 candidates, each with one swap. Run all 512 forward passes and keep the best by exact loss.

Repeat for 500 steps. That is 500 × 512, about 256 thousand candidate forward passes per suffix, and that count leaves out the 500 gradient computations. Hold on to the number, and its unit, for the comparison with PAIR.

Key takeaway: GCG costs on the order of 256K candidate forward passes per suffix, before counting gradients.

38. PAIR Runs Three Models That Pass Only Text

Searching for jailbreaks · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈38 min

PAIR, Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement, replaces gradients with language models. Three models pass only text to each other.

The attacker LLM, Mixtral 8x7B, writes a prompt P. The target model replies R. The judge, Llama Guard, reads P and R and outputs S: 1 if jailbroken, 0 if not. If S is 0, the prompt, the reply and the score join the attacker's history and it tries again; it stops at S = 1.

Budget: 30 parallel streams of at most 3 rounds, so at most 90 target queries per request. And the judge is a separate model reading only the text: no gradients, no logits.

Key takeaway: PAIR is a black-box loop: attacker writes, target replies, judge scores.

41. Gradient Search vs. LLM Search

Searching for jailbreaks · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈41.5 min

Side by side. GCG needs weights and gradients, spends about 256K candidate forward passes per suffix, outputs an unreadable token string, and reaches closed models only by transfer. 43. Test-Time Scaling: Spend More Compute per Question

Scaling the attack budget · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈43 min

Before the next attacks, a general idea from capability research: test-time scaling. Instead of training a bigger model, spend more compute on each question.

Two ways. One: sample many answers and keep the best, which needs a verifier, such as unit tests for code. Two: revise one answer step by step, each draft improving the last. Either way the weights are the same; only the compute per question grows.

Keep this in mind: the same idea applies to safety. An attacker can also spend more compute per request.

Key takeaway: Test-time scaling buys accuracy with compute per question, not bigger weights.

46. Many Tries Turn a Small Rate Into a Large One

An illustration, not from a study. Suppose each try succeeds with probability 1%, independently. The chance of at least one success in N tries is one minus 0.99 to the N.

N = 1: 1%. N = 10: 9.6%. N = 100: 63%. N = 1,000: essentially 100%.

This is why "any of N" and "per try" must never be mixed. Real tries are not independent and do not share one p, so treat this as intuition, not a model.

Key takeaway: "At least once in N tries" grows fast even from a tiny per-try rate.

50. Safety Training Shifted the Curve, Not Its Slope

Scaling the attack budget · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈50.5 min

Read the axes first. The x-axis is the number of shots, on a log scale. The y-axis is the NLL of a harmful reply, also log: higher means the harmful reply is less likely, so higher is safer. Colour shows how far RL training had run. This is one panel of the paper's Fig. 5, the insults evaluation.

Later checkpoints sit higher: with few shots, the harmful reply became less likely. But the fitted lines fall with roughly the same slope. In the tested settings, more shots kept lowering NLL at about the same rate; safety training delayed the attack rather than changing how it scales.

Two cautions. The axis is log shots, so there is no zero-shot point on it; "intercept" here means the height of the line at a few shots. And "same slope" is an observation over 1 to 32 shots, not a guarantee.

53. A Found Jailbreak Often Fails When Resent

Here is the twist. Take each augmented prompt that succeeded and resend it 100 times. How often is it harmful again?

At temperature 1, the share of resends judged harmful was 46% for Claude 3.5 Sonnet, 24% for GPT-4o and 15% for Gemini Pro. At temperature 0 it was about the same, 52%, 28% and 15%, because even then the API replies varied.

56. Cipher Attack: Teach a Code, Then Ask in It

58. Jailbreaks Are Adversarial Inputs Under a Looser Threat Model

Scaling the attack budget · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈59 min

Connect to Lecture 8. An adversarial example changes a classifier's input by a tiny amount within ε, and the label flips; success is easy to check.

A jailbreak changes a text generator's input with no small-perturbation constraint, and blocked content appears; success needs a judge.

Same game: search for inputs that break a learned rule. The looser threat model makes attacks easier and evaluation harder.

Key takeaway: Jailbreaks are adversarial inputs with no small-perturbation constraint and a harder success test.

61. "Success" Depends on Who Judges It

Evaluation and defenses · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈61.5 min

A ladder of judges. A keyword rule: no "I'm sorry" means success. A classifier: is toxic text present? An LLM judge with a rubric. Human experts: is the answer truly useful? Cheap and scalable at the bottom, costly and closer to real harm at the top.

The same response can pass one judge and fail another. And not refusing is not the same as giving harmful help.

Key takeaway: The judge is part of the result.

62. Automated Judges Disagree With Humans in Both Directions

Evaluation and defenses · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈62.5 min

Chao et al. compared judges with human experts on 100 prompt–response pairs. Three experts labelled each pair; they agreed 95% of the time, and their majority is the reference.

A false positive means the judge says jailbroken where the experts say not; a false negative is the reverse. GPT-4 as judge: 88% agreement, 16% false positives, 7% false negatives. The keyword rule from GCG: 80%, 23% false positives. Llama Guard: 76% agreement, but 47% false negatives. A BERT classifier: 66%, missing 74%.

Errors go both ways: some judges inflate ASR, others hide real jailbreaks.

Key takeaway: Each judge has its own false-positive and false-negative profile.

64. Even Response Length Moves the Score

A subtle one. The y-axis here is ASR measured by substring matching: a reply counts as a success if it does not contain refusal phrases such as "I'm sorry". HarmBench varied how many tokens the target model was allowed to generate.

Reading the curves: with more tokens, the measured ASR fell by roughly 15 to 30 percentage points, for example from about 69% to about 39% for PAIR on Llama 2 7B. The paper summarises this as up to 30%.

73. How Circuit Breakers Are Trained

Evaluation and defenses · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈74.5 min

How is that trained? Two losses at once, both on the model's internal states.

On harmful replies, the circuit-breaker set, the loss pushes the states away from the original model's, toward orthogonal, so the reply turns incoherent. On ordinary chats, the retain set, it keeps the states close to the original, so abilities are kept. Only small add-on weights, LoRA, are trained: about 20 minutes on one GPU.

Does it break every harmful state? No. It mainly covers harms close to its training data.

Key takeaway: Two losses: scramble states on harmful data, keep them on ordinary data.

Ask the obvious question: could the last table's input-embedding attack win if it were better optimized? Read the access first. It is white-box: the attacker edits the input embeddings directly, with no limit on size, so this is not something a chat user can type. It is a stress test.

As reported in the circuit-breaker paper: 15.7% on Mistral-7B and 9.6% on Llama-3-8B. Schwinn and Geisler re-ran the same embedding attack with a better optimizer and starting point: 57.2% and 51.7%. Then they generated a reply every five optimization steps, 20 replies per request, and counted success if any one was harmful: 100% on both. That last row is an any-of-20 rate.

So yes: optimization alone passed 50%. A black-box attack also worked: Best-of-N, with up to 10,000 text variants for each of 159 requests, reached 52% on the Llama-3-8B model, again an any-of-N rate.

Lesson: a defense is only as strong as the adaptive attack it survived, and each re-attack number comes with its own access and budget.

Key takeaway: Better-optimized attacks broke the circuit breakers, under stated access and budget.

78. Real Misuse Needs Monitoring, Not Only Blocking

Monitoring with self-assessment · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈79 min

Start with a real case. As reported in late 2025, suspected state-sponsored attackers used Claude Code to attempt intrusions into roughly thirty organizations. Blocking single replies is not enough to find that kind of misuse; someone has to monitor conversations.

The article lists what such a monitor must handle. Context: the same request can be harmless or harmful depending on context, and the evidence can be spread across a long conversation. Intelligence: a monitor only catches what it can follow. Unknown harms: new kinds of misuse that no classifier was trained for. Scale: it must be cheap enough to run on all traffic.

Note the timing: a monitor checks conversations after the reply. Its job is to find what got through.

Key takeaway: A monitor must understand context and new harms, and be cheap enough for all traffic.

79. Existing Monitors: Cheap or Flexible, Not Both

Monitoring with self-assessment · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈80 min

How do existing monitors compare? Two questions: is it cheap to run, and is it flexible, meaning it can take a new policy or catch a new harm without retraining?

A prompted classifier, a separate model that reads the policy, is flexible but costly. A probe, a small classifier on the model's activations, is cheap but has fixed labels set in advance. A trained classifier, such as Constitutional Classifiers at 23.7% extra compute, is costly and fixed. Self-assessment aims at the empty corner: cheap like a probe, flexible like a prompted classifier.

One test of flexibility: list allowed targets in the prompt, such as systems a security team may test. Self-assessment then flagged 0% of conversations on those targets and 100% on the others.

Key takeaway: Self-assessment aims to be as cheap as a probe and as flexible as a prompted classifier.

80. Self-Assessment: One Extra Question per Reply

Monitoring with self-assessment · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈81.5 min

Here is the mechanism. After Claude replies, the system adds one more user turn with an instruction, for example "Please reflect on the safety of this conversation". Claude answers with a short JSON assessment: flagged or not, a category, whether it refused, and a priority from 1 to 10.

It is cheap because the conversation is already processed, in the KV cache: only about 10 extra output tokens, under 2% extra compute. The two added turns are hidden from the user and then dropped.

The score is the probability of "true" in the flagged field; choosing a threshold on it sets the false-positive rate.

Key takeaway: One extra question per reply turns the model into its own monitor, for about 10 tokens.

81. Training Copies a Costly Teacher's Verdicts

Monitoring with self-assessment · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈82.5 min

How does Claude learn to assess? It copies a teacher: a prompted classifier that reads every policy document and reasons before it answers. Accurate, but expensive.

Data: synthetic chats, harmful and hard benign, labelled by the teacher. For each harm type, 1,024 harmful and 1,024 hard-benign scenarios, half in a non-English language. Hard benign means close to the line but allowed.

Two training stages. Supervised fine-tuning copies the teacher's assessment, with the loss on the assessment tokens only. Then RL with six checkable rewards, for example whether the category matches the teacher's. The student ends up matching the teacher with no policy text and no reasoning in its prompt.

Why loss only on the assessment? So the model learns to judge harmful replies, not to write them.

Key takeaway: Self-assessment is distilled from an expensive teacher into a few output tokens.

Setup, model, date

32 themes × 32 scenarios = 1,024 harmful and 1,024 benign per sub-policy, the benign ones "difficult negatives"; half in one of nine non-English languages. The teacher has all policies and a reasoning checklist; the student has neither. Six rewards: the JSON parses, and flagged, category, sub-policy, refused and priority agree with the teacher (priority only needs to be close).

82. It Catches Most of What the Teacher Flags

Does it work? The test: Claude Fable 5 on random real traffic, with the teacher's labels as the reference.

At 1% false positives, self-assessment caught 78% of all conversations the teacher flagged, and 94% of the high-priority ones, priority 7 or more out of 10, for under 2% extra inference compute.

84. As a Real-Time Blocker, Self-Assessment Is Weak

Monitoring with self-assessment · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈85.5 min

Could it block attacks in real time, not just find them later? The red-team test: an attacker model works through 24 offensive cyber tasks with production classifiers off. When a reply is blocked, the attacker rewinds and tries again.

With no safeguards, the attacker succeeded on 19 of 24 tasks, 79%. With self-assessment blocking, 13 of 24, 54%. With the prompted classifier blocking, 6 of 24, 25%. Self-assessment did make attacks costlier: each success took roughly four times as many generated tokens as with no safeguards.

Why weak? Each block shows the attacker what was caught, so it learns to avoid it; offline monitoring gives no such live signal. Run offline, self-assessment flagged 21 of the 24 attack runs. But it is not robust to an attacker who targets it directly.

Key takeaway: Self-assessment is better at finding misuse afterwards than at blocking a live, adaptive attacker.

85. Limits: The Monitor Shares the Model's Blind Spots

Monitoring with self-assessment · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈86.5 min

Three limits. Who judges? The teacher labels both the training data and the tests, so the student inherits the teacher's blind spots, and the tests cannot reveal them.

Unknown harms? The held-out test removed only fraud and cyber, both well-known categories. And judging itself: the monitor is the same model, with the same judgment, as the one that replied. Khullar and colleagues found that models rate an action as less risky when it appears in their own previous turn than when it is shown fresh.

So self-assessment is one layer of many: monitoring finds what blocking missed.

Key takeaway: A self-monitor shares the model's blind spots: one layer among several.
