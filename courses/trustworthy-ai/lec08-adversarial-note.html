1. Adversarial Robustness: When Small Changes Break Models

Hook · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈0.5 min

Welcome to Lecture 8. So far our failures came from the model or its training data. Today, someone else chooses the input, and they are trying to make the model fail.

Key takeaway: Today: what happens when the input is chosen by an adversary.

2. Contents

Hook · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈1.5 min

Five parts. First, a tiny change that flips a confident answer. Second, two perspectives on why this happens, and the threat model we need before any claim makes sense. Third, how attackers search for the worst input: FGSM, then PGD. Fourth, defenses and, more importantly, how to evaluate them honestly: adaptive attacks, benchmarks, and certificates. Fifth, one physical-world case, read carefully, and a checklist you can apply to any robustness claim.

3. 01 — A Tiny Nudge

Hook · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈2 min

One question frames today: if a model is accurate on test data, can we trust it when someone else chooses the input?

Key takeaway: Keep this question in mind all lecture.

4. A Tiny Nudge Flips a Confident Answer

Hook · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈5 min

The most famous picture in this field. On the left, an image GoogLeNet calls "panda" with 57.7% confidence. In the middle, a pattern that looks like coloured static. We add a tiny multiple of it, 0.007, to the panda. On the right is the result. To us it is the same panda. The network now says "gibbon", with 99.3% confidence.

How small is 0.007? The authors say it equals the size of the smallest bit of an 8-bit image encoding after GoogLeNet's conversion to real numbers: about one step of brightness per pixel.

Notice the line above the figure. Every example today comes with a threat model: what the attacker controls, what they know, and what they want. Here: they change pixels by at most one step, they know the model's gradients, and they want any wrong label.

Key takeaway: A change too small to see can flip a confident prediction.

The figure

Fig. 1 of Goodfellow et al.: x (panda, 57.7%) + .007 × sign(∇ₓJ(θ, x, y)) (the noise, itself classified "nematode", 8.2%) = adversarial image ("gibbon", 99.3%).

Setup, model, date

GoogLeNet on ImageNet; ε = .007 in the ℓ∞ sense; white-box attack (FGSM, slide 23). Published 2015.

What this establishes

An adversarial example exists for this image and model, at a change humans do not notice.

What this does not establish

Nothing about how often this happens on natural inputs, or about other models, budgets or physical images. One illustrative example.

Primary sources

  • Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy, "Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples" (ICLR 2015) — the panda (Fig. 1), FGSM, the "too linear" argument (§3) and Figs. 2 and 4.

5. Anatomy of an Adversarial Example

Hook · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈7 min

Let us name the parts. An adversarial example is x′ = x + δ. The change δ is too small to alter the true label, at least for a human, but it is chosen so that the model's answer changes.

The key word is "chosen". Random noise of the same size usually does nothing. The attacker searches for the specific δ that hurts the model most.

And "small" needs a ruler. In a moment we will fix it with a budget ε and a norm, the way we measure size.

Key takeaway: Adversarial example = small, deliberately chosen change that flips the output.

The figure

Schematic drawn for this lecture: input x + change δ (size ≤ ε) = x′; a human still says panda, the model says gibbon.

Assumptions

"Too small to alter the true label" is the working assumption behind small-norm threat models. It is a judgement about humans, not a mathematical fact, and fails for large ε.

6. Not One Unlucky Image

Hook · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈8.5 min

Is the panda a lucky find? This 2014 paper, which named the phenomenon, shows six more. In each row: a correctly classified image on the left, the difference magnified ten times in the middle, and the adversarial image on the right. AlexNet labels every right-hand image "ostrich".

The authors state the examples were chosen strictly at random. Adversarial examples are the rule, not the exception, for standard networks.

Key takeaway: For standard networks, adversarial examples exist for almost every input.

The figure

Fig. 5 of Szegedy et al.: (a) and (b) each show three triples (original, magnified difference ×10, adversarial). All adversarial images are classified "ostrich, Struthio camelus".

Setup, model, date

AlexNet on ImageNet; targeted attack via box-constrained L-BFGS. Published 2014.

What this establishes

Small targeted changes fooled AlexNet on randomly chosen images.

What this does not establish

Not a rate for any specific ε; the paper reports examples, not a robust-accuracy curve.

Primary sources

  • Szegedy et al., "Intriguing Properties of Neural Networks" (ICLR 2014) — the paper that named adversarial examples; Fig. 5 shows randomly chosen AlexNet examples.

7. Who Chooses the Input Changes the Question

Hook · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈10 min

Here is the conceptual shift. Normal test accuracy is an average case: nature draws inputs from the test distribution, and we count how many are right.

Robustness is a worst case: an adversary picks the input, within allowed changes, to make the model fail. That number is called robust accuracy.

A model can be 95% accurate and have near-zero robust accuracy. High accuracy answers the first question, not the second.

Key takeaway: Accuracy is average case; robustness is worst case.

The figure

Two-panel schematic drawn for this lecture: average case (nature draws inputs → accuracy) vs worst case (adversary picks inputs within allowed changes → robust accuracy).

9. Inputs Sit Close to the Boundary

Why + threat model · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈12 min

A picture to hold on to. In two dimensions: blue panda points, red gibbon points, a boundary between them. Around one panda point, a small ball of allowed changes. Part of that ball crosses the boundary. Any point in that part is an adversarial example.

Real images live in far higher dimensions: a 224×224 colour image has about 150,000 numbers. In such spaces, many tiny moves in the right directions add up, so points can be close to a boundary in some direction even when they look far from it.

This is a sketch, not a measurement. The next two slides give two more precise perspectives.

Key takeaway: Adversarial examples: the small ball around an input reaches across the decision boundary.

The figure

Illustrative 2-D sketch drawn for this lecture: two classes, a straight boundary, a dashed ball around one point with an arrow to a point across the boundary.

Assumptions

2-D pictures mislead about high-dimensional geometry; use it only for the vocabulary (ball, boundary, crossing).

10. Perspective 1: Models Are Too Linear

Why + threat model · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈14.5 min

Goodfellow and colleagues argued that neural networks behave too linearly. Take a linear score w·x. Move every pixel by ε in the direction of the sign of its weight. The score moves by ε times the sum of the absolute weights.

That sum grows with the number of pixels. With thousands of pixels, a per-pixel change too small to see can move the score a lot. In their words, linear behavior in high-dimensional spaces is sufficient to cause adversarial examples.

The plot supports it. For one MNIST input to a maxout network, the logits change as nearly straight lines as ε varies along the attack direction. The paper also shows the corresponding inputs beside this plot; we show only the logits.

Key takeaway: Many tiny aligned changes add up in high dimension.

The figure

Fig. 4 of Goodfellow et al.: left, the 10 logits of a maxout network vs ε along the FGSM direction for one MNIST input (correct class 4); the paper calls them "conspicuously piecewise linear". The right panel (the corresponding inputs) is omitted on the slide.

Setup, model, date

Maxout network, MNIST, one input. 2015.

What this establishes

For this model and input, logits respond almost linearly to large moves along the attack direction, consistent with the linearity view.

What this does not establish

That linearity is the sole cause. Later work (e.g. Ilyas et al., next slide) offers other perspectives; neither is settled.

Assumptions

The w·η = ε‖w‖₁ calculation is exact for a linear score; for deep networks it is a local approximation.

Primary sources

  • Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy, "Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples" (ICLR 2015) — the panda (Fig. 1), FGSM, the "too linear" argument (§3) and Figs. 2 and 4.

11. Perspective 2: Invisible Features Still Predict

Why + threat model · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈16 min

A second perspective, from 2019. Ilyas and colleagues propose that adversarial examples exploit non-robust features: patterns in the data that genuinely predict the label but that humans cannot see and that tiny changes can flip.

Panel (a) of their Fig. 1: they split a training set into a "robust" version and a "non-robust" version that looks like noise. A model trained only on the non-robust version still gets good standard accuracy on the real test set.

Key takeaway: Patterns humans cannot see still predict the label.

The figure

Fig. 1(a) of Ilyas et al., a conceptual diagram: a training image split into robust and non-robust features, giving a robust dataset and a non-robust dataset, each with good standard accuracy on the original test set.

Setup, model, date

CIFAR-10 and Restricted ImageNet; ResNet-50 and others. NeurIPS 2019.

What this establishes

In these experiments, features that look like noise carry enough signal to generalize.

What this does not establish

That every adversarial example is a feature in this sense, or that linearity plays no role.

Primary sources

  • Ilyas et al., "Adversarial Examples Are Not Bugs, They Are Features" (NeurIPS 2019) — the non-robust-features perspective and the retraining experiments of §3.

12. "Mislabeled" Adversarial Images Still Teach

Why + threat model · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈17 min

Panel (b), the surprising one. They take images, push each one adversarially towards another class, and relabel it with that class. To humans the dataset looks mislabelled: dogs labelled "cat". Yet a model trained on it does well on the original, correctly labelled test set. The adversarial changes carried real, predictive signal.

Key takeaway: Adversarial changes carry real, learnable signal.

The figure

Fig. 1(b) of Ilyas et al.: adversarial relabelling ("appears mislabeled to humans") still gives good accuracy on the original test set.

Setup, model, date

Ilyas et al. §3.2. NeurIPS 2019.

What this does not establish

That models trained this way are robust; the point is that the signal generalizes.

Primary sources

  • Ilyas et al., "Adversarial Examples Are Not Bugs, They Are Features" (NeurIPS 2019) — the non-robust-features perspective and the retraining experiments of §3.

13. Two Perspectives, Not One Cause

Why + threat model · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈18.5 min

Side by side. The linearity view says small moves add up; it explains well why a single gradient step like FGSM works. The features view says hidden patterns predict; it explains well why attacks transfer between models that learned the same patterns.

Both have evidence. Neither is accepted as the single cause, and they are compatible. Treat them as lenses, not verdicts.

Key takeaway: Two useful lenses; the cause is not settled.

14. Every Claim Needs a Threat Model

Why + threat model · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈20.5 min

Now the most important habit of this lecture. Before believing any statement like "this model is robust", ask three things.

What does the attacker control? Which inputs, how much change: the norm, the budget ε, digital or physical. What does the attacker know? Full weights and gradients, only scores or labels, or nothing. What does the attacker want? Any wrong label, or a specific one.

"Robust" with no threat model is not yet a claim. It is a sentence missing its subject.

Key takeaway: Controls, knows, wants: specify all three.

The figure

Three-box schematic drawn for this lecture: controls (norm, ε, digital/physical), knows (weights, scores, labels, nothing), wants (any wrong label, chosen label).

Primary sources

  • Carlini et al., "On Evaluating Adversarial Robustness" (arXiv 2019) — a practical checklist for evaluating a defense.

15. The Budget ε Bounds the Change

Why + threat model · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈22 min

Control first. The allowed changes form a ball of radius ε around the input: every x + δ with the size of δ at most ε.

Two common settings. On CIFAR-10 with the ℓ∞ norm, ε = 8/255: each pixel may change by about 3% of its range. On MNIST, ε = 0.3: each pixel by 30%, which is large but MNIST digits are nearly black-and-white.

These are conventions that let papers compare numbers, not laws. The right ε depends on what changes would really be imperceptible, or harmless, in your application.

Key takeaway: ε is a convention for comparison, not a law of perception.

Setup, model, date

Budgets as used in Madry et al. (Tables 1–2) and the RobustBench CIFAR-10 ℓ∞ leaderboard.

Primary sources

  • Madry et al., "Towards Deep Learning Models Resistant to Adversarial Attacks" (ICLR 2018) — the saddle-point view, PGD, adversarial training; Table 2 for CIFAR-10 numbers; Appendix on ℓ2 evaluation.
  • RobustBench leaderboard (CIFAR-10, ℓ∞ = 8/255) — live leaderboard; the lecture uses a snapshot fetched 10 Oct 2026, 16:47 KST.

16. ℓ∞ vs ℓ2: Two Shapes of "Small"

Why + threat model · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈24 min

How do we measure the size of δ? Two common rulers. ℓ∞ is the largest change to any single pixel. Its ball is a box: every pixel may move up to ε, so the attack tends to look like faint noise everywhere.

ℓ2 is the root-sum-square of the change: the square root of the sum of squared pixel changes. Its ball is round. The attacker can concentrate the change: a few pixels moved a lot, or many moved a little.

The pictures on the slide are examples of each shape, not definitions. To make the scale concrete, Cohen and colleagues note that with pixels scaled to [0, 1], an ℓ2 radius of 1.0 on ImageNet could change, in 0–255 units, one pixel by 255, ten pixels by 80, 100 pixels by 25, or 1000 pixels by 8. Same "size", very different-looking changes. A model robust under one ruler need not be robust under the other.

Key takeaway: The norm defines what "small" means; claims do not transfer between norms.

The figure

Schematic drawn for this lecture: an ℓ∞ box vs an ℓ2 ball around a point.

Setup, model, date

Scale example: Cohen et al., Table 1 caption (ImageNet; radius measured with pixels divided by 255, changes quoted in 0–255 units).

Primary sources

  • Cohen, Rosenfeld & Kolter, "Certified Adversarial Robustness via Randomized Smoothing" (ICML 2019) — Theorem 1, CERTIFY (§3.2), Table 1 and Figs. 2–3.

17. What the Attacker Knows

Why + threat model · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈25.5 min

Knowledge next. White box: the attacker has the weights and can compute gradients. Query access: the attacker can send inputs and see scores or labels, like a public API. No access: the attacker trains or downloads a model of their own and hopes the attack transfers.

Defenses are evaluated against the white box. Why the strongest attacker? Because models leak, get copied, or are approximated through queries; and because "the attacker does not know our model" is security through obscurity, not robustness.

Key takeaway: Evaluate defenses against the strongest attacker: white box.

18. Attacks Transfer Between Models

Why + threat model · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈27.5 min

Why does the no-access attacker have a chance at all? Transfer. Papernot and colleagues crafted adversarial examples on one kind of model and tested them on another.

Read the heat map like this. Each row is the model the attack was crafted on; each column is the model it was tested on; each cell is the percentage of examples that fooled the tested model. Crafted on logistic regression, 91.43% also fool an SVM. Some pairs transfer poorly: crafted on a neural net, only 8.36% fool k-nearest-neighbours.

So an attacker can often build a substitute, attack it, and succeed against a model they never saw.

Key takeaway: Adversarial examples often transfer across models and even model types.

The figure

Fig. 3 of Papernot et al.: cross-technique transferability matrix. Rows (source): DNN, LR, SVM, DT, kNN; columns (target) add an ensemble. Cell (i, j) = % of examples crafted on i misclassified by j. E.g. LR→SVM 91.43%, LR→LR 91.64%, SVM→SVM 100%, LR→Ens 44.14%.

Setup, model, date

MNIST. FGSM with ε = 0.3 for DNN and LR sources (an SVM-specific method with ε = 1.5 for SVM; other techniques use their own methods). 2016.

What this establishes

On MNIST, many attacks transfer across model families, sometimes at high rates.

What this does not establish

Transfer rates for modern image models, other datasets, or defended targets.

Primary sources

  • Papernot, McDaniel & Goodfellow, "Transferability in Machine Learning: from Phenomena to Black-Box Attacks using Adversarial Samples" (arXiv 2016) — cross-technique transfer (Fig. 3) and the attacks on Amazon and Google services (§5).

19. Transfer Reached Real Services

Why + threat model · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈29 min

Transfer was not only a lab trick. In 2016 the same authors attacked two commercial machine-learning services, Amazon and Google, that had been trained on MNIST through the services' own APIs.

The pipeline: query the service 800 times and record only the labels it returns; train a local substitute, here a logistic regression; attack the substitute with FGSM at ε = 0.3; send the result to the service. The services misclassified 96.19% (Amazon) and 88.94% (Google) of the adversarial digits.

Read the limits. This was MNIST digits and 2016 services; it shows black-box transfer is practical, not anything about today's APIs.

Key takeaway: With labels only, a substitute model let attacks reach real services (MNIST, 2016).

The figure

Pipeline schematic drawn for this lecture from §5 of Papernot et al.

Setup, model, date

Amazon Machine Learning (which used multinomial logistic regression) and Google Cloud Prediction API, 2016; MNIST models trained by the authors on the services; logistic-regression substitute trained with 800 label queries (ρ = 3 augmentation rounds); FGSM ε = 0.3. Tables 3–4.

What this establishes

Black-box attacks via substitute models were feasible against real hosted classifiers.

What this does not establish

Rates for modern hosted models, large images, or services with defenses.

Primary sources

  • Papernot, McDaniel & Goodfellow, "Transferability in Machine Learning: from Phenomena to Black-Box Attacks using Adversarial Samples" (arXiv 2016) — cross-technique transfer (Fig. 3) and the attacks on Amazon and Google services (§5).

20. Any Wrong Label, or a Chosen One

Why + threat model · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈30 min

Finally, what the attacker wants. Untargeted: turn a stop sign into anything else; push the loss on the true label up. Targeted: turn it into "speed limit 45" specifically; push the loss on the target down. Targeted is harder, and often more harmful.

Robust accuracy is usually reported against untargeted attacks: the model counts as correct only if no allowed change makes it wrong in any way.

Key takeaway: Untargeted is the usual benchmark; targeted is harder and often worse.

21. 03 — Finding the Worst Case

Attacks · ≈0.5 min · elapsed ≈30.5 min

An attack is a search. The lesson of this section: a weak search makes a model look more robust than it is.

Key takeaway: Attacks are searches; weak searches mislead.

22. An Attack Is a Search Inside the Ball

Attacks · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈32.5 min

Formally, the attacker solves: maximize the loss L of the model's output on x + δ, against the true label y, over all δ with size at most ε.

Three ingredients. The loss, which is high when the model is wrong. The gradient of the loss with respect to the input, which says in which direction the loss rises. And the constraint: stay inside the ball.

This mirrors training. Training lowers the loss by changing the weights; an attack raises it by changing the input, with the weights fixed.

Key takeaway: Attack = maximize the loss over the allowed ball.

The figure

Equation plus three boxes drawn for this lecture.

Going further

Other formulations exist. Carlini & Wagner (2017) minimize the size of the change plus a penalty: minimize ‖δ‖₂² + c · f(x + δ), where f is a margin loss on the logits Z, f(x′) = max(max_{i≠t} Z(x′)ᵢ − Z(x′)ₜ, −κ); f ≤ 0 means the target t wins or ties, and f = −κ once t leads by at least κ. They handle the pixel range with a change of variables, δ = ½(tanh(w) + 1) − x, and search over c. κ controls how confident the misclassification must be. Their attacks found adversarial examples with 100% success against defensive distillation, a defense that had reduced earlier attacks' success from 95% to 0.5%. Details: lec08tech.html.

Primary sources

  • Madry et al., "Towards Deep Learning Models Resistant to Adversarial Attacks" (ICLR 2018) — the saddle-point view, PGD, adversarial training; Table 2 for CIFAR-10 numbers; Appendix on ℓ2 evaluation.
  • Carlini & Wagner, "Towards Evaluating the Robustness of Neural Networks" (IEEE S&P 2017) — the C&W optimization attacks; they defeated defensive distillation.

23. FGSM: One Step Along the Gradient Sign

Attacks · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈35.5 min

The simplest attack: the Fast Gradient Sign Method. Compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the input once. Take its sign, plus or minus for each pixel. Move every pixel by ε in that direction. One step.

Why the sign? Under the ℓ∞ budget, each pixel may move at most ε, and if we replace the loss by its linearization, the best move is to push every pixel the full ε in the direction that raises the loss. That is the sign.

The figure shows it on a linear model, logistic regression distinguishing MNIST 3s from 7s. Panel (a) is the weights, (b) their sign, which is exactly the attack direction; (c) clean digits, (d) attacked digits at ε = .25. Error goes from 1.6% to 99%. The digits are still easy for us to read.

Key takeaway: FGSM: one step of size ε along the gradient sign; it exactly maximizes the linearized loss.

The figure

Fig. 2 of Goodfellow et al.: (a) weights of a logistic regression model on MNIST 3 vs 7; (b) the sign of the weights (the optimal ℓ∞ perturbation); (c) MNIST 3s and 7s (1.6% error); (d) FGSM examples at ε = .25 (99% error).

Setup, model, date

Logistic regression, MNIST 3 vs 7, ε = .25. 2015.

What this establishes

FGSM exactly maximizes the linearized loss over the ℓ∞ box; for this linear score model that is the optimal ℓ∞ attack, and it is devastating.

What this does not establish

That FGSM is strong against deep networks, especially defended ones; see slides 25 and 29.

Primary sources

  • Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy, "Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples" (ICLR 2015) — the panda (Fig. 1), FGSM, the "too linear" argument (§3) and Figs. 2 and 4.

24. FGSM by Hand: Only the Sign Matters

Attacks · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈38 min

Let us do one by hand, with three pixels and illustrative numbers. Pixel values 0.20, 0.50, 0.85; gradient +0.70, −0.10, +0.02. Signs: plus, minus, plus. With ε = 0.1, the new values are 0.30, 0.40, 0.95.

Notice pixel 3: its gradient is tiny, almost zero, but it still moves the full ε. FGSM ignores how much each pixel matters, only which way. That is why it is fast, and also why it is crude.

In practice we also clip to the valid pixel range [0, 1]. Here all values stay inside.

Key takeaway: FGSM moves every pixel the full ε; magnitude of the gradient is ignored.

Assumptions

Numbers invented for illustration; no model is implied.

25. One Step Can Miss the Worst Point

Attacks · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈40 min

The weakness of one step. Here is a 1-D loss, L(u) = sin u, along one direction. The shaded interval is the allowed ball around x. FGSM follows the tangent at x, the dashed line, all the way to the edge of the interval. The tangent predicts a high loss there, the hollow point. But the real loss curves: it peaks inside the interval, at the green point, and is lower where FGSM actually lands.

So FGSM can overshoot. It can also undershoot, if the loss keeps rising past a bend. Either way, one linearized step can miss the worst point when the loss is not linear, which is exactly the case for deep networks and especially for defended ones.

Key takeaway: One linear step can overshoot or undershoot a curved loss.

The figure

Illustrative 1-D figure drawn for this lecture with exact geometry: L(u) = sin u, x at u = 0.6, ε = 1.6 (interval [−1, 2.2]); interior maximum at u = π/2; FGSM endpoint at u = 2.2; tangent prediction at the endpoint shown hollow.

Assumptions

Illustrative function, not a real network loss.

26. PGD: Many Small Steps, Then Project

Attacks · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈43 min

The fix: Projected Gradient Descent, or more accurately ascent. Five steps. One, start at a random point inside the ball, not at x itself. Two, take a small step of size α along the gradient sign. Three, project: if the step left the ball or the valid pixel range, clip it back. Four, repeat K times; PGD-7 or PGD-20 means 7 or 20 steps. Five, restart from several random points and keep the one with the highest loss.

FGSM is the special case with no random start, step size α = ε, and one step.

Madry and colleagues argued PGD is the strongest attack that uses only first-order information, the gradient. That made it the default attack for evaluating and training robust models.

Key takeaway: PGD = random start + many small sign steps + projection + restarts.

Setup, model, date

Madry et al., §2.1 and §3. Typical CIFAR-10 settings in the paper: ε = 8/255, step 2/255, 7 steps for training, 20 for evaluation.

Assumptions

"Strongest first-order attack" is an empirical claim, supported by experiments like the next two slides, not a theorem.

Primary sources

  • Madry et al., "Towards Deep Learning Models Resistant to Adversarial Attacks" (ICLR 2018) — the saddle-point view, PGD, adversarial training; Table 2 for CIFAR-10 numbers; Appendix on ℓ2 evaluation.

27. Steps Stay Inside the Ball

Attacks · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈45 min

A picture of PGD in the ℓ∞ box. Start at a random point. Each step climbs the loss. When a step would leave the box, projection clips it back to the edge. The final point is the most damaging input found inside the box.

Projection matters for honesty: every iterate is a legal attack under the threat model. An attack that wanders outside the ball is answering a different question.

Key takeaway: Projection keeps the search inside the threat model.

The figure

Schematic drawn for this lecture: an ℓ∞ box, a random start, a path of steps, one step leaving the box and being clipped back.

28. Many Restarts Reach Similar Loss

Attacks · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈47 min

How do we know PGD searches well? Madry and colleagues ran PGD from 20 random starts on the same image and plotted the loss over iterations. Each coloured line is one run.

In all four panels, MNIST and CIFAR-10, standard and adversarially trained models, the runs rise and level off at similar values. Different starts find similar maxima. That suggests the landscape inside the ball has many local maxima of similar height, and PGD finds one of them reliably.

This is evidence that PGD is a strong first-order attack. It is not a proof that no worse point exists.

Key takeaway: Restarts plateau at similar loss: evidence, not proof, that PGD searches well.

The figure

Fig. 1 of Madry et al.: cross-entropy loss vs PGD iteration (0–100) for 20 random restarts; panels (a) MNIST standard, (b) MNIST adversarial training, (c) CIFAR-10 natural, (d) CIFAR-10 adversarial training.

Setup, model, date

MNIST (ε = 0.3) and CIFAR-10 (ε = 8) ℓ∞; ICLR 2018.

What this establishes

For these models, PGD from random starts reaches consistent loss levels.

What this does not establish

29. Weaker Attacks Overstate Robustness

Attacks · ≈3 min · elapsed ≈50 min

Why the attack matters, in one chart. The same adversarially trained CIFAR-10 model, the same ℓ∞ budget of 8/255, white box. With no attack, 87.3% accuracy. Against FGSM, 56.1%. PGD with 7 steps, 50.0%. The C&W-style margin loss with 30 steps, 46.8%. PGD with 20 steps, 45.8%.

Same model. The only thing that changed is how hard we searched, and the number dropped by ten points from FGSM to PGD-20. If a paper had reported only FGSM, it would have overstated robustness.

The rule: report the strongest attack you can find, and treat the number as an upper bound on true robust accuracy.

Key takeaway: Stronger search, lower number: robust accuracy is only as honest as the attack.

What this establishes

For this model, accuracy under attack falls as the attack strengthens.

Going further

32. Adversarial Training: Train on the Worst Case

Defenses + evaluation · ≈2.5 min · elapsed ≈54.5 min

The defense that has held up best: adversarial training. Write training as a game. The inner max: for each training example, find the worst δ in the ball, using PGD. The outer min: update the weights to do well on those worst-case inputs.

The figure gives the intuition. Left: points separable by a simple straight line. Middle: put an ℓ∞ box around each point; the line now cuts through boxes, and the stars are adversarial examples. Right: to clear every box, the model needs a more complicated, bent boundary. Robustness needs more capacity.

Key takeaway: Adversarial training: minimize the loss on the worst-case inputs.

33. Robustness Can Cost Clean Accuracy

Defenses + evaluation · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈56.5 min

Robustness is not free. On the slide is the CIFAR-10 panel of their Fig. 1; the paper also has MNIST and Restricted ImageNet panels with the same pattern. Tsipras and colleagues plotted clean, standard accuracy against the number of training samples for models adversarially trained with different budgets ε, under the ℓ2 norm. ε = 0 is standard training.

With enough data, the curves for larger ε sit lower: more robust models are less accurate on clean inputs. In their theoretical model, this trade-off is unavoidable. Whether it is unavoidable in practice is debated; it is a perspective with evidence, not a law.

There is also a compute cost. Every training step first runs an attack, so PGD-7 training costs roughly eight times a normal step.

Key takeaway: Robustness often costs clean accuracy and compute.

34. Some Defenses Only Break the Attack

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈58 min

Many proposed defenses did something else: they broke the attacker's gradient without removing adversarial examples. Athalye, Carlini and Wagner call this obfuscated gradients, and name three kinds.

Shattered: a non-differentiable step, like quantizing the input, gives useless gradients. Stochastic: randomness in the forward pass gives a different gradient each time. Vanishing or exploding: very deep computation or iterative purification flattens the gradient, then jumps.

In all three, gradient attacks fail, so the defense looks strong. But adversarial examples still exist; the attacker just needs a better way to find them.

Key takeaway: Obfuscated gradients hide adversarial examples from the attack; they do not remove them.

36. Adaptive Attacks: Attack the Defense You Have

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈61.5 min

How to evaluate a defense properly: an adaptive attack, designed for that specific defense. Read the defense and target its weak part.

Two standard tools. If a step is not differentiable, replace it on the backward pass with a differentiable approximation, often just the identity: BPDA, Backward Pass Differentiable Approximation. If the defense is random, average the gradient over many random draws: EOT, Expectation over Transformation.

And two diagnostics. With a huge ε, accuracy should go to about zero; if not, suspect the attack. If a black-box attack beats a white-box one, suspect masked gradients. These raise red flags; passing them proves nothing.

Key takeaway: Adapt the attack to the defense; diagnostics flag problems but prove nothing.

Setup, model, date

Athalye et al. §4 (BPDA, EOT); Carlini et al. (2019) for the checklist; Tramèr et al. (2020) re-evaluated 13 further defenses with adaptive attacks.

Primary sources

  • Athalye, Carlini & Wagner, "Obfuscated Gradients Give a False Sense of Security" (ICML 2018) — three kinds of obfuscated gradients, BPDA and EOT, and Table 1.

37. AutoAttack: A Fixed, Parameter-Free Ensemble

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈63 min

Adaptive attacks need expertise. A complementary idea: a strong, fixed, standard attack everyone runs. AutoAttack, from Croce and Hein, combines four attacks. APGD-CE: PGD with an automatic step-size schedule and cross-entropy loss. APGD-T: targeted, with a margin-style loss called DLR. FAB-T: finds a minimal change. Square: uses only queries, no gradients, which catches gradient masking.

An input counts as robust only if all four fail. On more than 50 published models, AutoAttack gave lower robust accuracy than reported in all but one case, by more than 10 points in 13 of 49.

Key takeaway: A fixed ensemble of diverse attacks lowered almost every reported number.

Setup, model, date

ICML 2020. No hyperparameters to tune, so results are comparable across papers.

What this does not establish

AutoAttack is not adaptive. It can still miss defenses that need a custom attack.

38. RobustBench: A Standardized Benchmark

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈64.5 min

RobustBench turns AutoAttack into a leaderboard. The protocol: fixed datasets and threat models, AutoAttack run on every entry, and outside adaptive attacks welcome; if someone finds a stronger attack, the entry's "best known" number is lowered.

Entry rules matter. Models must have non-zero gradients, a deterministic forward pass, and no optimization loop inside. Why? Those are exactly the cases where AutoAttack could be fooled by masking. So the rules exclude defenses AutoAttack cannot fairly test; such defenses need custom evaluation.

RobustBench is a standardized benchmark. It is not the universal standard for robustness, and it covers only its listed threat models.

Key takeaway: A standardized benchmark with explicit rules and explicit limits.

42. Empirical vs Certified Robustness

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1 min · elapsed ≈70 min

Everything so far was empirical: the attacks we tried failed. That gives an upper bound, which may fall.

The alternative is certified robustness: a proof that no change in a stated region can change the prediction. A certificate proves the prediction is stable, not that it is correct. Certified accuracy counts only examples that are both correct and certified, and that number is a lower bound on robust accuracy, whatever attack comes next.

Key takeaway: Empirical = upper bound that may fall; certified accuracy = lower bound (stable and correct).

43. Randomized Smoothing: Vote Under Noise

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈71.5 min

One practical certification method: randomized smoothing. Take any classifier f, the base classifier. Build a new one, g, the smoothed classifier: g returns the class f picks most often when Gaussian noise is added to x. In practice g cannot be computed exactly. The paper's PREDICT and CERTIFY procedures estimate it by sampling noisy copies, and they abstain when no class clearly leads.

The figure shows one noisy copy of the panda. Heavy noise, still recognizably a panda. If f is trained on noisy images, it can still classify them.

Key takeaway: Smoothed classifier g = majority vote of f under Gaussian noise.

The figure

Fig. 2 of Cohen et al.: a panda image x and the same image with Gaussian noise.

Setup, model, date

g(x) = argmax_c P(f(x + η) = c), η ~ N(0, σ²I); Cohen et al. Eq. 1; PREDICT and CERTIFY in §3.2. ICML 2019.

Primary sources

  • Cohen, Rosenfeld & Kolter, "Certified Adversarial Robustness via Randomized Smoothing" (ICML 2019) — Theorem 1, CERTIFY (§3.2), Table 1 and Figs. 2–3.

44. A Clear Vote Survives Any Small Shift

Defenses + evaluation · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈73.5 min

Why does voting give a certificate? Intuition: if the vote at x is lopsided, say 90% panda, then moving x a little only shifts the noise cloud a little; the vote cannot swing to another class until you move far enough.

Cohen and colleagues make this exact in Theorem 1. A bigger vote margin, and more noise σ for the same margin, give a bigger ℓ2 radius R within which g cannot change its answer. The exact formula is in the technical supplement. The figure shows why: the worst-case base classifier is a straight cut perpendicular to the shift. Note that raising σ also changes the votes, so more noise does not automatically mean a bigger radius.

Three careful points. It certifies g, the smoothed classifier, not the original f. The vote probabilities are estimated by sampling: if CERTIFY returns a class and a radius, then with probability at least 1 − α the radius is valid; the paper uses α = 0.001. If no class clearly leads, CERTIFY abstains. And the certificate says the prediction is stable, not that it is correct.

Key takeaway: A clear vote gives a provable ℓ2 radius for g, with statistical confidence; stability, not correctness.

The figure

Fig. 3 of Cohen et al.: Gaussian noise around x and x + δ (circles), with blue/red regions of two base classifiers; right, the worst-case f*, a linear classifier whose boundary is normal to δ.

Setup, model, date

Theorem 1: R = σ/2 (Φ⁻¹(p_A) − Φ⁻¹(p_B)); CERTIFY (§3.2). Formula and proof sketch in lec08tech.html.

What this establishes

If CERTIFY returns (c_A, R), then with probability ≥ 1 − α over the sampling, g predicts c_A everywhere within ℓ2 radius R.

What this does not establish

That c_A is the correct label; anything about the base classifier f; ℓ∞ robustness beyond what the ℓ2 radius implies; inputs where CERTIFY abstains.

Primary sources

  • Cohen, Rosenfeld & Kolter, "Certified Adversarial Robustness via Randomized Smoothing" (ICML 2019) — Theorem 1, CERTIFY (§3.2), Table 1 and Figs. 2–3.

45. Certificates Are Real, but Modest

Defenses + evaluation · ≈1.5 min · elapsed ≈75 min

How big are the certificates? ImageNet, ResNet-50 base, 500 test images, α = 0.001. Grey bars: the accuracy of the same smoothed model at radius 0. Blue bars: approximate certified accuracy at radius R, meaning the fraction of images that are both correct and certified, estimated by sampling.

At R = 0.5: 49%. At 1.0: 37%. At 2.0: 19%, using σ = 1.0, whose radius-0 accuracy is 44%. Recall that radius 1.0 can mean 100 pixels changed by 25 each; real, but not large. And the cost: 100,000 noisy forward passes per image, about 110 seconds each on the paper's hardware.

Certificates are real, but modest, and they cover only the stated ℓ2 region.

49. What the Stop-Sign Result Does Not Show

Physical + synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈81.5 min

Now read the limits as carefully as the result. What is shown: printed stickers can fool a classifier, across the tested angles and distances, in a slow drive-by on a straight road.

What is not shown. It attacks a classifier, not an object detector or a full driving system. The pipeline crops the sign by hand before classifying; with constrained pseudo-random crops instead, which keep most of the sign in view, targeted success fell to 70% (untargeted 90%). The drive-by went from about 250 feet at 0–20 mph on a straight road, not at speed, on any road, in any weather.

This is how to read every physical-attack paper: the result is real, and its scope is exactly what was tested.

Key takeaway: Real result, narrow scope: classifier, controlled cropping, slow straight drive.

51. Four Questions for Any Robustness Claim

Physical + synthesis · ≈2 min · elapsed ≈85 min

Here is the checklist. One: robust to what changes? Norm, ε, digital or physical. Two: against which attacker? What they know, what they want, and was the attack adaptive. Three: measured how? Empirical or certified, and on which date. Four: at what cost? Clean accuracy, compute, abstentions.

A claim missing any answer is incomplete, not necessarily wrong. Ask for the missing piece.

Key takeaway: What changes, which attacker, measured how, at what cost.
