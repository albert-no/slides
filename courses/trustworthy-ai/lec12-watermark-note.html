Title

This lecture is about two questions: how do you prove a model is yours, and how do you prove a piece of content came from a machine. We focus mostly on the second.

Key takeaway: watermarking and provenance are how we keep "real vs synthetic" meaningful online.

Contents

Key takeaway: the arc moves from a clean idea to its limits to the surrounding policy.

The Problem

AI output now passes for human work across every medium. Nothing in the content itself tells you a machine made it, or whose model it was.

Key takeaway: a watermark is a signal we deliberately add to recover that missing information.

What a Watermark Is

A watermark is an invisible mark hidden in content: imperceptible to a person, but readable by a detector holding a secret key. The concept predates computers (banknotes, paper).

Key takeaway: the new twist is hiding the mark in what a generative model produces.

Definitions

Watermarking scheme (Christ, Gunn & Zamir). A watermarking scheme for a generative model is a triple of algorithms $\mathcal{W} = (\mathsf{Setup}, \mathsf{Wat}, \mathsf{Detect})$: $\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})$ outputs a secret key $sk$ (security parameter $\lambda$); $\mathsf{Wat}_{sk}(\text{prompt})$ is a randomized algorithm producing a response in place of the unmarked model; and $\mathsf{Detect}_{sk}(x) \in \{\texttt{true}, \texttt{false}\}$ decides from the text $x$ and the key alone — with no access to the model or the prompt — whether $x$ was produced by $\mathsf{Wat}_{sk}$. The whole lecture instantiates this triple in different ways.

Background & further reading

  • Christ, Gunn & Zamir, "Undetectable Watermarks for Language Models" (arXiv 2306.09194) — the cryptographic formalization of watermarking used above; revisited in the "Undetectable Watermarks" entry.

Two Flavors

The same word covers two different goals: a model watermark proves a model is yours, while an output watermark proves a piece of content is AI-generated.

Key takeaway: always ask which question a "watermark" is answering.

Definitions

Model watermark. A statistical signature embedded in the parameters or input–output behavior of a model $f_{\theta}$, verified by querying a suspect model $f'$ and testing whether $f'$ reproduces behavior that only the owner could have planted. The claim proved is ownership: "$f'$ is derived from my $f_{\theta}$".

Output watermark. A statistical signal embedded, at generation time and under a secret key, in each individual output $x$, verified by a keyed detector applied to $x$ alone. The claim proved is provenance of the content: "this $x$ was generated by a keyed model".

Model Watermarks: Prove Ownership

An owner trains the model to react to a secret trigger that ordinary users never encounter. Querying that trigger later proves the model is a copy of theirs.

Key takeaway: a model watermark is a hidden signature baked into the weights.

Definitions

Backdoor-based model watermark (Adi et al.). The owner fixes a secret trigger set $T = \{(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\}$ of inputs $x_i$ chosen far from the task distribution, paired with secretly chosen labels $y_i$, and trains the model to fit both the task data and $T$. Verification queries a suspect model on the $x_i$ and counts agreements with the secret $y_i$: an independent model matches the arbitrary labels only with vanishing probability, so high agreement is statistical proof of derivation. Adi et al. frame the guarantee via cryptographic commitments to the trigger set, so ownership can be demonstrated to a third party without revealing $T$ in advance.

Background & further reading

  • Adi, Baum, Cissé, Pinkas & Keshet, "Turning Your Weakness Into a Strength: Watermarking Deep Neural Networks by Backdooring" (arXiv 1802.04633) — the standard backdoor-watermarking construction sketched above.
  • USENIX Security 2018 presentation page — the published venue for the same paper.

Output Watermarks: Flag the Content

Here the generator cooperates: it tilts its own output so the result carries a hidden, detectable pattern while still looking normal. This is our main focus.

Key takeaway: the generator plants the signal as it writes, so detection is far more reliable.

Definitions

Keyed output watermarking. The provider samples the response using randomness (or a bias) derived from a secret key $k$; the detector $D_k$ maps a candidate text to a verdict using only $k$ and the text. The two quantities that measure such a scheme — soundness (false positives on unmarked text) and completeness (missed detections on marked text) — are defined precisely in the "Undetectable Watermarks" entry; the statistical version of the same idea is the hypothesis test of the next entries.

Why We Care

Schools, elections, and courts all need to tell genuine content from synthetic. The stakes are concrete, not academic.

Key takeaway: online trust rests on distinguishing real from synthetic content.

Watermark vs Detector

A watermark assumes the generator cooperated and planted a signal. A detector looks at finished content where nothing was planted and must guess.

Key takeaway: a planted signal is fundamentally more trustworthy than a post-hoc guess.

Definitions

Detection as a binary hypothesis test. Model human text as a distribution $H$ and machine text as a distribution $M$ over documents. A detector is a (possibly randomized) decision rule; on a document it either flags or not. Its false-positive rate is $\mathrm{FPR} = \Pr_{x \sim H}[\text{flag}]$ and its true-positive rate is $\mathrm{TPR} = \Pr_{x \sim M}[\text{flag}]$. The total variation distance is $\mathrm{TV}(M, H) = \sup_{A} |M(A) - H(A)|$ over measurable events $A$.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (Neyman–Pearson / TV identity; classical, stated as in the privacy-course watermark deck). For every detector, $\mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR} \le \mathrm{TV}(M, H)$, and the supremum over detectors is attained: $\sup\,(\mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR}) = \mathrm{TV}(M, H)$. Moreover every optimal ROC point is achieved by thresholding the likelihood ratio $\Lambda = dM/dH$ (Neyman–Pearson lemma).

Proof of the inequality and the identity. A deterministic detector is an event $A$ (the set of flagged documents), and $\mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR} = M(A) - H(A) \le \sup_{A'} |M(A') - H(A')| = \mathrm{TV}(M, H)$; randomized detectors are mixtures of deterministic ones, so the bound persists. Equality holds for $A^{\ast} = \{x : \Lambda(x) \gt 1\}$, which realizes the supremum in the definition of TV. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes; the statement is classical.)

Why watermarks escape this. The bound says a key-less detector can only be as good as the gap between $M$ and $H$ — and that gap is shrinking as models improve. A watermark changes the game: the provider deliberately moves $M$ (under a key) so that, for the key holder, the two distributions are far apart in TV, while to everyone else the text looks unchanged. This is exactly the quantity Sadasivan et al.'s impossibility bound is written in — see "The Impossibility Claim".

How a Model Writes

A language model generates one token at a time: at each step it scores every possible next token, samples one, and repeats. A token is a word or word-piece.

Key takeaway: each generation step is a chance to hide a signal.

Definitions

Autoregressive language model. Over a vocabulary $V$ of size $N$, the model defines $p(x_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1})$. At step $t$ it produces a logit $\ell_v \in \mathbb{R}$ for every $v \in V$ and samples from the softmax $$p_v \;=\; \frac{e^{\ell_v}}{\sum_{w \in V} e^{\ell_w}}.$$ All watermarks in this lecture intervene either on the logits $\ell_v$ (biasing schemes) or on the randomness used to sample from $p$ (distortion-free schemes).

The Green-List Idea

At each step, a secret key splits the vocabulary into a favored "green list" and the rest, the "red list". The model is nudged to prefer green tokens.

Key takeaway: the watermark is just a per-step bias toward a pseudo-random token set.

Definitions

Soft watermark, generation side (Kirchenbauer et al., ICML 2023). Parameters: green fraction $\gamma \in (0,1)$, bias $\delta \gt 0$. At each step $t$:

  1. Compute logits $\ell^{(t)}_v$ for the next token as usual.
  2. Seed a pseudorandom function with the key and the previous token $s^{(t-1)}$ (the paper's default context width is $h = 1$: "the red list generator is seeded with the prior token") and use it to partition $V$ into a green list $G_t$ of size $\gamma N$ and a red list $R_t = V \setminus G_t$.
  3. Add $\delta$ to every green logit: $\hat{\ell}_v = \ell_v + \delta\,\mathbf{1}[v \in G_t]$.
  4. Sample the next token from the softmax of $\hat{\ell}$.

Background & further reading

  • Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers & Goldstein, "A Watermark for Large Language Models" (arXiv 2301.10226, ICML 2023) — the green-list scheme, its z-test, and the theorems in the entries below.

Splitting the Vocabulary

The green list is regenerated fresh at each step from the secret key, so without the key the split looks random and the bias is undetectable.

Key takeaway: the secret key is what makes the green list both reproducible and hidden.

Definitions

Pseudorandom split. The partition at step $t$ is $G_t = F_k(s^{(t-h)}, \dots, s^{(t-1)})$ where $F_k$ is a pseudorandom function keyed by $k$ applied to the last $h$ tokens of context. The split is thus (i) deterministic given the key and the context — the detector can recompute it from the text alone — and (ii) computationally indistinguishable from a fresh uniform split to anyone without the key.

The context-width tradeoff. Larger $h$ makes repeated contexts (and hence correlated splits) rarer, but makes the mark more fragile: editing one token corrupts the seeds of the $h$ following steps. Smaller $h$ (down to $h = 0$, a single fixed list — see "Small Edits Survive") maximizes edit robustness at the price of a learnable list and more repeated-context correlation.

The Nudge

The mechanism adds a small bonus $\delta$ to every green token's score before sampling. A larger $\delta$ strengthens the watermark but distorts the text more.

Key takeaway: $\delta$ is the single dial controlling watermark strength.

Definitions

The tilt. With $\alpha := e^{\delta}$, adding $\delta$ to green logits multiplies green probabilities by $\alpha$ before renormalizing: $$\hat{p}(v) \;=\; \frac{p(v)\,\alpha^{\mathbf{1}[v \in G_t]}}{\sum_{w} p(w)\,\alpha^{\mathbf{1}[w \in G_t]}}.$$

Theorem & proof

Proposition (green-mass boost; privacy-course deck, Proposition 1 there). Fix a split with pre-tilt green mass $g = \sum_{v \in G} p(v)$. The post-tilt green mass is $$\hat{g} \;=\; \varphi_{\alpha}(g) \;:=\; \frac{\alpha g}{1 + (\alpha - 1)g},$$ and $\varphi_{\alpha}$ is strictly increasing and strictly concave on $[0,1]$ with $\varphi_{\alpha}(0) = 0$, $\varphi_{\alpha}(1) = 1$, and $\hat{g} \gt g$ for all $g \in (0,1)$ when $\alpha \gt 1$.

Proof. The tilt multiplies each green weight by $\alpha$ and leaves red weights alone, so $\hat{g} = \alpha g / (\alpha g + (1-g)) = \alpha g / (1 + (\alpha-1)g)$. Differentiating, $\varphi_{\alpha}'(g) = \alpha / (1 + (\alpha-1)g)^2 \gt 0$ and $\varphi_{\alpha}''(g) = -2\alpha(\alpha-1)/(1 + (\alpha-1)g)^3 \lt 0$ for $\alpha \gt 1$. The endpoints are immediate, and $$\hat{g} - g \;=\; \frac{(\alpha - 1)\,g(1-g)}{1 + (\alpha - 1)g} \;\gt\; 0 \quad \text{for } 0 \lt g \lt 1. \qquad \blacksquare$$ (Verified line by line against the privacy-course watermark deck.)

Reading It Back

The detector knows the key, so for each generated token it can re-derive whether that token was green, then count the green fraction. An unusually high count signals the watermark.

Key takeaway: detection needs only the key, not access to the model.

Definitions

The green count. Given text $s = (s^{(1)}, \dots, s^{(T)})$ and the key, the detector recomputes each split $G_t = F_k(s^{(t-1)})$ from the text itself and counts $$|s|_G \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{1}\big[s^{(t)} \in G_t\big].$$ Everything needed — the key, the tokenizer, the hash — is available without the model, without the prompt, and without any API call. This is what makes green-list detection cheap and offline.

The Telltale Fraction

If the green list is half the vocabulary, human text lands near 50% green by chance, while watermarked text is pushed much higher, say 80%.

Key takeaway: the gap between the chance rate and the watermarked rate is the signal.

Definitions

Where "80%" comes from. Under the null the expected green fraction is exactly $\gamma$. Under the watermark, by the boost proposition ("The Nudge"), a step whose pre-tilt green mass is $g$ contributes green probability $\varphi_{\alpha}(g)$. For the paper's running parameters $\gamma = \tfrac12$, $\delta = 2$ (so $\alpha = e^{2} \approx 7.39$), a step with $g = \tfrac12$ has post-tilt green probability $\varphi_{\alpha}(\tfrac12) = \alpha/(\alpha+1) \approx 0.88$ (derivation: course notes). The realized fraction depends on the entropy of each step — low-entropy steps resist the tilt — so the slide's 80% is a representative figure; the distribution-dependent guarantee is Theorem 4.2 in "The One-Knob Tradeoff".

A Detection Threshold

Plot the green fraction for human and watermarked text as two overlapping bells, and place a threshold between them. Text above the line is called watermarked.

Key takeaway: detection reduces to "is the green fraction suspiciously high?"

Definitions

Null hypothesis (Kirchenbauer et al., verbatim): "The text sequence is generated with no knowledge of the red list rule." A writer who never saw the key hits each fresh pseudorandom green list independently with probability $\gamma$, so under $H_0$: $$|s|_G \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(T, \gamma), \qquad \mathbb{E}|s|_G = \gamma T, \qquad \operatorname{Var}|s|_G = T\gamma(1-\gamma).$$ z-statistic and rule. $$z \;=\; \frac{|s|_G - \gamma T}{\sqrt{T\gamma(1-\gamma)}}, \qquad \text{flag when } z \gt \tau.$$ At $\tau = 4$ the Gaussian tail gives false-positive rate $1 - \Phi(4) \approx 3.2 \times 10^{-5}$ (the paper quotes $3 \times 10^{-5}$). As a check on the approximation: for $\gamma = \tfrac12$, $T = 200$, the rule $z \gt 4$ means $|s|_G \ge 129$, and the exact binomial tail is $\Pr[\mathrm{Bin}(200, \tfrac12) \ge 129] = 2.5 \times 10^{-5}$ (derivation: course notes) — the Gaussian figure is accurate and slightly conservative here.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (normal-approximation error; standard Berry–Esseen, stated as in the privacy-course deck, Theorem 2 there). Under $H_0$, $z \to \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ in distribution, and for every $T$, $$\sup_{x} \big| \Pr[z \le x] - \Phi(x) \big| \;\le\; \frac{C\,\rho}{\sqrt{T}}, \qquad \rho = \frac{\gamma^2 + (1-\gamma)^2}{\sqrt{\gamma(1-\gamma)}}, \quad C \lt 0.5.$$

Proof. Under $H_0$ the indicators $Y_t = \mathbf{1}[s^{(t)} \in G_t]$ are i.i.d. $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\gamma)$ with $\sigma^2 = \gamma(1-\gamma)$ and third absolute central moment $$\mathbb{E}|Y_t - \gamma|^3 = \gamma(1-\gamma)^3 + (1-\gamma)\gamma^3 = \gamma(1-\gamma)\big(\gamma^2 + (1-\gamma)^2\big).$$ The Berry–Esseen theorem bounds the CLT error by $C \cdot \mathbb{E}|Y-\gamma|^3 / (\sigma^3 \sqrt{T})$, and dividing by $\sigma^3 = (\gamma(1-\gamma))^{3/2}$ gives $\rho$ as displayed. $\blacksquare$ (Moment computation: derivation, course notes; Berry–Esseen itself is classical.)

The Danger: False Positives

A false positive flags genuine human writing as AI. The false-positive rate is the chance a real human exceeds the threshold by pure luck.

Key takeaway: we must keep the false-positive rate tiny, because accusations are costly.

Definitions

Type-I error and p-value. The type-I error (false-positive rate) of the rule $z \gt \tau$ is $\Pr[z \gt \tau \mid H_0]$; the p-value of an observed $z$ is $\Pr[Z \ge z \mid H_0] \approx 1 - \Phi(z)$, the probability that pure luck produces evidence at least this strong.

Where the binomial null actually fails. The $\mathrm{Binomial}(T, \gamma)$ null needs the green indicators to be independent, which requires the hashed contexts to be distinct. Human text repeats phrases; a repeated $h$-gram reuses the same green list, so its indicators are perfectly correlated and the true null variance exceeds the binomial one — inflating false positives past the nominal rate. Kirchenbauer et al. note both standard fixes: enlarge the context width $h$, or count each distinct $h$-gram only once when scoring (verified in the paper).

Length Beats Luck

Over a long passage, the chance of a human reaching 80% green by luck shrinks to essentially zero, just as flipping many coins almost never gives 80% heads.

Key takeaway: longer text makes the verdict far safer.

Theorem & proof

Proposition (the "80% by luck" number; derivation: course notes via Hoeffding's inequality). Under $H_0$ with $\gamma = \tfrac12$, $$\Pr\big[|s|_G \ge 0.8\,T\big] \;\le\; \exp\!\big(-2T(0.3)^2\big) = e^{-0.18\,T},$$ which at $T = 200$ tokens is $e^{-36} \approx 2.3 \times 10^{-16}$ — "essentially zero" is literal.

Proof. The $Y_t$ are i.i.d. and $[0,1]$-valued with mean $\tfrac12$; Hoeffding's inequality gives $\Pr[\sum_t (Y_t - \tfrac12) \ge T\varepsilon] \le \exp(-2T\varepsilon^2)$; take $\varepsilon = 0.3$. $\blacksquare$

Scaling. Equivalently, in the z-picture (technical supplement): the watermarked green fraction $\gamma^{\star} \gt \gamma$ is roughly length-free, so $$z \;\approx\; \frac{(\gamma^{\star} - \gamma)\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{\gamma(1-\gamma)}} \;\propto\; \sqrt{T}:$$ evidence accumulates like $\sqrt{T}$ while the false-positive rate at fixed $\tau$ stays put. This $\sqrt{T}$ law is also what makes paraphrase a dilution rather than an erasure — see "The Honest Limit".

The One-Knob Tradeoff

Raising $\delta$ makes the watermark easier to detect on shorter text, but forces more unnatural word choices. Lowering it preserves quality but weakens the signal.

Key takeaway: detectability versus quality is the central watermark tradeoff.

Definitions

Spike entropy (Kirchenbauer et al., Definition 4.1). For a discrete probability vector $p$ and modulus $z \ge 0$, $$S(p, z) \;=\; \sum_{k} \frac{p_k}{1 + z\,p_k}.$$ It is minimized at $\tfrac{1}{1+z}$ when $p$ is a point mass and maximized at $\tfrac{N}{N+z}$ when $p$ is uniform, so it is a softened count of how many entries exceed $1/z$ — a measure of how much room the step leaves for the tilt to act.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (Kirchenbauer et al., Theorem 4.2; statement verified against the paper). Generate $T$ tokens by sampling $p^{(t)}$ from the model, drawing a fresh random green list of size $\gamma N$, and boosting green logits by $\delta$ before sampling. Let $\alpha = e^{\delta}$, $\beta := 1 + (\alpha - 1)\gamma$, and $z^{\star} := (1-\gamma)(\alpha-1)/\beta$. If the average spike entropy satisfies $$\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t} S\big(p^{(t)}, z^{\star}\big) \;\ge\; S^{\star},$$ then $$\mathbb{E}\,|s|_G \;\ge\; \frac{\gamma \alpha T}{\beta}\, S^{\star}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}|s|_G \;\le\; T\,\mu^{\star}(1 - \mu^{\star}), \quad \mu^{\star} := \frac{\gamma \alpha S^{\star}}{\beta},$$ and for $\gamma \ge \tfrac12$ also the strictly looser bound $\operatorname{Var}|s|_G \le T\gamma(1-\gamma)$. (The paper's remark: at $\gamma = \tfrac12$, $\delta = \ln 2$ this simplifies to $\mathbb{E}|s|_G \ge \tfrac{2}{3} T S^{\star}$, with spike modulus $\tfrac13$; as $\delta \to \infty$ the hard rule gives $\mathbb{E}|s|_G \ge T S^{\star}$ with modulus $1$.)

Proof (four steps, as given in the privacy-course deck — Theorem 4 there — consistent with the paper's appendix; verified step by step).

  1. Reduce to one step and one token. Fix a step with distribution $p$ and consider token $k$. Define the expected post-tilt probability of $k$ conditioned on $k$ landing green: $$f_k(p) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{G,R\,:\,k \in G} \left[ \frac{\alpha p_k}{\sum_{i \in R} p_i + \alpha \sum_{i \in G} p_i} \right].$$
  2. Symmetrize. $f_k$ is invariant under permutations of the other coordinates $\{p_i\}_{i \ne k}$ and jointly convex in them, so by Jensen's inequality, averaging over all permutations $\Pi$ of those coordinates, $$f_k(p) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\, f_k(\Pi p) \;\ge\; f_k(\bar{p}), \qquad \bar{p}_i = \frac{1 - p_k}{N - 1} \ (i \ne k).$$
  3. Evaluate at the symmetric point. With $N_G = \gamma N$ green and $N_R = (1-\gamma)N$ red slots, direct computation gives $$f_k(\bar{p}) \;=\; \frac{\alpha p_k (N-1)}{(N_R + \alpha N_G - \alpha)(1 - p_k) + \alpha p_k (N-1)} \;\ge\; \frac{\alpha}{\beta} \cdot \frac{p_k}{1 + z^{\star} p_k},$$ where the last inequality is algebra using $(1-\gamma) + \alpha\gamma = \beta$.
  4. Sum. The chance the sampled token is green is $\Pr[s^{(t)} \in G_t] = \sum_k \Pr[k \in G]\, f_k(p) \ge \gamma \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\beta} \sum_k \frac{p_k}{1 + z^{\star} p_k} = \frac{\gamma\alpha}{\beta}\, S(p^{(t)}, z^{\star})$. Summing over $t$ and applying the entropy hypothesis gives the mean bound. For the variance: the green lists are drawn fresh each step, so the indicators are independent (non-identical) Bernoullis with success probabilities $q_t \ge \mu^{\star}$, hence $\operatorname{Var}|s|_G = \sum_t q_t(1 - q_t) \le T \mu^{\star}(1 - \mu^{\star})$, using that $q \mapsto q(1-q)$ is decreasing on $[\tfrac12, 1]$ once $\mu^{\star} \ge \tfrac12$. $\blacksquare$

Worked numbers (paper, §4.1; all verified against the paper). $\gamma = 0.5$, $\delta = 2$, $T = 200$, OPT-1.3B on C4 RealNewsLike prompts, threshold $z = 4$ (about 128.2 green tokens out of 200; type-I error $3 \times 10^{-5}$). Measured average spike entropy $S^{\star} = 0.807$ over roughly 500 generations. The theorem then gives $\mathbb{E}|s|_G \ge 142.2$ (the empirical average is 159.5) and $\sigma \le 6.41$ tokens, hence 98.6% sensitivity (1.4% type-II error) under the Gaussian approximation; plugging the empirical mean instead gives a type-II rate of $5.3 \times 10^{-7}$ — a realistic approximation, the paper notes, but not a rigorous lower bound. Empirically 98.4% of generations are detected at $z = 4$ under multinomial sampling, rising to 99.6% with 4-way beam search.

Proposition (a cap on the knob; derivation: course notes — algebra on Theorem 4.2, not from the literature). The per-step guaranteed green probability can never exceed $$\mu^{\star} - \gamma \;\le\; (1 - \gamma)\Big(1 - \frac{1}{\beta}\Big),$$ with the bound approached as $S^{\star} \to 1$. Proof. Since $z^{\star} \ge 0$, spike entropy satisfies $S \le 1$, so $\mu^{\star} \le \gamma\alpha/\beta$ and $\mu^{\star} - \gamma \le \gamma(\alpha - \beta)/\beta$. Now $\alpha - \beta = (\alpha - 1)(1 - \gamma)$ and $\gamma(\alpha - 1) = \beta - 1$, so $\gamma(\alpha-\beta)/\beta = (\beta - 1)(1-\gamma)/\beta = (1-\gamma)(1 - 1/\beta)$. $\blacksquare$ Reading: as $\delta \to \infty$ (so $\beta \to \infty$) the guaranteed lift saturates at $1 - \gamma$ — the knob buys signal at a diminishing rate, while the quality cost (next entry) keeps growing like $\beta$.

The Quality Worry

If the best next word is on the red list, the model keeps settling for second-best, and the degradation accumulates into clumsy prose.

Key takeaway: the natural question is whether we can watermark without hurting quality.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (expected cross-entropy bound; Kirchenbauer et al., Theorem 4.3, stated in the corrected direction). Let $p$ be the model's next-token distribution at a step, $\hat{p}$ the watermarked distribution after the $\alpha$-tilt with a random green/red split of green fraction $\gamma$, and $\beta := 1 + (\alpha - 1)\gamma$, $H(p) := -\sum_k p_k \ln p_k$. Then $$\mathbb{E}_{G,R}\Big[ -\sum_{k} \hat{p}_k \ln p_k \Big] \;\le\; \beta\, H(p).$$ That is: in expectation over the key, the cross-entropy of watermarked text against the original model exceeds the model's own entropy by at most the factor $\beta = 1 + (e^{\delta} - 1)\gamma$.

Misprint warning (verified against the ICML paper directly). As printed, the paper's Theorem 4.3 reads $\mathbb{E}_{G,R} \sum_k \hat{p}_k \ln p_k \le (1 + (\alpha-1)\gamma) P^{\ast}$ with "$P^{\ast} = \sum_k p_k \ln p_k$" called the perplexity of the original model. Since $P^{\ast} = -H(p) \le 0$ and $\beta \ge 1$, the printed inequality points the wrong way — it is inconsistent with what the paper's own proof establishes. The statement above follows the privacy-course watermark deck, which restates the theorem in the direction their own proof gives. Do not quote the printed form.

Proof (two steps, as in the privacy-course deck; verified line by line).

  1. Pointwise domination in expectation. For $\alpha \ge 1$ the tilted normalizer satisfies $\sum_{i \in R} p_i + \alpha \sum_{i \in G} p_i \ge \sum_i p_i = 1$, so $\hat{p}_k \le \alpha p_k$ when $k$ is green and $\hat{p}_k \le p_k$ when $k$ is red. The split puts $k$ in $G$ with probability $\gamma$, hence $$\mathbb{E}_{G,R}[\hat{p}_k] \;\le\; \gamma \cdot \alpha p_k + (1 - \gamma)\, p_k \;=\; \beta\, p_k.$$
  2. Weight by the (deterministic, nonnegative) surprisals. Since $p$ is fixed and $-\ln p_k \ge 0$, $$\mathbb{E}_{G,R}\Big[-\sum_k \hat{p}_k \ln p_k\Big] = \sum_k \mathbb{E}_{G,R}[\hat{p}_k]\,(-\ln p_k) \;\le\; \beta \sum_k p_k (-\ln p_k) = \beta\, H(p). \qquad \blacksquare$$

Reading. The quality cost grows multiplicatively in $\beta$: high-entropy steps absorb the tilt with almost no cost (many good tokens are green anyway), while low-entropy steps — where the "best next word" is nearly forced — are exactly where the red list hurts. This is the formal version of the slide's "settling for second-best" and the reason the detection guarantee (Theorem 4.2) is also entropy-gated.

Distortion-Free Watermarks

Yes: instead of biasing scores, hide the mark in the randomness of sampling so the watermarked text follows the model's exact distribution. On average, quality is untouched.

Key takeaway: distortion-free watermarks preserve output quality by construction.

Definitions

Distortion-free decoder (Kuditipudi et al., Definition 1). A decoder $\Gamma : \Xi \times \Delta(V) \to V$ mapping a watermark key sequence $\xi$ and a next-token distribution $\mu$ to a token is distortion-free with respect to the key distribution if $$\Pr_{\xi}\big[\Gamma(\xi, \mu) = y\big] \;=\; \mu(y) \qquad \text{for every } \mu \in \Delta(V), \; y \in V.$$ Averaged over the key, the watermarked model is exactly the original model — no distribution shift at all.

Contrast. The green-list tilt is not distortion-free: by the boost proposition ("The Nudge"), the green mass strictly increases for every $\delta \gt 0$, so $\mathbb{E}_{\text{key}}[\hat{p}] \ne p$.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (exponential-minimum sampling is distortion-free; Kuditipudi et al., stated as in the privacy-course deck). Let $\xi = (\xi_1, \dots, \xi_N) \sim \mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^N)$ and define $$\Gamma(\xi, \mu) \;=\; \arg\min_{i} \; \frac{-\log \xi_i}{\mu(i)}.$$ Then $\Gamma$ is distortion-free.

Proof. The variables $E_i := -\log \xi_i$ are i.i.d. $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$, so $E_i / \mu(i) \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\mu(i))$, independent across $i$. In a race of independent exponentials, $\Pr[\arg\min = y] = \mu(y) / \sum_i \mu(i) = \mu(y)$. $\blacksquare$ (Statement and proof as in the privacy-course deck; verified against the paper.)

Background & further reading

  • Kuditipudi, Thickstun, Hashimoto & Liang, "Robust Distortion-free Watermarks for Language Models" (arXiv 2307.15593, TMLR) — verified from the abstract: with OPT-1.3B and LLaMA-7B, the watermark is detectable ($p \le 0.01$) from as few as 35 tokens even after corrupting 40–50% of tokens by random edits; on instruction-following (Alpaca-7B) about 25% of responses, at median length around 100 tokens, are detectable.

The Trick: Reused Randomness

Sampling already consumes random numbers; derive them from the key and the position. The same seed picks the same token, so the detector can replay it and check the match.

Key takeaway: the choice looks random but is secretly reproducible from the key.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (Gumbel-max trick; classical, stated as in the privacy-course deck, Theorem 6 there). Let $p = \mathrm{softmax}(\ell)$ and let $G_v$ be i.i.d. $\mathrm{Gumbel}(0,1)$. Then $$\Pr\big[\arg\max_{v} (\ell_v + G_v) = v\big] \;=\; p_v,$$ and $\max_v (\ell_v + G_v) \sim \mathrm{Gumbel}(\log Z, 1)$ with $Z = \sum_w e^{\ell_w}$, independent of the argmax.

Proof of the argmax law. $M_v := \ell_v + G_v \sim \mathrm{Gumbel}(\ell_v, 1)$ has CDF $F_v(m) = \exp(-e^{\ell_v} e^{-m})$ and density $f_v(m) = e^{\ell_v} e^{-m} \exp(-e^{\ell_v} e^{-m})$. Hence $$\Pr[\arg\max = v] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_v(m) \prod_{w \ne v} F_w(m)\, dm = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{\ell_v} e^{-m} \exp\!\big(-Z e^{-m}\big)\, dm.$$ Substituting $u = e^{-m}$ collapses this to $e^{\ell_v} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-uZ}\, du = e^{\ell_v}/Z = p_v$. $\blacksquare$ (Verified against the privacy-course deck.)

Proposition (Aaronson's rule is the Gumbel-max in disguise; privacy-course deck, Proposition 7 there). Let $r_v$ be i.i.d. $\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]$, derived pseudorandomly from the key and the recent context, and select $$x \;=\; \arg\max_{v} \; r_v^{1/p_v}.$$ Then $x \sim p$ exactly. Proof. $\log r_v^{1/p_v} = (\log r_v)/p_v = -E_v / p_v$ with $E_v = -\log r_v \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1)$, so $\arg\max_v r_v^{1/p_v} = \arg\min_v E_v/p_v = \arg\max_v (\log p_v + G_v)$, where $G_v = -\log E_v \sim \mathrm{Gumbel}(0,1)$. Since $\log p_v = \ell_v - \log Z$ differs from $\ell_v$ by a constant, the Gumbel-max theorem gives $\Pr[x = v] = p_v$. $\blacksquare$

Detection. The detector re-derives the $r_v$ from the key and checks that the $r$-values attached to the emitted tokens are improbably close to 1 under the uniform null — text written without the key shows no such alignment.

  • Scott Aaronson, "My AI Safety Lecture for UT Effective Altruism" (blog post) — Aaronson's own description of the OpenAI watermarking proposal: a cryptographic pseudorandom function replaces the sampling randomness, scored over n-grams; the detector needs neither the model nor the prompt. (The explicit $r^{1/p}$ rule above is the formalization in the Kuditipudi et al. line of work and the privacy-course deck; the blog describes the idea.)

Undetectable Watermarks

A 2024 result shows a watermark that no statistical test can detect without the key, so output looks perfectly normal, yet with the key the signal is unmistakable.

Key takeaway: watermarking need not leave any provable trace for outsiders.

Definitions

(All from Christ, Gunn & Zamir. Dating: the paper appeared on arXiv in 2023 and was published at COLT 2024.)

Empirical entropy. For an output $x$ on a given prompt, $H_e(x) = -\log \Pr[\mathsf{Model}(\text{prompt}) = x]$ — the surprisal of that particular output.

Soundness. For every fixed text $x$ of polynomial length: $\Pr_{sk}[\mathsf{Detect}_{sk}(x) = \texttt{true}] \le \mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$ — no innocent text is flagged, except with negligible probability over the key.

$b(L)$-completeness. $\Pr\big[\mathsf{Detect}_{sk}(x) = \texttt{false} \,\wedge\, H_e(x) \ge b(|x|)\big] \le \mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$ — every generated text carrying at least $b(L)$ bits of empirical entropy is caught. (A variant demands this on every contiguous substring.)

Undetectability. For every polynomial-time adaptive distinguisher $D$ with oracle access, $$\big| \Pr[D^{\mathsf{Model}, \mathsf{Model}} = 1] - \Pr_{sk}[D^{\mathsf{Model}, \mathsf{Wat}_{sk}} = 1] \big| \;\le\; \mathrm{negl}(\lambda):$$ even an adversary who queries the model however it likes cannot tell watermarked sampling from honest sampling.

Theorem & proof

Theorems (Christ–Gunn–Zamir, Theorems 1–2; statements verified). There is a watermarking scheme that is simultaneously undetectable, sound, and $O(\lambda\sqrt{L})$-complete for outputs of length $L$; a variant is complete on every substring with enough empirical entropy.

Construction and detection statistic (faithful sketch of their efficient scheme; full proofs in the paper). Work with binary tokens with model probabilities $p_j(1)$. A pseudorandom function keyed by $sk$ supplies $u_j \in [0,1]$, and the scheme emits $x_j = \mathbf{1}[u_j \le p_j(1)]$ — inverse-transform sampling, so as long as the PRF output is indistinguishable from uniform, the output distribution is exactly the model's: undetectability reduces to PRF security. Detection computes $$c(x) = \sum_j s(x_j, u_j), \qquad s(x_j, u_j) = \begin{cases} \ln \frac{1}{u_j} & x_j = 1, \\[2pt] \ln \frac{1}{1 - u_j} & x_j = 0. \end{cases}$$ For text written without the key, each $u_j$ is uniform and independent of $x_j$, so each score is $\mathrm{Exp}(1)$ — indeed $\Pr[\ln(1/u) \gt t] = \Pr[u \lt e^{-t}] = e^{-t}$ (derivation: course notes; scheme and statistic are Christ–Gunn–Zamir's) — and $\mathbb{E}[c(x)] = L$. For watermarked text, $x_j = 1$ forces $u_j \le p_j(1)$, which inflates the score by roughly the empirical entropy of the sequence; concentration of the sum separates the two cases once $H_e \gtrsim \lambda\sqrt{L}$.

Caveat (their Lemma 4). Undetectability is computational, not statistical: enumerating all keys distinguishes in exponential time. The guarantee is that no efficient test works. (The script's "no statistical test" should be heard with this qualifier.)

  • Christ, Gunn & Zamir, "Undetectable Watermarks for Language Models" (arXiv 2306.09194) — full definitions and proofs.
  • COLT 2024 version (PMLR 247:1125–1139) — the published venue.

The Robustness Worry

People edit AI text before sharing: fixing words, reordering sentences, paraphrasing, or round-trip translating. The question is whether the hidden signal survives.

Key takeaway: robustness is about whether the mark persists through editing.

Definitions

Removal vs spoofing. A removal attack takes watermarked $y$ and outputs $\tilde{y}$ preserving the meaning with $\mathsf{Detect}(\tilde{y}) = \texttt{false}$ (evading detection). A spoofing attack does the opposite: it produces text the detector flags that the model never generated (framing). Both matter; the theorems in the next entry address removal, and spoofing reappears in "The Impossibility Claim".

Edit distance. $d_{\mathrm{edit}}(y, \tilde{y})$ = the minimum number of token substitutions, insertions, and deletions transforming $y$ into $\tilde{y}$ (Levenshtein distance on token sequences). Robustness statements are phrased as: detection survives every $\tilde{y}$ within edit distance $\eta$ of $y$.

Small Edits Survive

A handful of changes flips only a few tokens; most green tokens stay green, so the green fraction still clears the threshold.

Key takeaway: light editing leaves the watermark intact.

Theorem & proof

Why hashed lists degrade gracefully (derivation: course notes). In the KGW scheme with context width $h$, one token edit changes that token's own indicator and corrupts the seeds of at most the $h$ following steps, so $\eta$ edits disturb at most $(h+1)\eta$ of the $T$ green indicators; the z-statistic drops by $O(\eta / \sqrt{T})$ and stays above threshold while $\eta \ll (\gamma^{\star} - \gamma)\, T / (h+1)$.

Theorem (provable robustness of the fixed-list Unigram-Watermark; Zhao et al., Theorem 3.7, statement verified against the paper). Remove the context from the hash entirely: draw one green list $G$ from the key and tilt with the same $\delta$ at every step. Let $y$ (length $n$) be watermarked with z-score $z_y$, and let the adversary output $u$ with edit distance $\eta = d_{\mathrm{edit}}(y, u) \lt n$. Then $$z_u \;\ge\; z_y - \max\left\{ \frac{(1 + \gamma/2)\,\eta}{\sqrt{n}},\; \frac{(1 - \gamma/2)\,\eta}{\sqrt{n - \eta}} \right\},$$ and when $\eta \le 2\gamma n / (1 + \gamma/2)^2$ the second term may be dropped. Since $z_y$ grows like $\sqrt{n}$ while the loss is $O(\eta/\sqrt{n})$, a constant $\delta$ tolerates a constant fraction of arbitrary edits. The structural reason: with a fixed list, token $i$'s green indicator depends on token $i$ alone, so one edit perturbs one indicator rather than a run of them. The price: a single fixed list can eventually be estimated from enough watermarked output. (The paper reports the scheme roughly twice as robust as the hashed KGW watermark in their experiments.)

Proof sketch (one Taylor step, as in the privacy-course deck; full proof in the paper's appendix). Write the statistic as a function $f(x, y) = (x - \gamma y)/\sqrt{y}$ of (green count, length). The whole attack collapses into two scalars — the change $k_x$ in green count and $k_y$ in length, with $|k_x|, |k_y| \le \eta$. A Taylor expansion of $f$ with remainder, maximizing the error over the box $|k_x|, |k_y| \le \eta$, yields exactly the two cases in the max. $\blacksquare$

Lemma (edit robustness of the distortion-free scheme; Kuditipudi et al., Lemma 2.6, faithful sketch — statement as in the privacy-course deck; full constants and proof in the paper). For their alignment-based detector with block size $k$ dividing the text length $m$, alignment-cost parameter $\gamma' \gt \tfrac12$ (note: $\gamma'$ here is an alignment parameter, unrelated to the green fraction), watermark potential $\alpha(Y)$, and any adversary output $\tilde{Y}$ with $d_{\mathrm{edit}}(Y, \tilde{Y}) \le \varepsilon m$: $$\Pr\big[\phi(\tilde{Y}, \xi') \le \phi(\tilde{Y}, \xi)\big] \;\le\; m\,n\,(2k)^{k/(4\gamma' - 1)} \exp\!\Big(- \tfrac{k\, C_0^2\, (\alpha(Y) - \gamma' \varepsilon)_+^2}{2} \Big),$$ i.e. detection survives any edit budget $\varepsilon m$ as long as the per-token signal $\alpha(Y)$ exceeds the damage term $\gamma' \varepsilon$.

Background & further reading

  • Zhao, Ananth, Li & Wang, "Provable Robust Watermarking for AI-Generated Text" (arXiv 2306.17439) — the Unigram-Watermark and Theorem 3.7.
  • Kuditipudi et al. (arXiv 2307.15593) — Lemma 2.6 and the measured robustness curves.

The Paraphrase Attack

Krishna et al. built DIPPER, an 11B-parameter paraphraser, and showed rewriting AI text evades watermark detectors, GPTZero, and DetectGPT — one detector fell from 70.3% to 4.6% caught at a 1% false-positive rate. Their proposed defense: the provider stores everything it generates and answers detection queries by retrieval.

Key takeaway: a paraphrase model between generator and reader sinks the green count toward chance.

Background & further reading

Krishna, Song, Karpinska, Wieting & Iyyer trained DIPPER, an 11B-parameter paraphrase model that can condition on surrounding context and reorder content, and showed (verified from the paper abstract) that paraphrasing evades the KGW watermark, GPTZero, DetectGPT, and OpenAI's text classifier — the 70.3% → 4.6% figure at a fixed 1% false-positive rate is DetectGPT's detection accuracy before and after paraphrase. Their proposed defense is retrieval: the provider keeps a database of everything it generated and answers detection queries by searching it for a semantic match, which resists paraphrase far better than any in-text statistical signal (at the cost of the provider storing and searching all its outputs).

  • Krishna, Song, Karpinska, Wieting & Iyyer, "Paraphrasing evades detectors of AI-generated text, but retrieval is an effective defense" (arXiv 2303.13408, NeurIPS 2023) — the DIPPER attack and the retrieval defense.

The Impossibility Claim

Sadasivan et al. make three claims worth stating precisely: recursive paraphrasing empirically weakens every detector class they test; theoretically, as the AI text distribution approaches the human one, the best key-less detector approaches a coin flip; and watermarks can be spoofed to frame humans. The theorem constrains post-hoc detectors, not planted watermarks.

Key takeaway: quote the impossibility result precisely — it is about detectors without a key.

Definitions

ROC and AUROC. A score-based detector is a function $D$ from documents to $\mathbb{R}$, flagging when $D(x) \gt \tau$. Sweeping $\tau$ traces the ROC curve $(\mathrm{FPR}(\tau), \mathrm{TPR}(\tau))$; AUROC is the area under it — $\tfrac12$ for a coin flip, $1$ for a perfect detector. $M$, $H$, and $\mathrm{TV}$ are as in "Watermark vs Detector".

Theorem & proof

Theorem (Sadasivan et al., Theorem 1; statement verified verbatim from the paper). "The area under the ROC of any detector $D$ is bounded as $$\mathrm{AUROC}(D) \;\le\; \frac12 + \mathrm{TV}(M, H) - \frac{\mathrm{TV}(M, H)^2}{2}."$$

Proof (derivation: course notes, reproducing the paper's bound; the paper's own proof is its Appendix C.1). For each threshold $\tau$, the flag event is $A_{\tau} = \{x : D(x) \gt \tau\}$, so by the TV identity of "Watermark vs Detector", $\mathrm{TPR}(\tau) - \mathrm{FPR}(\tau) = M(A_{\tau}) - H(A_{\tau}) \le \mathrm{TV}(M, H) =: t$. Hence the whole ROC curve lies below $\mathrm{TPR} \le \min\{1, \mathrm{FPR} + t\}$, and with $x = \mathrm{FPR}$, $$\mathrm{AUROC} \;\le\; \int_0^1 \min\{1, x + t\}\, dx \;=\; \int_0^{1-t} (x + t)\, dx + t \;=\; \frac{(1-t)^2}{2} + t(1-t) + t \;=\; \frac12 + t - \frac{t^2}{2}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

Scope — read the quantifiers. The theorem is about key-less detectors: any $D$ computable from the text alone. As models improve, $\mathrm{TV}(M, H) \to 0$ and the best such detector's AUROC tends to $\tfrac12$ — a coin flip. It does not constrain keyed watermarks, which deliberately move $M$ so that the keyed detector sees a large effective TV while outsiders see none. The paper's other claims (verified from the paper): recursive paraphrasing empirically degrades watermark-based, zero-shot, retrieval-based, and trained detectors on passages of roughly 300 tokens; and watermarks can be spoofed — an adversary can infer enough of the hidden signature without white-box access to compose human text the detector flags, framing the provider.

Complementary limits (Christ–Gunn–Zamir, statements verified). Low-entropy text cannot carry a mark: a model that answers with a fixed low-entropy string with probability $1 - \epsilon$ admits no detector catching more than $\epsilon + \mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$ of its output (their Lemma 3); any scheme that watermarks a non-negligible fraction of outputs with empirical entropy at most $t$ is distinguishable from the model with $O(2^t \cdot \mathrm{poly}(\lambda))$ queries (their Theorem 10); and undetectability has a flip side — for models that accept forced prefixes, an undetectable watermark is removable by querying one token at a time (their Theorem 11).

The Honest Limit

Measured follow-up work found paraphrase dilutes rather than erases the green-list mark: the per-token signal weakens, so detection needs longer text — roughly 800 tokens after strong human paraphrase at a 1e-5 false-positive rate. Short passages are effectively unmarked.

Key takeaway: watermarks raise the cost of casual misuse; on short text a motivated adversary wins.

Theorem & proof

Dilution arithmetic (derivation: course notes). If paraphrase shrinks the per-token green excess from $\gamma^{\star} - \gamma$ to $c\,(\gamma^{\star} - \gamma)$ with $c \lt 1$, then, holding the threshold fixed, $z \approx c\,(\gamma^{\star} - \gamma)\sqrt{T}/\sqrt{\gamma(1-\gamma)}$ recovers its old value at length $T' = T / c^2$: halving the per-token signal quadruples the text needed, but never zeroes the detector. That is the precise sense in which paraphrase dilutes rather than erases.

Background & further reading

Kirchenbauer et al.'s follow-up study measured watermark reliability under machine paraphrase, human paraphrase, and copy-paste mixing into surrounding text. Verified from the abstract: even "after strong human paraphrasing the watermark is detectable after observing 800 tokens on average, when setting a 1e-5 false positive rate" — paraphrases leak enough n-grams of the original that the diluted signal accumulates exactly as the arithmetic above predicts.

  • Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Shu, Saifullah, Kong, Fernando, Saha, Goldblum & Goldstein, "On the Reliability of Watermarks for Large Language Models" (arXiv 2306.04634, ICLR 2024) — the measured dilution study.

Image Watermarks Are Fragile

Pixels are easy to damage: cropping removes part of the pattern, screenshots and re-saves blur it, and heavy compression scrubs fine detail.

Key takeaway: every image transform chips away at the hidden mark.

Definitions

Robustness for media watermarks. An image watermark embeds a keyed signal into an image $x$, producing $\tilde{x} \approx x$ (imperceptibility), with a keyed detector; its robustness is defined relative to a class of transforms $\mathcal{T}$ (crop, rescale, recompress, screenshot, re-encode): the scheme is robust to $\mathcal{T}$ if the detector still flags $\tau(\tilde{x})$ for every $\tau \in \mathcal{T}$. Every claim of "robust" is implicitly indexed by the $\mathcal{T}$ it was tested against — transforms outside it (aggressive crops, regeneration by another model) are exactly where marks die.

Cropping Erases the Mark

Because the watermark is spread across the whole image, keeping only a corner leaves too little of the pattern for the detector to recover.

Key takeaway: less surface means less signal and a missed detection.

Theorem & proof

Signal-surface heuristic (derivation: course notes; qualitative). A spread-spectrum-style detector correlates the image against the keyed pattern, summing a weak per-region signal over the whole surface — the image analogue of summing green indicators over $T$ tokens. Retaining a fraction $\rho$ of the area keeps roughly a fraction $\rho$ of the signal terms while the noise in the correlation scales like $\sqrt{\rho}$, so the detection statistic falls like $\sqrt{\rho}$ relative to the full image: the same $\sqrt{T}$ evidence law as text, run in reverse. A small crop is a mild dilution; keeping only a corner puts the statistic back under the threshold.

Video Is Harder Still

Video adds thousands of frames, platform recompression on every upload, and clipping to a few seconds. The mark must survive all of it at once.

Key takeaway: robust video watermarking is an open engineering challenge.

No Shared Standard

Each company ships its own watermark, and a detector reads only its own brand, so content from elsewhere goes unflagged. There is no universal check.

Key takeaway: fragmentation limits how useful watermarks can be in practice.

The Tempting Shortcut

Suppose there is no watermark. A standalone detector tries to guess from style alone, with no key and no cooperation from the generator. Many products — GPTZero and kin — promise exactly this.

Key takeaway: detection without a planted signal is just an educated guess.

Definitions

Zero-shot (key-less) detection. A detector computable from the text alone — no key, no provider cooperation. The representative principled example is DetectGPT (Mitchell et al.): machine-generated text tends to sit in regions of negative curvature of a scoring model's log-likelihood, so the statistic compares $\log p_{\theta}(x)$ with the average log-likelihood of many small mask-and-refill perturbations of $x$; a large drop under perturbation flags machine text. Note this is exactly the class of detectors bounded by Sadasivan et al.'s Theorem 1 — and the one DIPPER pushed from 70.3% to 4.6%.

Background & further reading

  • Mitchell, Lee, Khazatsky, Manning & Finn, "DetectGPT: Zero-Shot Machine-Generated Text Detection using Probability Curvature" (arXiv 2301.11305) — the curvature criterion.

Why Detectors Struggle

Modern models write like fluent humans, the stylistic gap keeps shrinking, each new model breaks old detectors, and a light rewrite fools most of them.

Key takeaway: with no hidden signal, the verdict is fragile.

Theorem & proof

The slide, formalized. "The stylistic gap keeps shrinking" is the statement $\mathrm{TV}(M, H) \to 0$ as models improve; by Sadasivan et al.'s Theorem 1 ("The Impossibility Claim"), the best key-less detector's AUROC is then squeezed toward $\tfrac12 + \mathrm{TV} - \mathrm{TV}^2/2 \to \tfrac12$ — a coin flip. "Each new model breaks old detectors" is the same inequality applied to a moving $M$: a detector tuned to yesterday's $M$ carries no guarantee against today's. The escape hatches are exactly the two the lecture builds: plant a key (watermark) or sign at the source (provenance).

False Accusations

A wrong flag harms real people: students accused of cheating with no way to prove innocence, and the bias is measured — over half of non-native TOEFL essays were flagged as "AI" in a Stanford study (Liang et al., Patterns 2023).

Key takeaway: a confident but sometimes-wrong detector is dangerous.

Background & further reading

Liang, Yuksekgonul, Mao, Wu & Zou evaluated seven widely used GPT detectors on 91 human-written TOEFL essays by non-native English writers. Verified from the paper: the detectors "misclassified over half of the TOEFL essays as 'AI-generated' (average false positive rate: 61.22%)"; 89 of the 91 essays (97.8%) were flagged by at least one detector, and 18 of 91 (19.8%) were flagged unanimously by all seven — while the same detectors were near-perfect on essays by US 8th-graders. The mechanism is perplexity: detectors treat low-perplexity prose as machine-like, and non-native writing shows lower lexical variety; prompting ChatGPT to enrich the essays' word choice dropped the average false-positive rate from 61.22% to 11.77%.

Deepfakes

AI now fabricates people convincingly: voice clones from seconds of audio, live face swaps, and photoreal images of events that never happened.

Key takeaway: the fakes are good enough to fool most viewers.

Definitions

Deepfake. Synthetic or manipulated media that depicts a real person saying or doing something they did not, produced by deep generative models. The main technical families: face swap (replace a face in existing footage), reenactment / lip-sync (drive a real face with new motion or audio), voice cloning (text-to-speech in a target's voice from short samples), and full synthesis (diffusion or GAN generation of entirely new imagery). All of these are outputs a generator could watermark — which is why this half of the lecture ends back at provenance.

Real Harms

Deepfakes already drive voice-clone scams, fake political clips before elections, and non-consensual fabricated images. Detection alone cannot keep up with the volume.

Key takeaway: the harm is concrete and current, not hypothetical.

In January 2024 a finance worker at the engineering firm Arup joined a video call where the "CFO" and colleagues were all deepfakes built from public footage, and made 15 transfers totaling about HK$200M (US$25.6M). Everyone on the call except the victim was synthetic.

Key takeaway: deepfake fraud already works at corporate scale — seeing is no longer believing.

Days before the 2024 New Hampshire primary, an AI voice clone of President Biden robocalled voters telling them not to vote. The FCC fined the organizer $6 million and the state brought criminal charges.

Key takeaway: election deepfakes are real, and regulators responded with real penalties.

Background & further reading

The 2024 Reality Check

Despite the fear, Meta reported that AI-generated content was less than 1% of fact-checked election misinformation on its platforms in 2024. The predicted deepfake apocalypse did not materialize — cheap fakes and old-fashioned lies still dominate.

Key takeaway: keep the threat calibrated: real cases exist, but volume so far is small.

Background & further reading

The calibration point stands with the usual caveat: this measures fact-checked content on Meta's platforms, not all synthetic media everywhere.

The Detection Arms Race

Deepfake detectors and generators improve against each other in a loop: every published detector becomes a training signal for the next generator. In Facebook's 2020 Deepfake Detection Challenge the best model managed about 65% precision on unseen fakes.

Key takeaway: post-hoc deepfake detection is an arms race the detector is not winning.

Background & further reading

The Deepfake Detection Challenge (2019–2020, run by Facebook/Meta with industry and academic partners, 2,114 participants) is the standard reference point. Verified from Meta's results post: "the top-performing model achieved an accuracy of 65.18%" when "tested against a black box data set" of "challenging real world examples" not shared with entrants — note the precise metric in the source is accuracy on the unseen black-box set (the same models scored above 82% on the public test set, which is the generalization-gap lesson: detectors overfit the fakes they trained on).

  • Meta AI, "Deepfake Detection Challenge results: an open initiative to advance AI" (2020) — the 65.18% black-box figure.

Flip the Problem

Rather than chasing every fake, prove what is real: sign genuine content at the source and attach a record of how it was made, so unsigned content earns less trust.

Key takeaway: proving authenticity scales better than detecting forgery, this is provenance.

Definitions

Provenance. A verifiable record of a content item's origin and transformation history, bound to the item and checkable by anyone. Structurally it inverts detection: a detector tries to establish a negative ("this was machine-made") from statistical residue, while provenance establishes a positive ("this camera or app produced this, and these edits followed") with digital signatures — cryptographic, not statistical, evidence. The trust model shifts accordingly: from "trust my classifier" to "trust the signing keys and the hardware that holds them".

C2PA Content Credentials

C2PA is an industry standard for a tamper-evident label: the camera or app signs the file at creation, each edit appends a signed record, and tampering breaks the signature.

Key takeaway: content credentials give content a verifiable, tamper-evident history.

Definitions

C2PA. Verified from the coalition's site: "The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, or C2PA, provides an open technical standard for publishers, creators and consumers to establish the origin and edits of digital content. It's called Content Credentials." Technically, a signed provenance record is embedded in (or referenced from) the asset: it lists statements about origin and edit actions, is cryptographically bound to the content by hashes, and is signed by the capturing device or editing tool. Tamper-evident means exactly that any post-signature modification of the content or the record makes signature validation fail — the credential cannot be silently altered, only stripped entirely (see "The Strip Problem").

Background & further reading

  • c2pa.org — the coalition and standard overview.
  • spec.c2pa.org — the full technical specification.
  • contentcredentials.org — the consumer-facing verify tool and ecosystem site.

A Signed History

Credentials form a chain, capture then edit then publish, each step cryptographically signed, so a reader can replay the chain and spot a broken link.

Key takeaway: the value is an auditable, signed history rather than a single stamp.

Definitions

Chain validation. Each step in the life of the asset (capture, each edit, publish) appends a record signed by that step's tool, with each record hash-binding both the current content and the prior record. Verifying replays the chain: check every signature against trusted keys, and check every hash against the content actually present. Any mismatch localizes the break — the reader learns not just that the history is bad but where it stopped being attested. What the chain does not prove is that the original scene was true: it proves who signed and what changed, and the trust bottoms out in the signing keys (ideally held in camera hardware).

Credentials Go Mainstream

Content Credentials moved from spec to shipping hardware: Leica's M11-P was the first camera to sign photos at capture in 2023, Samsung's Galaxy S25 labels AI edits from January 2025, and Google's Pixel 10 attaches credentials to every photo by default from September 2025; Sony, Nikon, and Canon added support by firmware.

Key takeaway: signing at the point of capture is becoming a default, not a niche feature.

The Strip Problem

A credential can simply be removed, for example by a screenshot that drops the metadata, and absence of a credential is not proof of fakery. Watermarks help where credentials are stripped.

Key takeaway: watermarks and credentials are complementary defenses, not rivals.

Definitions

Metadata-bound vs content-embedded. A C2PA credential lives alongside the pixels (embedded metadata or a linked manifest): unforgeable while present (signatures), but removable by any pipeline that re-encodes the pixels — a screenshot carries none of it. A watermark lives inside the pixels: it survives re-encoding by design, but is statistical (thresholded detection, degradable by transforms) rather than cryptographic. The two failure modes are disjoint, which is the formal sense of "complementary": credentials give strong provenance when the chain survives; watermarks give weaker provenance that survives when the chain does not. Deployed stacks (SynthID plus C2PA in Google's products) layer both.

The Law Steps In

Labeling AI content is now a legal obligation, not a suggestion: China's labeling rules took effect in September 2025, the EU AI Act's transparency article applies from August 2026, and US states have a patchwork of election-deepfake laws.

Key takeaway: the next two slides look at the two big regimes in force.

China Labels First

China's labeling Measures took effect on 1 September 2025: AI-generated content needs an explicit visible label and an implicit label in the file's metadata, and platforms must check for labels and tag suspected AI content. A national standard (GB 45438-2025) specifies the formats.

Key takeaway: China is the first major jurisdiction with mandatory dual labeling in force.

Background & further reading

The Cyberspace Administration of China published the Measures for Labeling AI-Generated and Synthetic Content (《人工智能生成合成内容标识办法》) on 14 March 2025, effective 1 September 2025 (both dates verified from the CAC's own page). The Measures require explicit labels (visible or audible to users) and implicit labels (machine-readable, in file metadata) on AI-generated text, images, audio, video, and virtual scenes, and oblige distribution platforms to verify labels and to tag content they suspect is synthetic. A companion mandatory national standard, GB 45438-2025, fixes the technical label formats, effective the same day.

  • CAC, 《人工智能生成合成内容标识办法》 (official text, 14 March 2025; page in Chinese) — the Measures with the effective date.

EU AI Act: Article 50

Article 50 makes providers mark AI output in a machine-readable way and makes deployers disclose deepfakes, with carve-outs for art, satire, and law enforcement. It applies from 2 August 2026 — watermarking and credentials are the technologies that satisfy it.

Key takeaway: the EU mandates the outcome and lets the watermarking toolbox supply the mechanism.

Background & further reading

Verified from the Article 50 text: paragraph (2) requires providers of AI systems generating synthetic audio, image, video, or text to "ensure that the outputs of the AI system are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated"; paragraph (4) requires deployers of deepfake-generating systems to disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated, with exceptions including law-enforcement-authorized use and qualified carve-outs for evidently artistic, creative, satirical, or fictional work. Date of entry into application: 2 August 2026 (per Article 113). The regulation deliberately names the outcome — machine-readable marking — and leaves the mechanism to the state of the art, which today means exactly the watermarks and content credentials of this lecture.

  • EU AI Act, Article 50 (artificialintelligenceact.eu) — full article text with the entry-into-application note.

Frontier 2025–26

Where the field stands: watermarks are on by default at the largest providers with 10B+ items marked, provenance is moving into camera hardware, and labeling laws are in force in China and arriving in the EU. Open problems: robustness to strong paraphrase and a single cross-vendor standard.

Key takeaway: deployment and regulation arrived; robustness and interoperability did not, yet.

Key Takeaways

Two flavors of watermark; green-list text watermarks plant a countable signal; paraphrase dilutes the mark and short texts lose it; standalone detectors misfire and risk false accusations; provenance and credentials prove what is real; and labels are now required by law in China and, from 2026, the EU.

Key takeaway: a planted mark beats a guessed verdict.

Closer

The closing question is simply "Real?". The answer is to build the proof of authenticity in at the source rather than guessing after the fact.

Key takeaway: design provenance in, do not bolt detection on.
