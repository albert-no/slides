Lecture 3 — Membership Inference Attacks · Speaker Script

One entry per slide, in order. Section headers match slide titles.

Membership Inference Attacks

Welcome to Lecture 3 of Trustworthy AI. Today we study the most basic privacy attack on a trained model: deciding whether one specific record was part of its training set.

Key takeaway: Membership inference is the practical unit test for privacy.

Contents

Seven steps: the question, the basic loss-threshold attack, shadow models, stronger likelihood-ratio attacks, what it means for differential privacy, modern foundation models, and defenses.

Key takeaway: From one yes-or-no question to a full audit-and-defend toolkit.

01 — The Question

First section. We frame the single question every membership attack answers and explain why even a yes-or-no answer can be deeply harmful.

Key takeaway: Membership alone can be the secret.

One Yes-or-No Question

A membership inference attack, or MIA, takes a trained model and one record, and decides whether that record was used during training. Nothing fancier than in-or-out.

Key takeaway: MIA asks only whether a record was in the training set.

The Simplest Leak

Membership is the simplest thing an attacker can ask and the easiest to measure cleanly. If a model fails this test, it almost certainly leaks more.

Key takeaway: Pass the membership test before claiming any stronger privacy.

Member vs Non-Member

Picture two worlds: one where record $x$ was in training, one where it was held out. The attacker sees only the resulting model and must guess which world produced it.

Key takeaway: The attack is distinguishing two nearly identical worlds.

Why Membership Alone Hurts

If a model was trained only on cancer patients, then confirming that Alice is a member directly reveals her diagnosis. No record needs to be reconstructed for real harm.

Key takeaway: When the cohort is sensitive, membership leaks the secret.

Where It Bites

The risk appears wherever a model is trained on a sensitive cohort: one disease, one app's users, one location or income bracket. Belonging to the set is itself private information.

Key takeaway: Sensitive-cohort models make membership a privacy harm.

The Threat Model

Two access levels: black-box, where the attacker only queries inputs and reads outputs, and white-box, where weights and gradients are also visible. Most attacks need only black-box access.

Key takeaway: Black-box access is usually enough to attack.

A Score and a Threshold

Every attack reduces to the same shape: compute a score for the record, compare it to a threshold, and call it a member or non-member. The art is in choosing a good score.

Key takeaway: All membership attacks are a score plus a threshold.

02 — The Basic Attack

Second section. The core mechanism: models fit the points they saw, so overfitting leaves a measurable fingerprint we can threshold on.

Key takeaway: Overfitting is the engine that powers membership inference.

Models Fit What They Saw

Training drives loss down on the training points, so on average train loss is below test loss. A member therefore tends to have lower loss than a stranger.

Key takeaway: Lower loss is the basic tell of membership.

Loss Is the Score

The simplest score is the per-example loss itself. Low loss means guess member, high loss means guess non-member.

Key takeaway: Per-example loss is the first and simplest membership score.

The Two Bells

Plot member losses and non-member losses as two distributions. Members sit lower, but the two bells overlap, and that overlap is exactly the attacker's error rate.

Key takeaway: The overlap of the two loss distributions sets the attack's accuracy.

Overfitting Drives MIA

The wider the train-test gap, the further apart the two bells, and the easier the attack. A model that generalizes well hides its members.

Key takeaway: Privacy risk tracks the generalization gap.

The Threshold Attack

The whole attack is three lines: query the model for the loss, compare to a threshold, return member if it is small. No training and no internals required.

Key takeaway: One query and a threshold is already an attack.

Confidence Works Too

When the true label is hidden, use the model's confidence instead. Members get sharper outputs, so the top predicted probability, or its gap to the runner-up, works as a score.

Key takeaway: Confidence substitutes for loss when labels are unavailable.

A Theory Anchor

Yeom and colleagues proved the threshold attack is no accident: the membership advantage is bounded by the train-test loss gap. With zero generalization gap, no loss-based attack works.

Key takeaway: The loss attack is provably tied to overfitting.

Try It Yourself

A Colab demo makes this concrete: train a small classifier, plot member and non-member loss histograms, and sweep the threshold to watch attack accuracy rise and fall.

Key takeaway: The two bells are visible in a single Colab cell.

03 — Shadow Models

Third section. We upgrade from a hand-picked threshold to a learned attack, using copycat models whose membership labels we control.

Key takeaway: Build models you understand to learn the membership signal.

One Threshold Is Crude

A single global threshold ignores that classes have different loss scales and inputs differ in difficulty. We would rather train the attack than guess a cutoff.

Key takeaway: A global threshold leaves signal on the table.

The Shadow Idea

You cannot see the target's true members, so Shokri and colleagues build shadow models: models trained like the target on data you own, where you know exactly who is in and who is out.

Key takeaway: Shadow models give you ground-truth membership labels.

The Shadow Pipeline

Train several shadow models, collect their outputs labeled in or out, and train an attack classifier on those labeled outputs. Then apply it to the target model.

Key takeaway: Shadow outputs become training data for an attack model.

Learn the Member Signal

The attack classifier sees an output and predicts in or out. Trained on shadow data, it learns what a member's output looks like, then transfers that judgment to the target.

Key takeaway: The attacker learns the membership signal without ever seeing target data.

Why It Transfers

Because the shadows share the target's architecture and task, they overfit in the same way. The in-versus-out pattern carries across, even when the data is only similar.

Key takeaway: Shared architecture means shared memorization habits.

04 — Stronger Attacks

Fourth section. We move to state-of-the-art attacks that calibrate each record's intrinsic difficulty and we fix how we measure success.

Key takeaway: Strong attacks ask whether the loss is low for this record specifically.

Not All Records Are Equal

Raw loss conflates difficulty with membership: easy records have low loss even as non-members, hard ones high loss even as members. We need to ask, low compared to what?

Key takeaway: Raw loss confuses how hard a record is with whether it was a member.

Calibrate Per Example

The sharper question is whether the loss is low for this specific record, relative to models that never saw it. Compare against the record's own baseline, not a global one.

Key takeaway: Calibration compares each record to its own out-of-training baseline.

The Likelihood Ratio

Sablayrolles and colleagues frame this as two distributions for the loss of $x$, in and out, and the optimal score is their likelihood ratio. Estimate both, then divide.

Key takeaway: The Bayes-optimal membership score is a likelihood ratio.

LiRA: Likelihood Ratio Attack

Carlini and colleagues make it practical: train many shadows with the record in and many with it out, fit a Gaussian to each loss distribution, and score by the ratio.

Key takeaway: LiRA is the strong, practical realization of the likelihood-ratio attack.

Two In-vs-Out Bells

For one record, you get two narrow bells: loss-when-in and loss-when-out. The target's observed loss is scored by which bell it sits closer to.

Key takeaway: Per-record in and out distributions make the test far sharper.

Label-Only Attacks

When the model returns only a label, Choquette-Choo and colleagues perturb the input and check whether the label survives. Members tolerate more perturbation, so robustness becomes the signal.

Key takeaway: Even label-only access leaks membership through robustness.

Average Accuracy Lies

A headline like fifty-five percent attack accuracy sounds harmless, but it hides that a handful of records leak with near certainty. The worst case is the real harm.

Key takeaway: Average accuracy can mask catastrophic per-record leakage.

Precision at Low FPR

The right metric fixes a tiny false positive rate, such as one in a thousand, and asks how many true members are still caught. Confident, correct hits are what threaten privacy.

Key takeaway: Judge attacks by their hit rate at very low false positive rates.

Reading the ROC Tail

Plot true positives against false positives on a log scale and look at the far left edge, where false positives are rare. Strong attacks like LiRA pull away exactly there.

Key takeaway: The left edge of the ROC curve is where privacy is won or lost.

05 — What It Means

Fifth section. We connect attacks back to differential privacy: a real privacy bound provably limits any membership attack, and attacks let us audit that bound.

Key takeaway: Differential privacy and membership inference are two views of the same thing.

Differential Privacy in One Line

Differential privacy, or DP, bounds how much one record can change the output. For datasets differing in one person, every outcome shifts by at most a factor $e^{\varepsilon}$ plus slack $\delta$.

Key takeaway: DP caps the effect of any single record on the model.

DP Caps the Attacker

The very record-swap that DP bounds is what a membership attack exploits. So a genuine epsilon bound limits the success of any attacker, known or not.

Key takeaway: A real epsilon bound is a guarantee against every membership attack.

A Concrete Bound

Under epsilon-DP, the best attacker's true positive rate is at most $e^{\varepsilon}$ times its false positive rate, plus delta. Small epsilon forces true positives down near false positives.

Key takeaway: DP turns into an explicit ceiling on the attack's ROC curve.

Auditing Flips the Attack

Run the strongest attack you have, read its true and false positive rates, and back out an empirical epsilon. The attack becomes a measurement instrument.

Key takeaway: Attack success can be inverted into an empirical privacy level.

Trust but Verify

If the empirical epsilon exceeds the claimed one, the implementation is broken; if it sits far below, the proof is just conservative. Either way, auditing is now standard.

Key takeaway: Auditing catches both bugs and loose privacy proofs.

Auditing in Practice

In practice you inject canaries, records crafted to be easy to detect, train the model, and attack the canaries. High detection means weak privacy, and recent methods audit in a single training run.

Key takeaway: Canary-based auditing is cheap enough to run on every model.

06 — Modern Models

Sixth section. We bring membership inference to large language models and diffusion models, and confront the live debate over whether it even makes sense at web scale.

Key takeaway: On foundation models, membership inference is powerful and contested.

MIA Meets Foundation Models

Since 2023, attacks ask whether an article was in a language model's pretraining or a photo in an image model's data. The stakes are copyright and privacy at once.

Key takeaway: Membership questions now drive copyright and privacy disputes.

Detecting Pretraining Data

Shi and colleagues' Min-K percent scores text by its least likely tokens: members have few very-low-probability tokens, so averaging the most surprising ones flags seen text.

Key takeaway: Min-K percent spots pretraining text through its rare, surprising tokens.

Diffusion Models Too

Image generators leave the same overfitting trace: members get lower denoising error, and heavily duplicated images leak most. The mechanism is identical, only the domain changes.

Key takeaway: Diffusion memorization grows with duplication, just like in language models.

Is MIA Well-Posed at Scale?

Duan and colleagues challenge the premise: on genuine web-scale language models, many published attacks barely beat random guessing once the evaluation is done carefully.

Key takeaway: At true web scale, many membership attacks barely beat chance.

Why Scale Breaks It

Two forces blunt the attack: a single pass over trillions of tokens leaves little overfitting, and members and non-members come from overlapping distributions. Apparent success is often just topic detection.

Key takeaway: Little overfitting plus overlapping data makes membership hard to read.

The Benchmark Trap

Many reported wins came from flawed splits where members were simply newer than non-members. The attack learned the date, not membership, and the advantage vanishes once the split is fixed.

Key takeaway: Always check whether a benchmark measures membership or a confound.

An Open Debate

Skeptics argue web-scale attacks mostly measure distribution shift; optimists point to rare, fine-tuned, or duplicated data that still clearly leaks. The question is unsettled as of 2025-26.

Key takeaway: Whether web-scale MIA is meaningful is an open research question.

07 — Defenses

Seventh section. We survey defenses, from cheap regularization to provable DP-SGD, and the accuracy cost each one carries.

Key takeaway: Defenses range from heuristic to provable, and all cost some utility.

Shrink the Gap

Since the attack feeds on overfitting, the first defenses reduce it: regularization like weight decay and dropout, early stopping before memorization sets in, and simply collecting more data.

Key takeaway: Reducing overfitting directly weakens the attack.

Heuristics Are Not Proof

Regularization raises the bar but offers no guarantee: a stronger attack may still succeed, and there is no bound on the worst-case record. It helps without being sufficient.

Key takeaway: Heuristic defenses help but never guarantee privacy.

DP-SGD: The Principled Defense

For a guarantee, train with DP-SGD: compute each example's gradient, clip it to a maximum length, add Gaussian noise to the sum, take the step, and track the spent epsilon.

Key takeaway: DP-SGD trains with a provable privacy bound via clip-then-noise.

Why DP-SGD Stops MIA

Clipping caps any single record's footprint and noise hides whether it was present. The two worlds blur by construction, which is exactly the indistinguishability the attack tries to break.

Key takeaway: DP-SGD enforces the very indistinguishability membership attacks target.

The Privacy-Utility Cost

Strong defenses cost accuracy, and that gap is the price of privacy. A smaller epsilon buys a stronger guarantee at the cost of a larger gap, though better methods keep narrowing it.

Key takeaway: Privacy and accuracy trade off, but the trade keeps improving.

A Defender's Checklist

Before shipping: measure the train-test gap, run a LiRA-style audit, report precision at low false positive rates rather than average accuracy, and use DP-SGD when records are sensitive.

Key takeaway: Attack your own model before anyone else does.

Key Takeaways

Membership inference asks whether a record was in training; overfitting leaks it and loss is the simplest score; LiRA calibrates per record and we judge it at low false positive rates; DP bounds the attack while web-scale membership remains debated.

Key takeaway: Membership inference is the practical unit test of privacy.

In or out?

Closing slide. The whole lecture comes down to one bit per record, in or out, and the goal of trustworthy machine learning is to keep that bit private.

Key takeaway: Keep the in-or-out bit private.
